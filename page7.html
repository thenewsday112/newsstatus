On Tuesday, defense contractor Anduril and chipmaker Qualcomm announced a partnership to build laptops entirely in the United States, starting with a single model in 2027. They also announced a “long-term goal” of pushing the computer industry to create “a fully domestic supply chain.”

Even a single Made-in-USA laptop would be a surprise, because no major US computer company builds its laptops here — not Apple, not Dell, not HP, not Microsoft. We’re not aware of any company that does.

But we are aware of companies that have briefly and strategically assembled computers here seemingly to appease President Donald Trump. We’re also aware of companies that have promised US jobs only to deliver empty buildings. And we’re aware of at least one fake “US” company that lied to the government about making product domestically.

That’s why we now ask pointed questions when a company says it’s committed to US manufacturing. We asked TP-Link those sorts of questions just over a week ago. On Monday, we asked Anduril founder Palmer Luckey and Qualcomm president and CEO Cristiano Amon those kinds of questions.

Over the course of two days, Anduril has changed one important answer.

Anduril communications VP Shannon Prior now says the company’s laptop will have at least 65 percent of its components made in the US by the time it launches in 2027. It’s an important number, and one that Anduril did not commit to earlier this week.

Luckey held a call with reporters on Monday, during which he was clear that he wants to make literally every part of a computer in the United States — and that Anduril and Qualcomm have already mapped out a plan. “We have a technical roadmap to get to 100 percent domestic over time,” he told journalists.

Luckey also suggested his laptop would be built to comply with the requirements of the military and other government agencies — and that it would meet or exceed one specific US government standard that has an exact definition.

“We are not just barely meeting the bar for Made in America. We are going hard to make this an all-American play, and not even the Buy American Act requires what we’re trying,” Luckey said.

The Buy American Act dates back to 1933, but it was updated during the Biden Administration to require that at least 65 percent of the cost of a company’s products must be made domestically for it be considered a domestic product itself. That number is going up to 75 percent in 2029.

Many products need to be made in America to those standards, or federal agencies can’t buy them without receiving a waiver — which agencies currently use en masse, Luckey himself argued on the call. “There’s a number of government agencies that are required by law to buy American-made computers [...] All of those procurements are happening under waivers of those laws,” he said.

We also noticed that Anduril’s press release specifies that its first laptop will be “designed and assembled” in the United States, not necessarily “manufactured” here (though the release does talk about future manufacturing). This was also a sticking point with router maker TP-Link, which would not confirm full manufacturing even after suggesting it in an interview with The Verge.

That’s why we asked Luckey what percent of his first laptop’s components would be made in the US. His reply was not 65 percent or 70 percent. It was “TBD.” You can read the exchange for yourself:

The Verge: When it arrives in 2027, what percent of the components in the BlueChip laptop will be made in the US?

Luckey: TBD. I’m doing my very, very best. It’s gonna be a whole mix of things based on when do certain memory fabs come online? When do certain CPU, GPU fabs come online? There’s a whole— Like, am I able to get a display line here or not? Do I have to do that after launch? I’m being totally open kimono with you here. Those questions are not settled. It is not clear if I will be able. I know the glass is gonna be made in the US, I know the frame is gonna be made in the US around that display. I know that parts of the ribbon component are gonna be made in the U.S. I don’t know if I will have successfully pulled that component in or a dozen other components. So it’s a little up in the air. I’m doing my very, very best. I think most likely the thing that we ship is not going to be 100-percent American. I think it’ll be a blend of US and allies around the world, mostly US, probably a few things from some allies, and then that’ll be in the context of conversations with those allies about bringing those components into production in the United States, and I’m being honest with the government on this too. I mean, I’ve had conversations where I’ve said, listen. I would love to give you guys an American, 100-percent American laptop right off the bat, but that is not the environment the US government has created or US industry has created. I can’t let the perfect be the enemy of the good.

When we followed up by email to ask specifically about 65 percent, the answer was that the laptop prototype is currently at 32 percent and Anduril could not yet commit to 65 percent. Here’s Anduril spokesperson Stephanie Davis’ full reply on Tuesday:

For the prototype today, approximately 32% is U.S.-origin by component procurement cost, and more than 90% is sourced from the United States and allied countries combined.

We’re working to expand domestic sourcing and production ahead of planned availability in 2027, with the goal of eliminating foreign content and ultimately establishing an end-to-end American supply chain.

The final domestic-content percentage at launch remains unsettled, so we can’t confirm above or below 65% today. That’s why we’re asking you to revise the assertion that it will be “well below” that threshold.

Today, Prior writes that Anduril can indeed commit to 65 percent. Here is the most recent statement:

Our previous response to The Verge was incorrect. The BlueChip laptop is indeed being developed to exceed the 65% U.S. domestic-content threshold at launch in 2027. Meeting that threshold is a fundamental objective of the program. The precise domestic-content percentage of the final product is still being determined as we finalize suppliers and work through prototypes, and we look forward to sharing more next year.

Today’s statement is the first time Anduril has confirmed that we, and government agencies, theoretically shouldn’t need to wait until Anduril achieves its full goals to see a laptop that meets the Buy American standard. If everything works out, Anduril’s first laptop in 2027, with Qualcomm, should be that laptop.

We invited Luckey to explain how Anduril would get to 65 percent domestic component production in a follow-up interview, but we were told he’s not currently available. On the Monday call, he told journalists that Anduril already has a US glassmaker lined up for the laptop’s cover glass, is programming FPGAs (field-programmable gate arrays) to serve the purpose of power management chips and other circuits it can’t normally can’t get outside China, and is developing made-in-America batteries as well.

The press release adds that the enclosure is designed “for US machining” and the laptop will use metals “that US industry can produce and machine at scale.”

As for the laptop’s Snapdragon processor, we asked Qualcomm CEO Cristiano Amon about that. “TSMC has a manufacturing facility in Arizona, and that’s where our chips are going to be made,” he told The Verge.

#33. Forecasters are predicting a warmer and wetter winter. Here's how El Niño will affect your region.

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

This winter is expected to be warmer and wetter, as national weather agencies point to a "Super El Niño" altering weather patterns across the globe.

The latest forecast from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration estimates there will be warmer-than-average temperatures across much of the continental U.S., accompanied by above-average precipitation in several regions.

El Niño years are also associated with coastal flooding on the Eastern seaboard and the West Coast.

"It's a strong, if not historic El Niño going into this winter," Ken Graham, the director of NOAA's National Weather Service, said at the same October media briefing that Graham addressed.

Forecasters caution, however, that these predictions are just that, predictions. El Niño can shift the odds toward certain conditions but it does not ensure they will happen.

Still, the latest forecasts can help provide a roadmap for cities to prepare for potential impacts and allocate resources.

"These seasonal predictions are key to helping you prepare and giving you a heads up to assess all the risks and how to mitigate them," said Graham.

"Earlier and better decisions saves lives," he said.

It's not all negative. Forecasters say this year's El Niño could bring rainfall to areas experiencing drought conditions.

El Niño is a natural climate pattern that starts in the tropical Pacific Ocean. Every few years, a vast area of water in the eastern Pacific warms near the equator. That water releases enormous amounts of heat into the atmosphere, shifting weather patterns around the world. This year, meteorologists say the planet could experience the strongest one on record.

This El Niño weather pattern is on top of warming temperatures that result from fossil fuel emissions, which trap more heat in the atmosphere.

Josh Willis, a climate scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, uses the term "Godzilla" to describe both the size and the actual impact of El Niño — "like a monster that stretches all the way across the ocean." (He credits his colleague Bill Patzert with coining the term).

"It rises up out of the ocean like Godzilla, and it breathes fire on Indonesia and Southeast Asia: they're having severe drought and major wildfires," Willis says. "Its tail is kicking up hurricanes and storms and high tides on our end of the Pacific."

During another strong El Niño event in 2015-16, catastrophic flooding hit South Carolina and a blizzard walloped the East Coast.

Here's how El Niño may affect your region.

Forecasters expect warmer temperatures in Northern California, and above-average precipitation across the state, particularly in Southern California. There's also a chance for higher snowfall in parts of Nevada, the southern Rocky Mountains and California's San Bernardino Mountains.

This year's El Niño's effects were first felt on the West Coast with the arrival of a Kelvin wave. In early October, the large band of warm water raised sea levels as it moved north along the coast, bringing with it an elevated risk of floods and warmer ocean temperatures.

Pedestrians walk through a flooded street caused by king tides and heavy rain Wednesday, Oct. 28, 2026, in North Bay Village, Fla. Marta Lavandier/AP hide caption

By themselves, Kelvin waves don't cause flooding, said Dillon Amaya, an assistant professor of atmospheric sciences at North Carolina State University. Instead, they load the dice, he says, increasing the likelihood of flooding.

In early October, a Kelvin wave was one of the factors behind the extensive coastal flooding associated with Tropical Storm Rachel, Amaya says

Another Kelvin wave is now raising sea levels along California's coast. As of late October, the wave was between Los Angeles and San Francisco. Amaya said. Several more waves are expected through the fall and early winter.

Similar to the California coast, researchers expect the Pacific Northwest to experience warmer temperatures. But unlike much of the West Coast, parts of the region are forecasted to receive below-average precipitation. And without colder weather, where the temperatures drop and stay below zero, the result will likely be a smaller snowpack.

(Deep snowpacks can hold more water and slow melting snow feeds streams and rivers and helps sustain reservoirs.)

The region is already experiencing years of drought. And dry forests and parched soils significantly increase the likelihood and severity of wildfires.

Along the Pacific Northwest shorelines, communities are preparing for severe flooding. Many of these low-lying areas are shoring up and getting infrastructure repaired as they face a possible double whammy from El Niño and winter's high tides.

At the same time, parts of Western Washington and Oregon have experienced successive years of drought. Jon Gottschalck, a meteorologist and branch chief for NOAA, who spoke at the same October media briefing that Graham did, said that he expects dry conditions for western portions of Oregon and Western Washington state to improve and for drought conditions to potentially end.

The Southwest is already experiencing the anticipated wetter-than-average conditions.

Severe flooding in the Four Corners region, where the borders of New Mexico, Colorado, Arizona, and Utah meet, caused water levels to rise, and forced dramatic rooftop rescues and evacuations in late October. The area already had two months of unusually wet weather.

Aaron Schenk rafted down the Animas River through southwestern Colorado into New Mexico on Friday. He passed by an inundated event space. Aaron Schenk hide caption

Daniel Swain, a climate scientist with the University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources, a statewide research division, said El Niño is partly to blame for the recent floods. The severe thunderstorms were fueled by large amounts of moisture in the atmosphere.

That moisture, said Swain, comes from the record-warm waters off parts of the West Coast, which are driven by El Niño and a recent marine heat wave. He emphasizes that the heat wave can largely be attributed to human-caused climate change.

However, Swain cautioned that the wetter-than-average conditions do not mean an end to the ongoing water crisis in the Colorado River basin. Years of drought and growing demand for water have left Lake Powell and Lake Mead— reservoirs that supply water to millions of people in the Southwest—at record low levels.

"What would it take to refill all of the huge reservoirs in the Colorado system and to maintain them at that level for long enough to really change the bigger picture?" Swain asked.

"The amount of water that would be required to do that," he paused, "it's difficult to comprehend."

Low water levels at Wahweap Bay at Lake Powell along the Upper Colorado River Basin are pictured, June 9, 2021, at the Utah and Arizona border at Wahweap, Ariz. Ross D. Franklin/AP hide caption

Similar to the overall U.S. weather trend this winter, forecasts show the Northeast could have both warmer temperatures and above-average precipitation.

Studies also suggest that El Niño can strengthen and accelerate the frequency of nor'easters by pushing warm, moist air to the north. And nor'easters, along with high tides, can lead to coastal erosion up and down the coast.

Forecasters are predicting that the northeast and Mid-Atlantic will experience similar amounts of flooding as happened during a 2023 El Niño event — a record setting year.

They say there could also be more snow. During the strong El Niño of 2016, a brutal northeast blizzard produced over 30 inches of snow and 60 mph wind gusts.

"El Niño can favor a more mild than average winter in the northern United States, but listen, in the winter months it doesn't mean you won't have snow," Graham, NOAA's director, said. "It doesn't erase the risk."

The greatest chance for above-average precipitation is predicted for areas of the southern U.S. There also may be snow in areas that rarely, if ever, see it. That includes northern Texas, NOAA's Gottschalck said.

Florida is already bracing for heavy amounts of rain in areas that don't normally experience huge downpours. The northern part of the state, along with southern parts of Georgia and South Carolina, have the highest chance across the continental U.S. of above-normal precipitation this winter. This follows an unusually quiet Atlantic hurricane season with only one named hurricane, Isaias, roiling the Florida panhandle.

Still, the state's tornado risk is typically higher during El Niño years. In 1998, during an intense El Niño event, NPR's member station, WUSF, reported that Central Florida experienced one of the deadliest tornado outbreaks, killing 42 people.

The Midwest—especially in the Upper Midwest—is expected to have a smaller snowpack than normal because of warmer temperatures.

Michigan, Ohio and Indiana tend to be drier during El Niño winters, while Iowa tends to be wetter, according to the U.S. Geological Survey.

Gottschalck said the drought situations are expected to persist this winter across the Midwest. In fact, there is growing concern the current drought will only get worse.

#458. Harry Kane is 'still processing' his Ballon d'Or win, so he went back to the only thing he knows

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

MUNICH – Less than an hour into a casual second-round cup match, a pocket of Bayern Munich fans in one corner of the Allianz Arena had moved on from reveling over Michael Olise's brace against second-division side Magdeburg. Their attention was instead on the group of substitutes who were warming up behind the opponent's goal and one person in particular.

It was a likely place for Harry Kane to spend a Wednesday evening, no matter the circumstances. Considering Bayern's relative strengths, the coaching staff had already hoped to give Kane the night off, especially with Borussia Dortmund awaiting this weekend and a trip to Atletico Madrid in the UEFA Champions League coming up on Tuesday. This was no ordinary midweek cup game, though, the pre-match guard of honor from his teammates as clear a sign as any of the occasion. It was Kane's first time back since winning the Ballon d'Or on Monday, doing so in his hometown of London.

The warm-ups were the only glimpse the crowd got of him on Wednesday, Bayern getting business done swiftly with a 6-1 win. Yet, Kane's appearance on the sidelines alone was celebratory, both after becoming the first Englishman to win the individual accolade in 25 years and the first player from the Bundesliga to do it since Matthias Sammer accomplished the feat in 1996. It was the type of thing that required an afterparty that allegedly stretched long after a delayed, bloated ceremony came to an end at around 11 p.m. local time, though Kane insisted he "didn't party too hard." It also allowed him to follow up a crowning achievement the only way he knows how – "straight back to work."

Kane trained on Tuesday and would have been ready to play if called upon on Wednesday, and is expected to prepare as usual for Saturday's clash with Dortmund. It comes as little surprise for a player who has rarely chased individual accolades. He has repeatedly insisted that his goal every four years is to win the World Cup with England, not the golden boot and left boyhood club Tottenham Hotspur for greater collective opportunities with Bayern in 2023 despite being just 47 goals behind Alan Shearer's Premier League goalscoring record at the age of 30. The spotlight has followed Kane for much of his career but he has always chosen to share it. There is no flexibility with the attention the Ballon d'Or carries, though; only one person can win it, no matter how team-oriented that person happens to be.

"It's strange," Kane said. "It's not my normal thing to enjoy. Even the guys, when I was walking out to warm up and they're all there clapping. I don't know. I get quite, maybe, embarrassed by it, but I know this is a big deal and I felt that obviously in the event, I felt that since with the reception and how much messages I've got from not just friends and family but people sending my number to other coaches and people just that have known me or know of me and they all wanted to just say well done. I think I'm still processing everything, to be honest, but look, I know it's a really big deal so I appreciate everyone, and you know, even on Saturday, I know we're going to do something before the game and all this, and it's important to do because it's a big celebration as well."

Even in his hometown on Monday, surrounded both by loved ones and his Bayern teammates who chanted his name in the London Palladium as Ronaldo revealed he had won the sport's ultimate individual prize, Kane felt out of place.

"A lot of nerves and just different emotions compared to a football match or anything like that and a lot of waiting around and just trying to picture what it'd be like if I did win but honestly, just really special," he said. "The support I've had from friends, family, fans, text messages, social media has just been overwhelming almost."

There is a lot Kane has not considered about life post-Ballon d'Or, either, like where the trophy might rest or what will come of the gold-flaked schnitzel that was made by a Bavarian restaurant and delivered to his home.

"I didn't actually eat it yesterday," he said. "With the match being today, I have a routine of what I eat and stuff but now, obviously, I appreciate it. It's a nice tradition that we have so to have a gold one, let's just keep them coming."

True to form, Kane has found the most comfort in a busy week in a return to his familiar routine.

"I think just being back to football," he said. "It feels like the Freiburg game [on Sunday] was six weeks ago. So much has happened in 48 hours. It was nice just to be back. A bit of normality, seeing the fans, getting a reception before the game, obviously, and then honestly just being back with the boys and just watching football and watching the boys play again so it's been a whirlwind of a 48 hours, and it's nice to just kind of be here and feel at home again."

He admitted he needs to add a new item to the routine at home, though – protecting the Ballon d'Or from his four children, aged nine to three.

"My wife and kids are still in London right now," he noted. "They get back on Friday so I haven't actually seen my kids since I won. I spoke to them, obviously, on FaceTime and stuff so I'm excited to see them and show them the trophy. I know they'll be trying to play with it and pick it up and it's quite heavy and so I might have to keep an eye on it this time but normally, the two boys, especially, just smash the ball around and then whoever wins picks up the trophy that I bring home but I'll have to explain it's a little bit more precious, so we'll have to keep an eye on it."

#193. 2 right-wing provocateurs facing election-related charges in Minnesota

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

James O'Keefe and Cam Higby face charges after posting undercover videos.

Two right-wing provocateurs, James O'Keefe and Cam Higby, are facing election-related charges in Minnesota after posting videos of an undercover stunt intended to expose alleged deficiencies in election security.

Hennepin County Attorney Mary Moriarty said each man faces a count of unlawful voting and identity theft.

"Elections are the bedrock of democracy and their protection is one of our highest priorities," Moriarty said in a statement. "Individuals engaging in unlawful conduct related to our elections will be held accountable."

O'Keefe, a self-proclaimed "guerilla journalist" infamous for his undercover videos, and Higby, a social media influencer, posted the video at the heart of the charges in August. It shows the two men approaching election judges at multiple voting precincts in Minneapolis wearing burqas, speaking in fake accents, and allegedly misrepresenting their identities, in an effort to expose what O'Keefe and Higby say are gaps in the rules that allow residents to vouch for the identity of other voters.

The video, which remains pinned on Higby's X account, is called "I Wore Burqa To EXPOSE Election FRAUD In Minneapolis."

No ballots were cast in the course of their efforts.

Prosecutors said Wednesday that O'Keefe and Higby deceived the election judges by using the identities of real Minnesota voters "without those voters' knowledge or permission" in violation of state law. Each count carries up to five years in prison and a fine of up to $10,000. Neither man is in custody, according to charging documents.

In a statement, O'Keefe called the charges "weaponized lawfare" and defended his undercover video as journalism protected by the Constitution. He vowed to fight the charges.

"This is not political," he said. "It is fundamental. It is about whether citizens still have the right to question their government."

In a post shared on X, Higby said, "No laws were broken. They want me in PRISON for exposing election vulnerabilities in their state." O'Keefe did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Steve Simon, the state's top election official, said in a statement that "any attempt to disrupt or deceive our election workers will not be tolerated."

The charges announced Wednesday in Minnesota are not O'Keefe's first brush with the law. In 2010, O'Keefe pleaded guilty in Louisiana to a misdemeanor charge of entering a federal property under false pretenses after he posed as a telephone repairman to gain access to a U.S. senator's New Orleans office.

#237. China’s tech darlings are losing their shine. Here’s what’s behind the sell-off

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

China's technology stocks are losing their luster as investors question whether the country's artificial intelligence boom can deliver profits strong enough to justify lofty valuations.

The STAR 50 Index plunged over 40% from its July peak as concerns mount over returns on AI investments. The index tracks 50 of the largest and most liquid companies on Shanghai's technology-focused STAR Market — home to semiconductor, artificial intelligence and other high-tech firms.

Even battery giant CATL, long considered one of China's technology success stories, has come under pressure, falling 37% since the start of the year.

What started as profit-taking after a strong rally has turned into a broader reckoning over whether earnings can support current valuations.

"I think the sell-off primarily started as a valuation correction and some profit taking, but now there is a critical look happening at whether earnings can justify the AI excitement," said Benjamin Cavender of China Market Research Group.

China’s technology rally accelerated after DeepSeek emerged in early 2025, fueling optimism that domestic companies could challenge U.S. counterparts in artificial intelligence.

That optimism has since faded as investors question how quickly companies can turn heavy AI spending into profits.

Tai Hui, Asia-Pacific chief market strategist at JPMorgan Asset Management, said investors want to see how China's tech giants translate their AI spending into earnings, noting that several AI initial public offerings attracted strong initial demand before enthusiasm faded.

However, the challenge extends beyond technology.

China's prolonged property downturn has battered household confidence, while weak consumption continues to weigh on the advertising and e-commerce businesses of major internet platforms.

Vey-Sern Ling, senior equity advisor at Union Bancaire Privée, warned that rising AI investments could further pressure near-term earnings, with longer-term monetization prospects still uncertain.

"Chinese tech stock valuations have reached multi-year lows, which should limit downside, though upside catalysts remain elusive," he said.

The pressure is also evident among China's battery champions.

CATL, a major beneficiary of the global electric-vehicle and energy-storage boom, posted roughly 59% revenue growth in the third quarter, while net profit rose only about 15%.

Competition, shifting product mix and changes to taxes and rebates are squeezing profitability despite its dominant global position.

"CATL's problem is the gap between growth and profitability," Cavender said.

Ling also warned that lower unit profitability could weigh on near-term earnings, even as CATL maintains its global leadership.

The gap between rapid growth and slower profit gains highlights a broader challenge for Chinese technology manufacturers: rapid expansion is not necessarily translating into stronger margins.

Not all Chinese technology stocks face the same pressures.

Brendan Ahern, chief investment officer at KraneShares, noted that Shanghai's STAR Board companies are benefiting from semiconductor spending by Chinese and American tech giants.

Hong Kong-listed stocks, meanwhile, remain more exposed to sluggish domestic consumption, due to their heavier weighting in consumer-facing internet platforms.

Meanwhile, JPMorgan's Hui said China's property downturn remains a fundamental obstacle, with households increasingly parking savings in cash rather than equities.

He nevertheless saw limited downside for broader Chinese benchmarks, citing relatively undemanding valuations and light positioning among international investors.

Some strategists even believe the sell-off may be creating opportunities.

Jason Hsu, chairman and chief investment officer at Rayliant Global Advisors, said the sell-off in Chinese technology stocks has deepened alongside weakness in U.S. equities, as investors grow more skeptical about returns on AI spending.

A recovery in American technology stocks could therefore provide support for Chinese equities.

But cheaper valuations alone may not be enough, according to Cavender, saying that a sustained rebound would require stabilizing earnings, clearer returns on AI spending and stronger domestic demand.

For investors, China's technology sector is no longer about how fast companies can grow, but whether that growth can turn into sustainable profits.

Barack Obama wants to set the record straight: he loves The Cheesecake Factory as much as the next dad.

#389. ‘The Mosquito Bowl’ Review: Football Heroes Take to the Battlefield in Peter Berg’s Emptily Macho WWII Epic

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

This pummelling Netflix production seeks pathos amid the gunfire as it tells the true story of four college football stars joining the Marines in the wake of Pearl Harbor, but not one of these noble warriors feels like a real person.

No one familiar with the oeuvre of Peter Berg — the action merchant behind the Afghanistan war drama “Lone Survivor” and the improbably pumped-up boardgame adaptation “Battleship” — should approach “The Mosquito Bowl” expecting great restraint or good taste. Ostensibly, Berg’s Netflix-backed combat epic is fighting the same fight as Terrence Malick’s “The Thin Red Line,” likewise charting the physical and psychological torment of American soldiers in Guadalcanal during the Pacific Theater of the Second World War. But they are two very different history lessons. There’s not a hint of Malick’s poetic anti-war melancholy in Berg’s brawny, cacophonous Semper Fi salute to American military brotherhood, which certainly doesn’t stray from standard war-is-hell sentiment — rarely, in fact, has war been depicted as more loudly ruinous — but positively revels in onscreen carnage, as if to underline the mettle of its Marine heroes. Related Stories 'KPop Demon Hunters,' 'Squid Game' Stars Fete Netflix's Korea Decade as Ted Sarandos Teases 'Tantara,' 'All of Us Are Dead' Season 2 'KPop Demon Hunters' Coming Soon to Toca Boca World Gaming App

Not that said mettle is in any danger of going unnoticed. For the four men at the pretty hollow center of “The Mosquito Bowl” aren’t just soldiers but sportsmen — stars of the college football circuit who bring their strength and smarts from the field to the battlefield. And if that comparison sounds glib to you, then buckle up, because the film has plenty more: “The Marine Corps is the greatest offensive line in the history of football,” says one character, a sentiment that sounds sort of rousing without making much sense. That goes for much of the script by Berg and Mark L. Smith, which is drawn from a popular 2022 historical novel by Buzz Bissinger — the journalist whose 1990 bestseller “Friday Night Lights” became the basis of Berg’s sturdy 2004 football drama and the more popular TV series spun off from it.

Berg and Bissinger are cousins; the screen adaptation of “The Mosquito Bowl” was likely a fait accompli all along. Yet while the book’s ennobling mixture of Greatest Generation portraiture and brutal evocation of the rigors of combat is perfectly tailored to the filmmaker’s interests, it also hands the screenwriters a bit of a structural red herring. The title refers to a football game that the principals were tasked with organizing on Guadalcanal; it was played on Christmas Eve 1944 between the 4th and 29th regiments of the 6th Marine Division, and featured a wealth of drafted football talents who had set aside their sporting careers to serve their country. Outwardly, the book and film both seek to imbue this event with the significance of the famed Christmas truce soccer matches between British and German forces in the no-man’s-land of the First World War.

When push comes to shove, however, the game simply isn’t all that consequential. It’s skipped over surprisingly swiftly on the page, and despite much anticipatory buildup, the film, too, struggles to make a climactic set-piece of an event that has considerably less visceral impact than the various juddering, blood-on-the-lens battle scenes on either side of it. Nor does it work as a human interlude, because the human dimension of “The Mosquito Bowl” is so flimsy. Its four principals are colorless, interchangeable alpha males, uniformly stout of build and square of jaw; they’re granted mildly differing character attributes by the script, but even as the closing credits roll, you’d be hard pressed to remember who was who.

John McLaughry (Nicholas Galitzine), a graduate (and future football coach) of Brown University, is given the leading perspective — which is to say he occupies the framing scenes that have him visiting a college football field years after the war with a stoic, misty gaze, and reflecting vaguely on all that has been won and lost. We rewind to 1941, when the earnest young man’s budding future with the New York Giants is put on ice by a surge of patriotic duty in the wake of Pearl Harbor; the same goes for jocular Purdue grad Tony Butkovich (Ray Nicholson, appropriating his father Jack’s rakish perma-grin), soulfully withdrawn Wisconsin Badgers star Dave Schreiner (stage star Tom Francis, a Tony nominee for “Sunset Blvd.”), and fellow Wisconsin grad Bob Baumann (Bill Skarsgård), whose personality lands somewhere between those last two — the writers having run out of all-American archetypes quite early on.

Historical dates are lightly fudged to give the four men a shared soldierly narrative, and so they collectively endure the grueling trials of Parris Island basic training, horrible but familiar from many a military drama, before shipping out to the Pacific, where the shattering, deafening mayhem that awaits them is horrible but familiar from many a war movie. Knowing that he has nothing new to show us, Berg’s solution is to show us ever more of the same, ladling on the frank images of death and devastating injury with relentless rat-a-tat-tat sound design to the point that the film does attain a blunt sensory force.

The aesthetic, with DP Jacques Jouffret’s aggressively desaturated palette and nauseous POV shooting coupled with antsy, abrasive editing, is ripped from “Saving Private Ryan,” but cranked up to video game levels, with a grinding, guitar-heavy score by post-rock band Explosions in the Sky; the film’s technical proficiency so overwhelms its slender character-building that it begins to feel almost heartless.

The actors, all talented, are given no scope to be any more than capable. There’s scant room for inner life here, nor for reflection on anything more specific than war’s general cruelty — though, needless to say, the enemy gets even shorter shrift. There’s a jingoistic undertow to “The Mosquito Bowl,” with its flag-waving celebration of football and Marine fortitude, that sits queasily with decontextualized shots of Japanese soldiers committing ritual seppuku or being summarily executed by American captors, or the main characters’ repeated gung-ho statements about “killing the Japanese.” The film strains to say something about the morally corrupting effects of warfare, but doesn’t challenge its heroic leads on the racist invective they parrot, or circle back to any regret they may feel deeper into their service. Trivial and shoehorned in as it seems, when the titular football game rolls along, you can practically feel the film’s sigh of relief: at last, a blessed few minutes of macho combat without consequences — or politics.

I'd been teaching English in South Korea for four years when I learned about the Haenyeo — female freedivers who harvest seafood without using modern equipment. I was interested. So, in the summer of 2018, I completed diving lessons with the hopes that maybe I could work with them one day.

A few years later, my Korean husband, a scuba diver, read on a diving group chat that a small village on the southwestern side of Jeju Island was recruiting new divers. Their youngest diver was 68 — I wanted to give it a try.

In January 2024, I started as a diving intern, and after two years of training, I received my Haenyeo license in April.

I was born in upstate New York, the middle of three kids. I went to SUNY Oswego and graduated with a degree in communications.

After graduating in 2012, I didn't know what I wanted to do. I applied to a few graduate schools and jobs, but nothing worked out. I was stressed out and wanted to leave. I thought about going to Japan to teach English, as I'd studied Japanese for a year while in university.

Instead, I settled for South Korea, where some schools offered housing. I didn't know much about Korea at that time — I wasn't really a big K-pop fan before I came.

So, just four months after graduating, I moved to Jinju, a three-and-a-half-hour high-speed train ride from Seoul, to work as an English teacher. In the mornings, I'd do 20-minute lessons with kids. In the afternoon, I'd have speaking classes.

I continued teaching until August 2020. That same year, I got married and started a pension business with my husband, while still teaching English on the side.

To get my license, I had to dive 50 days within a year, sell 1.2 million South Korean won, or $884, worth of catch, and get a unanimous vote from the divers in the collective. It took me two years.

Language was an issue; despite having spent years in the country, I still couldn't always understand what was happening. A lot of the work is physically demanding. If you don't feel good, you can't just push through. I can't dive if I have a cold or my sinuses are blocked because I can't equalize underwater. It's important to keep my body physically strong and healthy through working out.

I enjoy my life. Every day is different, and I do a lot of things besides diving. I run a pet hotel with my husband; sometimes I still tutor and give lectures. I work more hours, but I also have a lot more flexibility to pursue other things. I don't need permission for a break or a day off.

On normal days, I wake up and take the dogs for a walk. Then, I go diving depending on the weather. If it's not great, I only dive for two or three hours. A normal day of diving may take four or five. After that, I take the dogs for another walk.

In our village, we typically dive five days a week. We'll take our catch and accumulate it after one cycle. After that, we'll sell it to the regional fishing co-operative. They'll pay our president, and she then distributes the money.

Being a diver isn't a full-time job. A lot of what we earn depends on the catch. I make around $2,000 a year. Teaching is much more financially stable. But I love learning from women who have been carrying on this work for generations.

When I'm diving, nothing else matters. I just have to focus on diving and catching seafood. Nobody can call me. I don't have to look at my phone and waste time doomscrolling.

When I'm deep underwater, it can be meditative, especially on days when the ocean is calm, and there's plenty to catch. I see myself wedded to this village for the rest of my life.
