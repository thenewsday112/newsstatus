#275. Apple's first touchscreen MacBook Pro will set you back nearly 3 bands

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple's MacBook Pro is entering its iPad era.

Apple announced its first MacBook Pro with a touchscreen on Wednesday during its "Pro and Tell" event in Los Angeles. The touchscreen models mark a major evolution of Apple's traditional MacBooks that rely on keyboards, computer mouses, or trackpads for input.

The laptop, featuring what Apple calls its "PROLED XDR display," is powered by the company's M5 Pro and M5 Max chips and includes a Dynamic Island cutout in the screen, similar to recent iPhones.

The touchscreen MacBook Pros are available for preorder on Wednesday and will officially launch on November 6. Prices for the 14-inch model start at $2,899, and the 16-inch model starts at $3,399.

That's a notable price jump from the 14-inch MacBook Pro with M6, which Apple announced on Tuesday. That model starts at $1,999 and has a Liquid Retina XDR display.

Apple's price hikes come as the tech industry weathers a global memory shortage driven by the expansion of AI and cloud infrastructure. With other PC makers also boosting prices, consumer demand across the industry has wavered, according to Counterpoint Research. The tech market research firm said global PC shipments have fallen 21% year-to-year from July to September.

Apple has raised prices for its products several times in recent months, including in June when the company raised the cost of some Mac and iPad models by as much as $300.

The touchscreen MacBook Pros are the latest product drop from Apple. The company announced a new iPad Mini with an OLED screen on Monday and a new iMac with its M6 chip on Tuesday.

#438. Peltola campaign aides, DSCC officials helped recruit second Dan Sullivan in Alaska race: NYT

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

People working with Democratic Senate nominee Mary Peltola's campaign and senior aides at the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee participated in efforts to recruit a man named Daniel J. Sullivan to enter the Alaska Senate contest, in a bid to draw votes away from incumbent Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan, The New York Times reported Wednesday.

The Times — citing interviews, texts and documents prepared for a potential lawsuit against Peltola's campaign — reported that "not only did Ms. Peltola's team work with people trying to recruit Mr. Sullivan to run for Senate, but the effort was much broader."

"The group worked to coax other would-be spoilers into the race, to dilute the incumbent's support and help catapult the Democrat to victory," the newspaper reported.

Peltola's former campaign manager Elisa Rios told the Times that Peltola was not directly involved in the project, but she was "definitely aware," according to the report.

The Times noted Rios last week tried to retract comments she made confirming the effort to recruit spoilers.

Peltola's campaign and the DSCC "denied participating in efforts to recruit spoilers to the ballot in Alaska," The Times said.

The newspaper's report noted ballot-stacking tactics are "common in politics and generally considered legal — and have been employed by both parties in Alaska."

The report comes days after the Department of Justice filed a lawsuit allegingthe campaign by the non-incumbent Sullivan was geared toward him acting as a spoiler for the incumbent Sullivan because of voter confusion.

The DOJ has asked a federal judge in Alaska to block certification of any Senate race result "that may be impacted by voter confusion ... pending an investigation to determine whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan."

The winner of the election could determine whether Democrats regain a majority in the Senate, or if Republicans retain their current majority.

President Donald Trump has said he ordered the DOJ's lawsuit.

After the Times published its report, Trump demanded that Peltola and Daniel J. Sullivan both be removed from Alaska's election ballot.

Trump, in a Truth Social post, claimed the report showed Democrats "illegally" sought out the same-named man "in order to defraud, confuse, and obfuscate the Election, thereby taking votes away from the REAL Senator, Dan Sullivan."

Peltola's campaign, in a statement to CNBC on The Times article, said, "The Alaskans for Mary campaign has no involvement with either Sullivan campaign. Like Mary, our campaign is focused on lowering costs and fixing the rigged system in DC."

A DSCC spokesperson in a statement to CNBC said, "The DSCC is not involved with Dan J. Sullivan."

"Republicans have a long history of recruiting and propping up candidates in an attempt to boost their own campaigns – including recruiting a prisoner with a New York address to run against Mary Peltola and spending nearly half a million dollars to secure a spot on the ballot in Alaska for an Arizona resident in the Senate race this cycle," the spokesperson said.

The spokesperson pointed to several examples of Republicans promoting third-party and dark horse candidates in the current election cycle and past ones to increase chances of a GOP candidate winning a race.

Peltola's campaign and DSCC, as well as Alaska's election officials, have asked the judge overseeing the DOJ's lawsuit, Sharon Gleason, to dismiss the suit before Election Day, Nov. 3. The League of Women Voters of Alaska and the advocacy group Common Cause have also sought to intervene in the case to oppose the DOJ's efforts.

Alaska officials said the DOJ "lacks authority to intervene in state elections at all, much less in support of a favored candidate," and that the lawsuit and "requested relief cast a cloud over Alaska's election."

State officials said in a court filing that Alaska's Division of Elections "has taken significant steps to distinguish" between the two Sullivans on the ballot, "so that voters can easily determine which candidate is which."

Gleason on Monday evening fast-tracked the case, increasing the chance she will decide whether to dismiss the case in U.S. District Court in Anchorage before Election Day.

Daniel J. Sullivan prevailed in a legal fight that ended with the Alaska Supreme Court allowing him on the nonpartisan primary ballot over the opposition of state election officials who argued his presence would confuse voters.

He won enough votes in that contest to win a place as one of four candidates on the general election ballot.

On that ballot, his name is spelled out "Sullivan, Daniel J. Jr." with no other words next to it.

Directly below his name, Sen. Sullivan is listed as Sullivan, Dan. S. with the words "Registered Republican, incumbent" next to his name in parentheses.

Peltola, a former U.S. House member, is the only Democrat on the ballot.

Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, which allows voters to rank candidates by preference. That method could allow voters to rank both Sullivans as choices, allowing a vote for the non-incumbent to be transferred to the incumbent senator if the non-incumbent is eliminated in the first round of counting.

The Times quoted Peltola's former campaign manager Rios, who the newspaper reported was fired in August, as saying, "Alaskan voters need to know" about the effort to recruit others into the race.

"There was a lot of speculation, and voters should get the full picture when making their decisions," Rios was quoted as saying.

Rios told The Times last week that she wanted to retract her prior statements.

"As discussed on our call earlier today, I am confirming that I have retracted all statements made regarding the 2026 Alaska Senate Race in connection with any article you may or may not be writing," Rios wrote, according to The Times. "I am not willing to be a source for any story in any capacity."

The newspaper said it told Rios she would be part of its article, and noted her interviews "were consistent with The Times's other reporting."

#346. Here's how the touchscreen on the MacBook Pro with PROLED display works

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The touch sensor isn't a separate layer of the screen.

A new touchscreen Mac requires new software, and with the announcement of the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR display, Apple detailed just how you'll use your fingers to navigate macOS. The new laptop has an integrated touch sensor built directly into the display. Apple claims this reduces latency, making it "feel like you're touching the content itself." It's worth noting most modern OLED displays with touchscreens handle touch input this way.

As you would expect, you can slide your finger across the screen to scroll through content, and pinch with two fingers to zoom in on photos, documents and web pages. Additionally, it's possible to draw directly on the screen with a finger, allowing you to doodle and annotate notes. Apple suggests professionals will find the touchscreen useful for interacting with 3D objects and getting a feel for how their iOS apps will work, while for everyday users will appreciate it in apps like Pages and Keynote.

Borrowing a page from the iPad, you can use your finger to hide the dock. Likewise when you use touch to interact with menus, there's more spacing between items to make it easier to select a specific one. None of the above is groundbreaking, but it never hurts to have multiple ways to interact with a device.

The new MacBook Pro sees Apple finally embracing an idea it has spent nearly two decades calling misguided. Steve Jobs famously hated even the thought of adding a touchscreen to the MacBook, going so far as to say using one would be "ergonomically terrible." His successor, former CEO Tim Cook, joked creating a laptop with tablet-like features would be akin to making a fridge with a built-in toaster. Now it will be a question of whether people will want to spend at least $2,899 for the privilege of owning a touchscreen Mac before Apple trickles the feature down to its more affordable computers.

#285. I'm the ex-VP of HR at Microsoft. Here's what the 60,000 employees mulling a buyout may not realize.

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Microsoft is offering buyout packages to some 60,000 employees, or more than 25% of the workforce. That's a huge number in any terms, and it sent a signal and shock wave through the industry this week.

I'm the former VP of HR for Microsoft, and although I've been away from the company for some time and have no insider information, having been on the inside at Microsoft and in the industry for 50 years, here is what Microsoft employees should be considering — and what they may not realize about taking a buyout offer.

During a buyout, position, tenure, level, and other factors are taken into account for each bespoke offer. The offer is likely to include compensation, benefit continuation, and possibly some acceleration or continuation of equity vesting.

That said, Microsoft might not offer more generous buyouts than severance packages, but companies usually offer better terms to people who take a voluntary separation than people who get laid off. That is not only to induce employees to go voluntarily, but also because the costs to the company are less if these workers voluntarily leave. There is less legal risk, time, and expense. It's also simply easier for the company to manage.

If you're offered a buyout, I usually find it could be the best offer you'll get. If it's not, you could be settling for the same offer as someone in a layoff and miss out on collecting unemployment.

If you get a buyout offer, looking at your budget should be your next step. Does the offer provide you with enough to retire? Enough to support an extended job search? The buyout can be a good forcing function for some very hard career choices.

Spend time looking at your cash flows, and pay particular attention to benefits. For many, healthcare costs are a substantial part of their budget. Weighing the buyout offer against continued coverage is often a tipping point in the decision.

Also consider any equity vesting dates you may have. Compare when you might vest with and without the offer, as those dates can mean a significant change in your investments. Whether you should take the buyout is highly dependent on both the offer and your situation. If you had retirement anywhere in sight, this offer might be just the trigger you need to move on. Perhaps the best offer you'll ever see.

However, if you need to continue working, there's a great deal more to consider.

The tech industry job market has been rough for the last year or so, and shows all the signs of getting worse. If the buyout doesn't afford you the ability to retire, you should plan on an extended period of job searching, maybe six months or more. It's tough out there. Be prepared.

One thing to consider if you need to continue working is that taking the buyout might give you a short head start in the job market. Assuming Microsoft and others follow this up with more layoffs like they did the last time, the technical job market will be flooded with quality people. Of course, you can search for a job while you have one, but taking the buyout gives you time for a full-time job search.

In any case, if a job search is in your future, don't delay. This market shows no sign of getting better.

Finally, if you're offered a buyout, don't ignore it. It's a sign. I'd read it as a clear signal from the company about your value, at least from their perspective. You can bet on the odds, but if follow-up layoffs happen, I'd bet that those who declined the buyout are somewhere on that list.

This buyout is the latest signal of a broad realignment of the job market, at least in the tech sector. Even if you aren't offered it or don't think you're at risk of later layoffs, you'd be wise to keep all your options open.

Get your résumé up to date. Spend time honing it, emphasizing the clearest set of skills you can. When a market is contracting, the safest bet is having a particular skill someone is looking for. When times are tight, the generalist is often the first person let go.

Work with and expand your network. Have coffee or a Zoom chat with everyone you know. Peers in other companies, even other industries, are invaluable when things get tough. Especially when the world is changing, and predicting the next hot spot is so hard, having as many warm connections across industries is a huge asset.

Don't go asking for jobs as much as keep those connections alive. You never know when someone's manager will say, "We need someone like that." And your friend can say, "I know just the person."

Whether you are offered a buyout, or whether you decide to take it or not, having a network of people to connect with is your single biggest career asset.

Carefully decide your near- and long-term financial future, and make the decision from hard facts. Then connect with people you know across industries, far and wide. Those connections will be excellent sounding boards and could well provide the path to your next role, or the friends you need in retirement.

They added that "the VEO is similar to a standard severance offer, which varies by market. After years of resisting, Apple today launched a MacBook Pro with a touchscreen starting at $2,899. The release comes nearly 10 years after the company first tried integrating touchscreens on its laptops, with its Touch Bar on a MacBook Pro, which it later went on to remove.

The new MacBook Pro more fully embraces the touchscreen, offering a new PROLED XDR Display in both 14-inch and 16-inch screen sizes. The device is powered by M5 Pro and M5 Max chips as options to choose from, along with up to 128GB of unified memory and up to 16TB of storage. The display has 2,000 nits peak HDR and SDR brightness and a 10,000,000:1 contrast ratio.

To make the new MacBook easy to use, Apple designed a hinge that stabilizes the screen when users touch it. This also helps to stabilize the camera when you are on a video call and trying to access content on the display.

In addition, the screen will include a Dynamic Island-style cutout instead of the notch that’s present on most of the current MacBooks. Plus, the new MacBook features a six-speaker array with a new woofer design for better sound.

The 14-inch Pro weighs 3.0 pounds (1.38 kg), and the 16-inch Pro weighs 4.0 pounds (1.82 kg) — lighter than its non-touch predecessors.

Both feature three Thunderbolt 5 ports, an HDMI port, an SDXC card slot, MagSafe, and a headphone jack. Users can hook up three external displays on the M5 Pro variant and up to four displays on the M5 Max variant.

The Touchscreen MacBook Pro 14-inch starts at $2,899, and the 16-inch version starts at $3,399.

Consumers can place a preorder for the new touchscreen MacBook Pro from October 30, and the device will be available starting November 6.

The new Touchscreen MacBook marks two marquee product launches for new CEO John Ternus, who also introduced the company’s first foldable, the iPhone Duo, in September. Various Apple executives, including Steve Jobs and Tim Cook, have scoffed at the idea of a touchscreen Mac in the past. Jobs famously called the idea “ergonomically terrible” at one point. However, rumors of the company working on such a variant started circulating nearly three years before today’s introduction.

The launch follows yesterday’s unveiling of a new standard MacBook Pro with the M6 chip in 14-inch size starting from $1,999.

#425. The Inside Story of Why Apple Finally Brought a Touch Display to the Mac

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s a bright day in Cupertino. Apple’s new CEO strolls to an outdoor seating area within the company’s otherworldly ring-shaped headquarters and pulls up a chair. An employee notes the angle and asks if he minds the sun. John Ternus doesn’t mind at all.

Nearly two months into his tenure, he’s been busy. While the tech world buzzes about AI agents and the upcoming IPO of a company reporting spectacular losses, Ternus’ leadership of a ridiculously profitable firm has so far been defined by a sequence of significant launches—the long-awaited foldable iPhone, a household device that can control digital appliances, new Mac Minis, and AirPods. Intentionally or not, all that activity sends a message that, even as AI dominates the public imagination, hardware products still rule.

Ternus doesn’t endorse that take, but he doesn’t object when I suggest it. “We’ve been working on these products for quite some time,” he says. “It feels like everyone here is just kind of riding high right now, enjoying it.”

I ask if Apple is about to break a long-imposed taboo and introduce a Macintosh with a touch screen.

We are referring to the new MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display that Apple is releasing today at an event in Los Angeles. It’s got a mighty M5 Pro or M5 Max chip and a show-stopping tandem OLED screen. (A tandem screen has multiple light-emitting layers, producing more dramatic contrasts.) But its most conspicuous feature is one that’s been deeply woven into iPhones, iPads, and even Apple Watches for years but not Apple’s laptops: a touch display. After years of resistance, a Mac screen has finally gone digital. As in fingers.

This turnaround is a big deal to Apple fans. Ternus shrugs off the drama. “The Mac is first and foremost an indirect input device, built around the trackpad and keyboard. But I think there’s times when it’s comfortable and handy to have touch.”

Ternus and Apple want to make sure that the Mac faithful don’t panic that the 42-year-old franchise is losing its identity to Apple’s gangbusters mobile products. “We needed to get to a point where adding touch didn’t make the product lesser in any way. It didn’t make it heavier. It didn’t make it thicker. That came together with the OLED screen, the best display we’ve ever put into a Mac,” Ternus told me. “All of these things, and, oh yeah, it has touch as well.”

Apple has a long history of refusing to implement certain desirable features, only to pivot when it feels the time is right. Steve Jobs once proclaimed iPods would never do video, until they did. He also once said “no one’s going to buy” a jumbo-size phone—and then Apple released a supersized iPhone 6. Then there was that time Jobs declared styluses inappropriate for tablets. Now the Apple Pencil works with iPads and the brand-new Duo.

The 2026 MacBook Pro instantly qualifies as the canonical example of this switcheroo. Apple executives had been on record regarding touch on PCs as a misbegotten idea. Never mind that just about every PC maker not headquartered in Cupertino has implemented touch on their laptops. And customers of Microsoft, Dell, Acer, Lenovo, and HP have snapped them up. Clearly, people wanted touch on their laptop displays. Even toddlers knew it—put some rug rats in front of a computer and their little arms shoot straight to the screen in futile attempts to swipe and scroll as they do on their iPads. People were baffled why Apple—the pioneer in touch displays since the iPhone—was so steadfast in its rejection.

In 2015, while doing a story about a new iMac, I pressed Phil Schiller, then the senior vice president of worldwide product marketing, on this very question. Usually, top Apple executives field such queries with cryptic remarks. In this case, Schiller began a filibuster. “I’m going to get a little bit philosophical,” he said, then launched into what I later described as the Grand Unified Theory of Apple Hardware.

He explained that every Apple hardware product—the Watch, iPad, iMac, MacBook—is basically a computer and a piece of glass. Each one has a specific function. And each one has an interface appropriate to the hardware. Multitouch is fine for products like the iPhone, he said—but not the Macintosh. He provided two reasons: ergonomics and the software interface.

“From the ergonomic standpoint, we have studied this pretty extensively, and we believe that on a desktop scenario, where you have a fixed keyboard, having to reach up to do touch interfaces is uncomfortable,” he told me.

As for the interface, Schiller argued that Apple’s two main operating systems were built on different design principles. The iPhone and iPad’s iOS was multitouch from the get-go, and didn’t use macOS conventions that require precision, such as cursors and tiny boxes to close windows. “These two worlds are different on purpose, and that’s a good thing—we can optimize around the best experience for each of those and not try to mesh them together into a least-common-denominator experience,” he told me.

A year later, when Apple introduced a MacBook Pro with the ill-fated “touch bar”—a dynamic strip of virtual function keys above the keyboard’s top row—Schiller doubled down on the view that touch should never find its way into a Mac display. He said that such a move would mean totally redesigning the menu bar for fingers, and that would clash with the experience of pointer devices like a touchpad or mouse. “You can’t optimize for both,” he said.

To double-check this assumption, Apple conducted user tests with touchscreens on Mac. “What the heck, we could be wrong,” Schiller told me then. “We’ve absolutely come away with the belief that it isn’t the right thing to do. Our instincts were correct.”

When I met with Apple executives Johny Srouji and Greg Joswiak this week to discuss the touchscreen MacBook Pro, you bet I invoked Schiller’s list of concerns. (Apple’s reps chose not to make Schiller, who now holds the title of “Apple Fellow,” available for an interview. In fact, they laughed when I asked.) It wasn’t only Schiller drawing a red line. In 2021, Ternus told journalist Joanna Stern, “The Mac is totally optimized for indirect input. We haven’t really felt a reason to change that.”

“Ten years is a very, very long time,” says Srouji, Apple’s chief hardware officer. “Many things have changed, whether it's the display technology, the custom silicon, et cetera.” Eventually, he told me, the confluence of chips, display tech, and software architecture “met our bar.” “I like my engineers to be gated by physics, not imagination or timelines,” he says.

Joswiak, who holds Schiller’s old title of SVP of worldwide marketing, concedes that touch was overdue, at least for users with sophisticated needs. Its pro customers—engineers, scientists, software developers, and media creators—were impatient with Apple for its failure to let them interact directly with the screen. “Most pros own an iPad, and almost every pro owns an iPhone,” Joswiak says. “So this is something that they’re natural with. There’s a generation of young creators and young people using our MacBook Pros that expect to touch.”

Between the lines, Srouji and Joswiak seem to consider their competitors’ touchscreen implementations shabby and lazy. The Apple ethos, as every one of its executives will happily explain to anyone who asks, is an expectation of quality that’s sky-high. “If you’re working on something easy, you’re wasting your time,” says Srouji, who has been at Apple since 2008 and has pretty much driven its innovation by spearheading the company’s charge to custom-build silicon.

“The easy things, I want to outsource,” he continues. “So the custom silicon is hard. The touch is hard. The display is hard. Everything we build is hard.” This swaggering philosophy dates from the Steve Jobs days and, in 2026, stands in contrast to the endgame of the ascendant AI industry, which hopes to turn over the toughest parts of industrial production to superintelligent entities.

Srouji presents Mac touch as a suite of innovations, starting with that screen that Ternus described as the Mac’s best ever. It also involves custom silicon and a rearchitected thermal system to prevent the display from feeling hot when people pinch, stretch, and swipe on it. Srouji contends that current touchscreen laptops suffer from “wobble” when fingers make contact with the display; to minimize this effect, Apple has redesigned the hinge on the MacBook Pro screen to stabilize it.

When I get a demo of the 16-inch version (as with previous Pros, it’s also available in a 14-inch version), I see what they mean about the display. It’s noticeably dense and bright, and the obsidian blacks are the deepest I’ve seen on a computer screen. In part because the OLED screen doesn’t require backlighting, this MacBook weighs less than its predecessor.

#535. Flu Season Is Back: How to Protect Your Family and Prevent Spread at Home

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Flu season is back, and families want practical ways to limit the spread at home. Here is what to know about timing, symptoms, prevention and medical care.

Flu season in the northern hemisphere typically runs October through March and can stretch into May, according to Mayo Clinic. Southern hemisphere flu usually falls between April and September. Outbreaks often begin in classrooms, where younger children with developing immune systems are most vulnerable.

Common flu symptoms include fever or chills, cough, sore throat, runny or stuffy nose, muscle aches, headaches and fatigue, according to the CDC. Vomiting and diarrhea can also occur, more often in children. Most people recover within two weeks, but complications such as pneumonia can be life-threatening, and flu can worsen conditions like asthma and chronic heart disease.

Flu viruses travel in droplets when an infected person coughs, sneezes or talks, and can also spread through contaminated surfaces, Mayo Clinic explains. A person is typically contagious from about a day before symptoms appear until roughly five to seven days afterward. David A. Cabezas, a family medicine specialist at UF Health Urgent Care, recommends isolating the sick person, wearing masks, washing hands often and improving ventilation to limit household spread.

Clean high-touch surfaces regularly with EPA-approved disinfectants, focusing on doorknobs, light switches, countertops, phones and remotes, according to University of Rochester Medicine. The American Lung Association advises carrying hand sanitizer with at least 60 percent alcohol when soap and water are not available. Amy Pridemore, assistant clinical professor at Auburn University College of Nursing, told Campus Insights Media keeping sick children home until they are fever-free for 24 hours helps stop germs from moving through schools. “The younger they are, the more likely they are to get sick because they don’t have that strong immune system yet. That’s why you see a lot of illnesses start in the kindergarten classrooms and in the pre-K classrooms before they get a little older and develop that immune system a little more,” Pridemore explained.

Higher-risk groups include young children, adults over 65, pregnant people and those with weakened immune systems or chronic conditions such as asthma, diabetes and heart disease, according to Mayo Clinic.

Everyone 6 months of age and older should get a flu vaccine every season, especially people at high risk, according to the American Red Cross. The CDC maintains a list of who should and shouldn’t get the shot. Flu vaccination during the 2019-2020 season prevented an estimated 7 million illnesses, 3 million medical visits, 100,000 hospitalizations and 7,000 deaths in the United States, the CDC reports. Pridemore said the shot helps the body recognize and fight the virus, making illness less severe even for people who still get sick.

Antiviral drugs are the primary treatment and require a prescription, according to the CDC. They work best when started within two days of symptoms and can shorten illness by about a day. Otherwise healthy people can often recover at home with rest, fluids and over-the-counter remedies. Seek emergency care for difficulty breathing, chest pain, persistent dizziness, seizures or severe weakness. In children, warning signs also include rapid breathing, bluish lips or nail beds and signs of dehydration such as no tears when crying.
