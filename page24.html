#446. All the Amazon Echo devices announced today: Echo Time, Alexa Photo Frame and more

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Along with speaker and Echo Show upgrades, Amazon unveiled a smart alarm clock and photo frame.

Apple isn't the only major tech company announcing new devices this week. Amazon has a bunch of new and updated products to show off as well. The company has been dripfeeding its reveals over the last several weeks, and now it's announcing its latest Echo devices.

Along with updated Echo speakers and a revamped Echo Show 16, there are some brand new devices in the mix. Echo Time is a smart alarm clock, while the Alexa Smart Frame is a way for you to show off family photos (and ask questions to an AI assistant, if you want). The company also has a new Echo Studio speaker with a camera.

Here's a peek at all the Echo devices Amazon announced today.

Amazon says it refreshed the Echo Dot with an upgraded speaker. There's an extra beamform microphone, which brings the total in the device to four, while the company says it updated the noise reduction algorithm, so the speaker should be able to more easily understand your Alexa commands.

The Echo Dot now has an AZ3 chip that's said to allow for "faster, more natural conversations with Alexa+." Voice ID can recognize the user for personalized features like access to their own playlists.

This speaker can function as a smart home hub, with support for devices that use the Zigbee, Thread and Matter standards. In addition, it can act as a Wi-Fi extender for Eero routers.

The new Echo Dot is available today. It comes in one of four colors (graphite, glacier white, agave green and coral pink) and it costs $99. A kids version comes with a year of access to Kids+ and Sleep Studio services like Calm. This model (which is available in designs called octopus and penguin) costs $110.

There's a fresh version of another of Amazon's speakers, the Echo Studio. This new model has a camera built in. Amazon says the camera won't record footage. Instead, it acts as a way for you to get a live view of what's going on in your home, such as to see what your pets are up to while you're out.

The camera supports face recognition, which is used for personalization (in similar fashion to Apple's HomeView). There's Voice ID support as well. You can also access Alexa+ visual intelligence features and ask the assistant questions based on what the camera can see.

Perhaps the most interesting thing about the camera is that it can help tune audio through a feature called Advanced Room Adaptation. It uses Omnisense to detect how many people are in the space then adapt the audio for them and the acoustics of the room. The tool can also automatically adjust the volume based on where you are at a given moment.

Moreover, the Echo Studio with Camera can be used as part of an Alexa Home Theater system. The device costs $250 and an optional stand is $40. The regular Echo Studio remains available.

Next up is something new, the Alexa Photo Frame. As you might imagine, this is a smart picture frame. Amazon says it designed this to be subtle and look like a normal photo frame.

The Alexa Photo Frame starts at $200 for a model with a black frame. If you'd prefer one with a real birch or walnut frame, you'll need to fork over $229.

Also new is Echo Time, a device that has zero chance of replacing my beloved Alarmo. It's a smart alarm clock that's supposed to help folks sleep better via an array of wind-down and wake-up features.

Amazon says Echo Time has an ultrasonic transducer that can detect when you're sleeping. It uses light to help wake you up on a schedule that's attuned to your circadian rhythm. So, if you tell Alexa+ you need to be awake by a certain time, the clock can detect when you're in a lighter part of your sleep cycle and start rousing you then (anywhere between 10 and 60 minutes before you've gotta get up). There are a range of alarm tones to choose from too.

There's no camera in the Echo Time, which has hundreds of clock faces available. The 2.3-inch, circular screen can drop to under 0.3 nits of brightness, so it shouldn't disturb you at night.

Echo Time is available today and it costs $199. The color options are graphite, glacier white and coral pink.

A kids version includes Sleep Studio, a year of Kids+ access and different clock face options. It's available in grape and cosmic lime designs, and it costs $210. Parents can manage their kid's Echo Time.

Last but not least is the latest Echo Show. The Echo Show 16 is said to be 40 percent thinner with bezels that are 80 percent thinner than previous models. Amazon says there's a Nanomatte option available for the 16-inch display. This features the same anti-glare tech it used for the Amazon Alexa Tablet Pro 12.

There are several Screen Styles you can switch between, namely a family organizer, photo frame, Fire TV interface and a smart home hub. The latter includes a new feature called Alexa Home Map. It can use cameras to map your house and show where various devices are, which could help you control and manage smart home devices. Omnisense tech can detect when you enter the room and adjust things like the thermostat automatically.

The device will organize all of your Alexa conversations into cards to help you revisit topics that you've discussed with the assistant. These are arranged by topic and you can pin cards for easy access. These are also available on the Alexa app and website.

On the audio front, the Echo Show 16 boasts four beamform microphones, eight woofers and two tweeters. Amazon says the 13MP camera is the most advanced one it's ever included in this product line — it can seemingly recognize users from up to 17 feet away. This is the first Echo Show with night vision support too. There's a button that can disable the camera and mics.

Pre-orders for the Echo Show 16 open today and the device will ship by the end of the year. The standard model costs $500 and one with a Nanomatte display will run you $570. If you pre-order an Echo Show 16, you'll get a $100 discount.

The Echo Show 16 is primarily designed to be hung on walls, though you can snap up a regular stand for $70. A stand that can tilt and swivel costs $125.

#414. Luxury round-the-world cruise ends abruptly as fuel costs skyrocket

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A luxury cruise around the world has abruptly stopped, stranding passengers in Cambodia, as its operator blamed skyrocketing fuel costs.

The Villa Vie Odyssey, a luxury vessel with rooms costing up to $11,000 a month, will disembark passengers and crew and find temporary accommodation, per a Tuesday online release.

The vessel can carry up to 650 passengers, according to its U.S.-based operator Villa Vie Residences, and has been on a continuous voyage around the world for two years since it departed Belfast, Northern Ireland.

Its amenities include a fitness center and spa, a business center, three restaurants, five bars and lounges and complimentary alcoholic beverages.

The cruise drew international attention as it was marketed as a residential cruise offering a permanent home at sea while visiting hundreds of destinations.

The ship was due to arrive at Phu Quoc, a Vietnamese island, later this week, and from there travel to destinations including India, South Africa, Brazil and Australia, before docking in Gibraltar in August 2030.

"Extraordinary increases" in fuel costs meant the company would need to restructure and recapitalize its business over the next 60 days, Villa Vie Residences said in an online statement. "No outcome is assured," it added.

The company said monthly fuel costs per cabin had risen from $1,800 to as much as $8,000 in parts of Asia, which represents a 344% rise, according to CNBC's calculations.

"The company cannot simply pass increases of that scale on to residents, and the resulting shortfall has placed its current operating model under unsustainable financial pressure," the statement said.

It added that, since the Iran war began on Feb. 28, "we have taken significant steps to reduce fuel consumption, including slowing the itinerary and spending more time in port."

"Those measures have not been enough to offset the extraordinary increase in fuel costs," the company added.

- CNBC's Celia Fernandez contributed to this report.

#473. Sauerkraut at Every Meal? These Fermentation Fanatics Tried That Long Before MAHA

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Waking early, I put a small jar of my sauerkraut into my messenger bag, hop on my bicycle, ride to Seattle's King Street Station, and board a train heading to the Portland Fermentation Festival, the yearly gathering affectionately nicknamed “Stinkfest.”

Usually the sheer existence of a festival like this is enough to get people revved up to come together and talk about their favorite ferments, but in an interesting twist, fermented foods are enjoying a popularity boost from a different crowd. The MAHA movement, and particularly the Donald Trump cabinet, are big into fermented food, eating meat, usually steak, with fermented sides like kimchi or sauerkraut at every meal.

I've come to Portland, Oregon, wondering if there was going to be any overlap in the Venn diagram between festival attendees and the MAHA steak-and-ferment crowd. I mentioned this to my cousin-in-law Erick, who's been part of the Portland food scene for years, and he laughed out loud.

“It'll be more like a Venn dumbbell,” he said, making a pair of thumb-and-forefinger circles and tracing a line in the air between them. “No overlap, but food will be the bridge.”

The festival is almost exactly what the fermentation-friendly might hope to find. Right inside the main door next to the check-in desk is a bacterial petting zoo—pure festival charm—and a table where attendees can drop off their own ferments to share. Further on, lining every wall of the main room, are exhibitors who have paid a grand total of 25 bucks for a table, handing out free samples of fermented foods.

The space quickly fills up–several hundred people flow through over the course of the evening–and it appears that there's both a line in front of each exhibitor and perhaps a more organic continuous line with people flowing clockwise from one exhibitor to the next. People are notably excited and happy. There's a kid walking around with a tray of handmade pickle pins he's giving people to wear and there's a collective gasp when 20 or 30 minutes in, Dillon T. Pickle, the mascot of the Portland Pickles West Coast League baseball team, walks into the room. At one point, a big baby crawls across most of the main floor, unhindered and happy.

In the middle of it all is festival co-organizer, journalist, and cookbook author Liz Crain. She explains that the Fest, now in its 14th installment, was started in 2009 by friends who love to ferment. There were members of a little company called Picklopolis and a club called The Kraut Collective, and, with some help from author and fermentation rockstar Sandor Katz, they launched a Portland tradition.

When I ask how the festival has evolved since its first year, she gets a little philosophical.

“Keep Portland Weird. People get excited,” she says in a tone where several punctuation options would be in the spirit of the festival. “People lined up on the sidewalk all the way around the building for the first one. I poured hard cider that I made from a tree in my backyard.”

“There's nothing for sale, and that gives it a potluck-y party style. Most big festivals have consumers and purveyors, but without those distinctions here, it makes conversations a bit deeper, a bit more personal,” she says. “There's no gatekeeping, no secret recipes. It's always been wacky and fun.”

She isn't sure how much MAHA crossover I'll encounter, but she has an openness to it.

“RFK is certainly infatuated with sauerkraut and steak. There's some good to be found in the overall MAHA tenets, but so much of it has been muddied by the anti-vax stance and ludicrous conspiracy theories,” she says, though she believes it's important to “look closely and critically at pesticides and favor less-processed foods.”

“But a diet that's based solely on meat, particularly beef, animal fats, and sauerkraut … well that's not a very diverse diet,” she says. “Let's just say I wouldn't want to sit next to that person on a plane or be stuck in an elevator with them.”

I pick up on these ideas with Chris Curtin, a fermentation microbiology researcher at Oregon State University.

“Historically, everything has shown that narrowing the scope of your diet misses some things. They don't turn out to be the best in the long run,” he says. An Australian living in the US for the last decade, he can clearly see the way trends are moving and notes that registered dietitians have consistently put fermented foods at the top of their superfood lists for the past decade.

Curtin describes his work as caring about what happens when we mess around with microbes to see what happens to the raw material. As he puts it, "I'm interested in tinkering if it involves microbe wrangling." He also gets the festival's popularity among those who ferment.

“Fermenting is such a readily accessible scientific experiment that you can do in your kitchen. People go, find foods they like and want to make in their own kitchens. It's accessible and tactile.”

This plays out at the festival as I begin to sample the merchandise. The first thing I taste is an “orange whey Julius” that includes whey and OJ and is pleasingly reminiscent of the Orange Julius I'd get as a treat as a kid at the Mall of New Hampshire.

Next to that is Nat West's booth with Peruvian corn beer called chicha. West has run a cider company, dabbled in politics, driven a TriMet city bus in Portland, and now runs the Vancouver, Washington, farmers’ market. In a chicha, the fermentation starts by the brewer chewing ground field corn. At the festival, attendees are drinking what West has chewed. As people in line joke about drinking this guy's spit, or at least the byproducts of it, West interjects, “nobody's died yet!”

“The ptyalin enzymes in the saliva kickstart the starch-to-sugar process,” he explains as I take a sip and gaze longingly at the draft beer in the hands of someone who's wandered into the show from the Hotlips pizza next door.

Further along, Portland's Jorinji Miso has a double booth with samples of miso soup with bonito flakes along with koji (rice fermented with Aspergillus oryzae spores) and amazake, a koji-based drink.

The couple who run Maypop Wine Co. have their hands full at the Fest. The company’s cofounder Vince Kelsea explains that they make “anything but pinot” in the Willamette Valley—a lush north-south valley in Oregon renowned for its pinot noir. Making unfiltered, low-intervention wines like Maypop in a region famous for its pinot sounds like they're making things hard for themselves, but the attendees maintain a several-deep line at the booth all night long.

In one corner is Kiyomi Hasagewa, a third-generation Japanese bean purveyor. She’s sharing tastes of a 15-year-old miso she dehydrated and ground so she could bring it from Hokkaido to the festival. She sprinkles it over the rice and tops it with a wedge of sun-dried daikon which her elderly mother fermented in a mashed pumpkin paste. It is easily the most interesting bite in the room.

Back in the entry hall are my personal favorites, the ferments that attendees have brought to share. Liz Crain has brought along plum and dandelion wines, pickle brine, a blackberry-blueberry-lemon water kefir, and a monster jar of sour dills. I note with pride that the jar of sauerkraut I brought is next to Liz’s pickles, near-empty. Nearby is another giant jar of “fermented carrot coins, cherry bomb, garlic, ginger, horseradish,” which I anticipate as a pleasant assault on my senses, but it's really just pleasant. A garlic blast for sure, but one with all of the harsh edges smoothed by time and beneficial bacteria into something lovely.

Also at the community table are Portland occupational therapist Eva Ortega and her fermentation buddy Letti Kittel who drove down from Seattle.

“I've only made unsuccessful ferments so far,” says Ortega, undeterred. “I'm here to learn.”

Kittel says she's been nerding out on fermenting for about a year and has brought a kombucha starter with its bacteria-and-yeast scoby for others to bring home and try. And while both of them have no comment about the MAHA connection, Ortega puts some of Kittel's starter in a little jar and drops it into her purse.

I'm expecting another round of “no comment” when the MAHA diet comes up with three other fermenting friends, Megan Andricos, Josh Monifi, and Desiré Demarest, but Andricos, aka DJ Sappho, followed a similar diet 20 years ago in what they call their “fermented-pickled food and protein phase,” which consisted of “mostly kimchi and meat.”

They're off that diet now, but they liked being on it. “Kimchi evens out my gut biome. I felt better. Not superhuman, but better.”

Also on the community table is something called “Higgins Kraut,” made with Napa cabbage, carrots, garlic, and ginger, which, with a lovely melding of flavors, won my personal Best-in-Show award.

Turns out it's made by one of the show’s other founders, George Winborn, who was involved for the first 10 years. He'd been farming in Dallas when he moved to Portland, to a place that had no yard and nowhere to farm.

“Doing the Fest helped scratch the itch,” he says. He's now getting Wellspring Farm in Gresham, Oregon, off the ground and noted how the festival has become a bit more commercial, a bit less homemade. But all that's relative, considering we're standing here over our jars of sauerkraut, and the exhibitors are very small businesses, if they're businesses at all.

“I still love the DIY and starter tables,” he says. “It reminds you that everyone can do this.”

The US-based operator of the Villa Vie Odyssey said it was working with passengers on disembarkation plans and "temporary living arrangements".

The email also assured employees NOA was “a safe environment where you can input proprietary information without the risk of sharing that data with any third party.” Ostensibly, this means NOA was a closed-loop enterprise tool that did not retain or share data for model training.

Macmillan staffers say they were warned during training sessions that NOA could hallucinate and would always need to be fact-checked by humans. Still, employees say, they were encouraged to use NOA for composing emails, drafting copy, doing marketing research, and generating ideas. #493. Please Let the Internet Die

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The internet must be put out of its misery.

Any rational observer can admit that the current iteration of the web—and the social fabric woven there—is not what they signed up for. Symptoms of advanced cultural decay can no longer be ignored. This thing is eating itself.

Giants including Meta and Google have been forced to settle massive consumer lawsuits brought over privacy violations and mental health harms allegedly caused by the addictive mechanisms of their platforms. Schools are banning smartphones as multiple countries institute social media restrictions for under-16s. Parents are in a panic about their kids aspiring to careers as social media celebrities, losing themselves in a vast ecosystem of online casinos, or joining extremist movements.

Generative AI, briefly considered an amusing novelty, is now a turbocharged slop engine for the web, filling feeds and comment sections with useless, grotesque content. As humans scroll past headlines about AI taking their jobs, confusing fantasy for reality, and contributing to cognitive decline, any consumer appeal the technology might have is turning to anguish. The data centers that power it have become increasingly unpopular, and politically radioactive.

To be online, many now admit, is to be endlessly manipulated and exploited. In December, Oxford University Press declared rage bait to be the word of the year, noting that use of the term had tripled over the previous 12 months. The lexical spike represented a heightened awareness that monetized social accounts churn out bad-faith takes and extremist content, automating this abusive behavior with generative AI, simply to profit off hostile engagement. Billionaire oligarchs have opened the floodgates to such inauthentic activity, conspiracy-mongering, and hate speech by rolling back moderation.

In short, when you hang around online in 2026, you see little worth saving. Perhaps a decade ago, you felt that the glimmers of community and joy, of artistic and intellectual exchange, were enough to balance out the toxic junk. No longer.

It’s not necessarily that people have gotten worse. “Dead Internet Theory”—the proposition that a vast majority of the interactions on the web are fake, bots talking to other bots—has accrued more supporting evidence recently. One cybersecurity firm concluded this spring that agents already outnumber human users, their traffic surging by 8,000 percent through 2025. No wonder seemingly everyone has at some point caught themselves scrolling through a wasteland of brainrot and asked, “What the hell am I even looking at?” Underlying that question is another: “Is it always going to be like this?”

With this anxiety hammering at my prefrontal cortex day in and out, I was more than intrigued by the absolutist title of technologist and Pulitzer-winning journalist John West’s new book, The Internet Will Die, and So Will You: On Learning to Live in Our Digital Age. In his discerning treatise, West confirms what many have suspected for a good while now: The platforms once seen as enduring archives of knowledge and communication are being cannibalized by ideological warfare and basic greed.

But that’s not the whole story. For one, West argues, people misperceive the “vastness” of the internet, which really amounts to “a vanishing fraction, a rounding error, of the human experience.” Moreover, it is remarkably fragile—West opens with the story of how a 2001 train derailment in a Baltimore tunnel took out a major bundle of fiber-optic cables, leading to internet slowdowns nationwide—and continually undergoes cycles of death and rebirth as a result of this natural instability.

If the internet is much smaller and less solid than imagined, West’s book suggests, then it’s crucial to recognize the forces that determine how those deaths unfold, or which pieces are preserved while others crumble. Because the market has falsely sold us the web as a comprehensive library of accumulated wisdom, each transient phase of this global network comes to define the present, for better and worse. Today we witness a dismal paralysis brought on by algorithmic incentives to churn out inflammatory garbage: Full-time influencers are in a losing arms race for attention with low-effort, high-volume channels that streamline production by delegating the creative process to bleeding-edge software.

West draws a sharp contrast between this holographic environment of monetization and a passionate, personalized internet that spanned from the primordial GeoCities to Tumblr, describing how these innovative communities have been gradually erased. While some endure in half-dead and highly corrupted form, others are lost. In middle school, West maintained a web page devoted to his love of anime; later, he kept a blog documenting his college years. Looking back as an adult, he’s vexed by the fates that have befallen each.

“I really wanted to delete my anime site,” West tells WIRED. “I don't have access to the AOL account anymore, and I couldn't do it.” Instead, it lives on as a zombie page, cluttered with spam ads and dead-end links. As for the college blog, he had neglected to pay domain fees toward the end of his time writing it, and so the final year of posts disappeared. “I kind of got obsessed with this idea that the things we want to last really don't, and then oftentimes the things that we kind of wish would go away hang on for way too long,” West says.

These shifting sands are a far cry from the flawless, collective, permanent memory that internet users originally believed they were building. “‘Forever’ is such a strong word, and it's the wrong word,” West says. Online spaces are memento mori, the same as individuals who self-promote across them (and that includes certain longevity-obsessed biohackers). Sure, there was a time when a celebrity could be “canceled” for tasteless posts unearthed years later, but in 2026, it’s not so easy to surface specific messages when you go hunting for them in your AI-powered Gmail account. Zeitgeist-defining platforms like Vine can be vaporized in an instant, a fact reinforced in January 2025, when Americans posted farewell TikTok videos ahead of an expected US ban on the app.

This random ephemerality, West points out, has eroded any confidence in claims previously backed up by hypertext cross-references, and thus any kind of consensus truth. An analysis of hyperlinks in articles on The New York Times website between 1996 and mid-2019 found that 25 percent of all links were inaccessible. According to a 2016 study, “three out of four citations in scholarly articles have drifted—the websites they purport to cite no longer say what they once did,” he writes. “Over half of Wikipedia’s entries contain at least one link in its references section that goes nowhere, a Pew report found.”

The impulse to drive engagement spreads to users themselves: West noticed, for instance, that when he recently reread a favorite book, Marilynne Robinson’s Gilead, he had the nagging thought that while he loved returning to the novel for the fourth or fifth time, he wouldn’t be able to count it toward his annual Goodreads stats. West says this fixation on the internet’s hollow validations creates “the exact conditions for a space to be filled by something that isn't human.”

Wouldn’t it be liberating, then, to actually deny our attention to astroturfed outrage, engineered virality, and influencer sponcon? To let the mainstream digital economies propped up by AI-enabled surveillance finally fall to ruin? If what the titans of the industry have to offer us these days are “pervert glasses” and the suppression of ads for a probably unflattering documentary, why are we helping them stay in business? Leaving their platforms for dead might be the last lever of power available to the masses.

Doing so could free people to explore dormant corners. West notes that some of the most interesting parts of the internet are the ones that nobody sees, like web portals to streams of YouTube videos that have almost no views. “Some of it’s fucking weird and creepy, but a lot of it’s just people singing a song and having a blast,” he says. West also cites the bustling, zero-revenue, circulation of poetry in web journals as proof that “you can find meaning and make meaning online all the time,” provided there is no financial motivation.

When you eliminate what the platforms want you to fixate on, it’s clear what you’ve been missing, and how you were steered away from it. Your opinions are shaped in a bubble by avatars you’ve never met, weaponized against equally hermetic rival camps. The internet doesn’t really have winners, unless you count the few without the sense to stop chasing relevance at any cost. That’s why the main characters are so malleable in their beliefs; never afraid to pivot, betray, and discard what they once held to be the core of their identity. And that’s when they stood for anything to begin with.

Absent a sudden, lasting outage due to a fresh and severe catastrophe, the internet as it exists won’t perish anytime soon. There are, however, splintering cracks in its facade, which may create room for emergent and strangely evolved life-forms. There’s no reason to assume that the present tech empire will rule the century to come; the organic and anarchic web stands to mount a triumphant renaissance if (or when) it falls.

“Look, the internet is rotting,” West says. “The question is, where are we going to put our energy?” A metaphor he often turns to in his book is compost. “Compost is just rot harnessed for a purpose,” he says. Can a decaying internet be fertilizer for something better? It’s a rosy notion, but speaking as a perennially failed composter—it’s just too gross, and I’m too lazy—I have my doubts about whether enough hardy, patient gardeners can be mobilized to make our digital landscape bloom once more.

Then again, as West’s book sought to remind me, I am destined to die as well. Maybe I’ll be mixed in with the soil.

In mid-October, CEO Elon Musk sort of made good on a long-standing promise: Tesla’s new second-gen Roadster flew through the air. Technically, the vehicle used compressed air to “thrust” itself—the “flying” lasted mere seconds, and the system takes six to seven hours to refill.

Never mind, though—some buyers were eager to unsheath their credit cards to reserve one of the cars. “I was freaking out,” says Alex Lawrence, the owner of used-electric-vehicle dealership EVAuto, who already had one reservation for a second-gen Roadster and watched the demonstration live online. “I placed another one.”

It’s not a decision most would make lightly. Tesla took years to get the sports car even close to production. The automaker first introduced the vehicle, a successor to its first car ever, as a surprise at a 2017 event, and immediately began taking $50,000 reservations. At the time, the plan was to deliver the cars in 2020; Musk and company now say the vehicle will enter production in 2028, with delivery dates unclear. (If reservation holders had put that money into Tesla stock, they would be up nearly $1 million today.)

The Roadster is also expensive to reserve—and will likely get pricier to buy. The vehicle’s base model, which doesn’t include acceleration thrusters or the flight system, still costs $50,000 to reserve. The second-tier Thrust variant, which comes with the thrusters, requires a $250,000 deposit. The actual flying car, called Rocket, is listed as “invite-only,” and even Musk has acknowledged the vehicle won’t be street legal. Meanwhile, the automaker’s reservation terms note that purchase prices are “only an estimate”—and those estimates don’t even exist yet.

Whatever! “I’m going to do everything in my power to get the flying one,” Lawrence says. When he sees the Roadster, after all, he sees money signs. His EV dealership has a large and growing social media following, and the business, not the man, technically put down the money for the Roadster reservations. “We’re going to put it on the track and use it for marketing content,” he says.

Utah-based tech founder Travis Cook upped his nearly 10-year-old reservation for the base Roadster variant to the Thrust because he actually wants to live with the thing. He calls himself a “car aficionado” and has a dealer’s license so he can buy and sell fun cars. He loves little more than driving an unusual vehicle, he told WIRED from behind the wheel of a Cybertruck as he used the company’s Full Self-Driving (Supervised) feature.

“When I roll up somewhere, all the other car aficionados come out, and they're so excited,” he says. “I just get a buzz from that.”

Cook says he has no regrets about his years-old reservation, though he may get frustrated if the price shoots up closer to half a million. “I’m not sure I’m willing to drop too much money on the car—but I probably would,” he says. Close family tolerates his obsession, though his wife doesn’t enjoy going 0 to 60 in his other cars and probably won’t in the Roadster, either. “She’s not going to want to go in it,” he says.

The tech founder has faith in Musk and Tesla. The October demonstration was even crazier than he had expected. “When it launched off the top of the ramp and went and landed, my jaw was on the floor,” Cook says.

Despite the Roadster’s memeable debut, the vehicle doesn’t seem to be a top priority for Tesla. Musk has said for years now that the automaker isn’t really in the business of making cars anymore and instead is focused on robotics, artificial intelligence, and autonomy. He has touted the new Roadster as the company’s last human-driven car ever. The supercar garnered just two mentions in Tesla’s hourlong earnings call with investors this month, with Musk calling it “arguably the coolest demo of any product ever.” Musk, meanwhile, has other preoccupations—he has donated at least $445 million to Republican candidates ahead of the US midterms.

But Lawrence believes Musk will pull off what he says he will and that his own business’s investment in the Roadster will be worth it in the end. The looping, wedge-shaped car demo left him a little nostalgic, a little existential.

“We don’t do crazy stuff like this anymore, like going to the moon. There’s not a lot today that will stop you in your tracks,” he says. “I felt like I was 15, watching that.”

#504. ICE Is Running One of the Largest Anti-Left Surveillance Operations in US History

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Attorneys for 15 Minnesota residents facing federal conspiracy charges have obtained evidence of a sweeping, possibly unconstitutional spying operation carried out by Immigration and Customs Enforcement against left-wing social movements in Minnesota and beyond.

The high-profile Minnesota 15 prosecution makes clear that the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), flush with federal funds and newly hired personnel, is now President Donald Trump’s primary tool against dissent, with a powerful array of post-9/11 investigative authorities brought to bear against everyday Americans organizing their communities.

Nothing of this scale has been seen in the United States, experts say, since the federal government’s counterinsurgency against the New Left and the anti-war movement in the 1960s and 1970s.

Among the records revealed during the discovery process in these cases are dozens of reports, audio transcripts, emails, intercepted Signal chats, photographs, database queries, and more. They reveal the inner workings of the domestic terrorism investigations, running under the names Project Whipple Shield and Operation Puppet Master, authorized by the controversial National Security Presidential Memorandum 7.

Published following Charlie Kirk’s assassination in September of last year, NSPM 7 instructs federal law enforcement to use every tool available against organizations and people who criticize the administration's policies and espouse "anti-Christian" ideas. The full scope and extent of the investigations it authorizes remains unknown.

Defense attorneys for the Minnesota 15 claim that the prosecution stems from a broader nationwide surveillance operation aimed at opponents of the Trump administration’s immigration policies. One attorney in the case directly compares the DHS-run campaign to the FBI’s infamous Counterintelligence Program, or COINTELPRO, which unconstitutionally surveilled, infiltrated, and blackmailed racial justice, civil rights, and anti-war movements from the 1950s through the early 1970s.

Three former law enforcement officials who reviewed filings concur, telling WIRED that the case is almost certainly part of a wider intelligence-gathering operation that began long before the administration invaded Minnesota in December 2025.

Dueling motions in the case over discovery of DHS’s surveillance program reveal that personnel with Homeland Security Investigations—one of ICE’s two components—repurposed funding mechanisms and investigative tools for serious criminal probes and turned them on domestic dissenters. Operation Keyhole, the HSI undercover program that bankrolled the Minnesota 15 prosecution, dates back to 2008. As of June 2026, it was underwriting a half dozen active undercover investigations in the Minneapolis–Saint Paul area.

Around 120 HSI agents, many drawn from specialized units across the country that have served as the leading edge of the administration’s militarized immigration enforcement operations, were assigned to the case—including 11 undercovers. They used highly invasive surveillance techniques, including the deployment of Reaper drones and Patriot Act tools usually restricted to money laundering and terrorism investigations. Undercover operatives also infiltrated churches in apparent violation of a court order.

The Minnesota 15 indictment stems from the Trump administration’s demand that the Justice Department “go big and go loud” to crack down on community response networks during last winter’s Operation Metro Surge. Twin Cities activists organized volunteers to monitor DHS agents and their vehicles using encrypted Signal groups, homebuilt vehicle databases, and a centralized dispatch system. Government prosecutors claim that this amounts to a criminal conspiracy to block immigration enforcement, unlawfully identify and stalk federal agents, and even assault them.

Their defense attorneys say the heavy charges filed against the Minnesota 15 don’t align with their alleged conduct. “This is the first time I’ve had a client who’s accused of a conspiracy and went on a speaking tour,” says Kevin Riach, who represents defendant Isaac Sant.

An internal ICE document laying out the scope of Operation Puppet Master—which led to the indictment of the Minneapolis 15—ends by noting, “This case has also been accepted under CUC Op Keyhole.” According to prosecutors, Operation Keyhole is a long-standing program created by HSI’s Saint Paul office to fund “undercover activity”—primarily equipment purchases and personnel costs—in a region covering Minnesota, North and South Dakota, Iowa, and Nebraska. The program has funded at least 77 investigations into “narcotics trafficking, child sexual abuse, financial investigations, and counter-proliferation” since it started in December 2008.

The CUC prefix, per an internal 2008 DHS handbook unearthed by the independent media outlet Unicorn Riot, refers to Certified Undercover Operations, a formal designation with clearly defined oversight mechanisms, procedures, and reporting requirements. Operation Keyhole’s parameters are reviewed every six months by a committee comprising senior DOJ and DHS officials. As of June 2026, when it was last renewed, Keyhole funded six active investigations, including Project Whipple Shield, which was initiated in February to probe claims of assault, stalking, and doxing of DHS agents and other federal employees, and underpins the Minnesota 15 indictment.

At least 11 undercover agents working under Operation Keyhole infiltrated Twin Cities anti-ICE activists, participating in martial arts and know-your-rights trainings, marches, general strikes, and community meetings. They also infiltrated activists’ communications network for identifying possible undercover ICE vehicles and dispatching activists to tail them through the Twin Cities, and penetrated several activist group chats on Signal, transmitting their contents back to other HSI colleagues for analysis and eventual inclusion in the indictment.

Activist conferences as far away as Seattle, Michigan, Chicago, and New York City were placed under surveillance as part of the investigation, and HSI teams photographed and ran the license plates of every car parked in the lot of a meeting location or seen transporting activists. There is also drone footage from unarmed Customs and Border Protection Reaper drones of the activists moving around the Twin Cities area.

The undercovers were part of a much broader group of more than 50 HSI agents, including at least two dozen from HSI’s little-known Special Operations Group, that took part in the investigation and surveillance of the defendants, both digital and in-person. Feds with decades of experience on complex financial crimes, drug cartel investigations, human trafficking, and organized crime were pulled into the Minnesota 15 investigation.

Several of the Minnesota 15 case agents are from out of state and appear in court records for the prosecutions of anti-ICE activists in other cities. This includes Los Angeles–based HSI agents Robert Kurtz III, who specializes in financial fraud, and Ryan Ribner, who allegedly assaulted SEIU California president David Huerta at a June 2025 protest of an ICE enforcement operation, arrested him, and then claimed the then 58-year-old Huerta attacked federal law enforcement. (Huerta’s charges were dropped in July after videos contradicting Ribner’s account surfaced.) HSI records show Ribner was also involved in at least two prosecutions of Southern California residents who physically confronted ICE and Border Patrol agents last year.

Other agents from the conspiracy investigation have also been involved in recent high-profile incidents. Tim Gerber, the “team leader” of an HSI Special Operations Group squad, led the investigation of former CNN host Don Lemon for reporting on a protest at a Minneapolis Church where an ICE agent was a pastor. Fellow SOG agent Yue Her was present at the scene of a 2025 incident when Renee Good’s killer, ICE Special Response Team agent Johnathan Ross, was dragged by the car of a man he tried to apprehend.

“This operation does not seem in any way limited to Minnesota,” says Spencer Reynolds, a former DHS attorney and counterterrorism lawyer who now works at the NAACP Legal Defense Fund. “As we saw with the boots on the ground in the streets, with Greg Bovino leading CBP and Border Patrol special forces units on sweeping raids across the country, this is the intelligence and undercover investigative apparatus that is running in parallel to the door-kicking.”

Mike German, a retired FBI agent who worked undercover domestic terrorism cases for years before becoming a sharp critic of post-9/11 federal law enforcement, likens the Minnesota 15 case to federal law enforcement’s long history of suppressing left-wing social movements, including the CIA’s Operation MH/Chaos, which surveilled the anti-war movement during the Vietnam War for evidence of foreign influence. One difference is the sheer scale—this time, it’s bigger.

According to a 2011 book by former CIA officer Frank Rafalko, the CIA secret investigation into suspected foreign influence on the New Left and anti-Vietnam War movement had 42 officers working the case at its peak. Court records show 119 HSI agents worked on the Minnesota 15 case alone.

“Broad monitoring of social media, communications, and financial transactions are used to link an ever increasing number of people as potential subjects of investigation and prosecution,” German says. “These are exactly the kind of intelligence activities J. Edgar Hoover adopted to suppress the Civil Rights and anti-war movements in the 1960s.”

The key difference today, German says, is the vast pool of data available to the feds.

HSI used highly invasive measures—including scrutiny of wire transfers, PayPal reimbursements, and other financial transactions; forensic digital analysis of website traffic; 15 terabytes worth of infiltrated Signal chats; and information from a commercial data broker—to identify individuals under investigation.

After a local activist uploaded a license-plate search tool on Fly.io, a developer-focused public cloud platform, to an infiltrated Signal chat, HSI agents reviewed website DNS queries and Stripe data along with data purchased from a commercial broker. This gave HSI access to SSH session logs, outbound connection records, the Fly.io account’s signup IP, and a Stripe card fingerprint on an account that had never completed a payment. Agents fused that with Minnesota DVS records and commercial data from the Thomson-Reuters CLEAR platform to produce a driver's license number, Social Security number summary, years of address history, phone numbers, property records, and jointly registered vehicles.

Documents concerning financial records requested through the Treasury Department’s 314(a) suspicious activity reporting program—created by the Patriot Act to enable speedy tracking of finances for terrorism and money-laundering investigations—show that the feds targeted records of 29 “subjects” during queries conducted on March 31, 2026. Emails viewed by WIRED between several HSI agents based in Los Angeles and Arizona show that batches of records were turned over pursuant to those inquiries, including some dealing with SEIU that a senior Minnesota HSI agent sent to a LA-based colleague to be filed “wherever these records are stored.”

HSI investigators used similar workarounds to warrants and grand jury subpoenas to obtain phone records for several journalists who covered Operation Metro Surge, as first reported by The Guardian. Court records show the feds used an administrative summons request reserved for violations of customs and taxation requirements, which does not fit the activity that made up the criminal charges filed by the Minnesota US attorney against former CNN anchor Don Lemon and others.

The Minnesota 15 case is the clearest example yet of DHS pursuing the Trump administration’s efforts to crack down on what have long been upheld as First Amendment–protected activities. But it’s unlikely alone.

Riach, the defense attorney, says the HSI reports show the agency is complicit in what he called “the new COINTELPRO.”

“What we saw during the immigration surges last year was that ICE is the administration’s paramilitary force, the projector of strength,” Riach says. “Here, we see that HSI are taking the FBI’s place as the investigative secret police—it’s the classic one-two punch of authoritarian regimes around the world.”

#202. 10-year Treasury yield creeps higher even as Fed keeps rates the same

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Bond yields moved higher on Wednesday as investors digested the Federal Reserve's decision to hold interest rates steady at its October meeting.

The 10-year Treasury note yield — the main benchmark for mortgages, auto loans and credit card debt — was more than 3 basis points higher at 5.026%.

The yield on the 2-year Treasury note, which more closely follows short-term Federal Reserve rate decisions, slipped more than 3 basis points to 4.704%.

The yield on longer-dated 30-year U.S. Treasury — which tend to move on geopolitical events — was also up by more than 4 basis points to 5.354%.

One basis point equals 0.01%, or 1/100th of 1%, and yields and prices move inversely to one another.

Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh said the bank remained focused on "price stability" despite the pause and that the employment picture has been "quite positive."

Despite the hold for October, expectations for a 25 basis points hike at the central bank's next meeting on Dec. 9 currently stand at 85.7%, according to CME's FedWatch tool.

More economic data is due Thursday. Personal consumption expenditures index — the Fed's preferred inflation gauge — as well as the quarterly GDP growth rate and personal income and spending data will offer investors additional insights into the domestic economy and inflation picture.

That data will be critical to determining where the Fed may go from here, according to TruStage chief economist Steve Rick.

"Holding rates steady doesn't necessarily mean the Fed is finished raising them, especially if inflation doesn't start to cool," he said. "Policymakers will want to see whether these price pressures are temporary or becoming more widespread throughout the economy."

#510. AI Emergency Alerts Are Coming. Humans Might Not Be Ready

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This story was produced by Grist and copublished with WIRED. It is part of Distorted, a Grist series exploring the spread of misinformation in the wake of extreme weather, and is published with support from the CO2 Foundation.

Earlier this month, after Hurricane Isaias made landfall on the Gulf Coast as a Category 3 hurricane, the emergency management expert Laura Myers got a call from a meteorologist in Alabama. Myers is director of the University of Alabama’s Center for Advanced Public Safety, and she talks to state and local emergency managers around her region all the time. The meteorologist told Myers that a local emergency manager had published a graphic showing where Isaias might threaten the Alabama coast with a “sting jet” of high winds.

The problem? The emergency manager had generated the graphic using an artificial intelligence chatbot, and it wasn’t accurate.

“That county emergency manager was trying to get his stakeholders to focus on the risk in that county, and so he had asked AI to help him,” she said. “It did what he wanted it to do, but it was not meteorologically accurate.”

Most emergency management agencies don’t permit employees to use generative AI, but Myers and most other emergency management experts agree that its usage is likely already pervasive at state and local disaster departments. There are hundreds of companies that are marketing AI tools to emergency managers for tasks like flood damage assessment and resource coordination, but public communication is one of the areas where government employees and startups have the most interest.

Emergency department personnel with the city of New York monitor the watch-command room in Brooklyn on February 23, 2026, ahead of a winter storm.

The reason? Most emergency managers aren’t very good at sending out alerts and quickly communicating about disasters with the public. That’s not because they’re emergency managers, but because they are human beings. They tend to write in jargon that non-experts might not understand, leave out information on the assumption that the public already knows it, and include irrelevant details that could distract people from what they actually do need to know.

“A lot of agencies are really limited in resources and the number of people that they have,” said Jeannette Sutton, a disaster researcher at the University of Albany who has created a comprehensive dashboard that provides templates for disaster alerts. “I think they recognize it as a priority, but they don't prioritize it until they realize they should have. They're just trying to get their job done without screwing something up.”

Emergency managers are the government officials who are in charge of disaster response and recovery. At the state and local level, It is these officials and their agencies, not the Federal Emergency Management Agency, that make up the first line of defense after an extreme weather event. As such, emergency managers often like to say that “trust is our currency,” and it’s in their communication with the public that they build and lose this trust.

As climate disasters become more frequent and widespread, there have been numerous examples of flawed human communication scrambling an emergency response. In 2016, for instance, adjacent counties in California issued a “voluntary evacuation” and a “mandatory evacuation” for the same wildfire. In Australia in 2019, officials warned about wildfires by using the phrase “Watch and Act,” which the government later said caused “significant confusion” because no one knew what it meant.

In some cases, inadequate communication can mean the difference between life and death. This happened in Montecito, California, after the 2017 Thomas Fire. A few weeks after the fire, a heavy rainstorm produced a deadly mudslide. The government of Santa Barbara County struggled to communicate the risk to residents still traumatized from the blaze and experiencing a phenomenon that Sutton calls “warning fatigue.” The county sent alerts to everyone in the “voluntary evacuation zone” for wildfires, telling residents to “stay alert to changing conditions and be prepared to leave immediately.” Most of those residents should have left immediately, but to many the evacuation notice was optional; others just didn’t want to evacuate for a second time in a month. The mudslide killed 23 people and injured more than 160 others.

Governments that put out bad warnings are apt to lose the public’s trust, said Sutton. “They're losing the opportunity to be that voice to help people to be safe.”

In theory, alerts are a perfect use case for artificial intelligence, and in particular for the generative AI systems that have developed at a dramatic pace over the past few years. FEMA provides training sessions for emergency managers to learn best practices for synthesizing and sending alerts, but many officials don’t remember all their training when an emergency arrives. A large language model is different. It can read hundreds of thousands of alerts in mere minutes, digest all the research on what works in similar scenarios, and then apply that to edit a human alert in seconds.

“If you’ve only sent one [alert] in your life, and that was 15 years ago, and you need to remember all your training, and you’re thinking ‘Hey, there's a chemical plume coming my way, the phone's ringing, the radio's blaring, I need help,’ the AI is probably gonna do a better job than you are,” said Rob Dale, a former meteorologist who now runs the emergency management department in Ingham County, Michigan.

Dale has already used AI to draft a few practice alerts behind the scenes, but he’s never had to send one out. Ingham County is a small county in central Michigan, and it’s immune from major weather events like hurricanes and fires. If such an event did happen, Dale said he would have no issue allowing a chatbot to draft his incident communication, but he’d still want control over sending the alert out.

Some experts believe the technology is already good enough to deploy. EM1, a startup that produces AI software for emergency managers, has used Sutton’s research to train a model that its executives say is already better than almost any human emergency manager. The company has tested this in-house model on a number of scenarios including wildfires and tsunamis. It can already match or exceed human capabilities on most benchmark tests.

Sutton gave Grist an example of how the software works: Grist provided her with a recent alert sent by a New Mexico county that was trying to warn residents about flooding on the San Juan River.

After a series of erroneous evacuation alerts to L.A. residents sparked panic during an active wildfire season in January 2025, the director of the county’s emergency management department apologized.

The original alert read, “Breeched banks. The water is rising. EVACUATE NOW. McGee Park open for people and pets, RV.” Sutton asked the EM1 model to evaluate and revise the alert. In a few seconds, it produced what she said is a far better alert, one that checks all the main boxes of disaster communication.

The new alert read: “San Juan County: Floodwater is rising near [location]. People in [evacuation area]: LEAVE NOW for higher ground. Do not walk or drive through floodwater. McGee Park is open for people and pets. Check [official update source] for updates.” The model identified and fixed several problems with the original text, which does not say who is sending the alert, name the specific location where flooding is happening, or clarify who needs to evacuate and give advice on how to do so safely. It misspells the word “breached,” and it doesn’t provide any guidance on where residents can seek further information.

EM1 has contracts with around a hundred government agencies and school districts, but none are using it to automate their extreme weather alerts just yet. For the moment, they’re only using it to workshop plans and internal preparations. Even so, all the experts interviewed for this story said they are certain that many emergency managers are already using chatbots to write messages, even if they won’t admit it. Sutton said that the emergency managers who take her alert training sessions often present her with high-quality alerts they have drafted in seconds using ChatGPT or Gemini.

“Official adoption is pretty low, but people are unofficially using it, absolutely,” said Sarah Miller, a professor of emergency management at Pierce College in Florida and a longtime instructor at the National Disaster Preparedness Training Center. “They're using their own personal accounts, or they're using free ones.”

It sounds like an easy trade-off: if AI is better and faster than humans, and the difference between a good alert and a bad alert can be life and death, we should be using AI for every heat wave and wildfire. Yet even researchers who are bullish on chatbot technology say that making the switch could backfire. Members of the public who are skeptical of AI or who don’t trust the government in the first place may spurn agencies that publish AI-generated graphics or social media posts. In Syracuse, New York, for instance, a union of public employees sued the local government for using an AI service to answer non-emergency 911 calls.

“We trust things that we perceive [are] authentic and care about us,” said Ginny Katz, the founder of HazAdapt, a consultancy that helps disaster officials figure out how to use technology. “AI-generated content does not feel authentic.” There’s also a risk that chatbots could allow emergency managers to send out more alerts, and faster, which could increase the risk of warning fatigue.

As is the case in other areas where AI is being deployed, disaster experts say that for the moment the software is only as capable as the person using it. A seasoned emergency manager might be able to speed up their communication with the public by having a chatbot draft a series of alerts, but an inexperienced one rushing to create an urgent alert won’t know how to evaluate the output of a model like ChatGPT or Claude.

Even though the technology is a ways off from being able to perform without a human expert in the loop, that hasn’t stopped many understaffed emergency management departments from using it. As for how the proliferation of AI-generated content is affecting public trust in government, experts say it’s too early to tell.

“If you had a human sending you a text message versus a computer sending you a text message, which one would you believe is being more accurate?” said Justin Snair, the founder of Preppr, another company that offers AI software to emergency managers.

The answer, he said, depends on the person receiving it.
