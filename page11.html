Asked about AI and the trend of college students booing tech CEOs at graduation ceremonies, Instagram head Adam Mosseri acknowledged in an interview that a lot of people really don’t like AI, and it’s not just young people and creatives. Appearing on the Colin and Samir podcast to talk about the launch of widescreen and long-form video for Instagram, Mosseri said he thinks there’s a trend of “more anxiety and more frustration and more anger about AI,” particularly in creative industries:

It’s not only getting worse, it’s going to continue to get worse. I think we’re going to see more frustration and anger, some of it founded, some of it unfounded, over the next couple of years, and that’s going to make it hard for a lot of people, not just in the industry.

Mosseri claimed Instagram doesn’t rank AI creators differently, but raised the question of ranking quality, saying, “There’s a lot of great AI content out there, there’s also a lot of AI slop. Do we rank AI content as well as we rank non-AI content? Probably not.” Talking about AI slop again after previously bringing it up in a New Year’s slideshow dump, he said he thinks the term AI “slop” is used “too liberally.”

Instagram began cracking down on AI-generated profiles in August, after launching a label for AI accounts earlier this year, but misleading AI content like celebrity deepfakes remains an issue. Earlier this month, Mosseri said Instagram is “actively working out what to do” about AI fakes, but he noted, “I don’t think it’s practical for us to try and ban AI content from the platform.”

Mosseri said Instagram is “now starting to require AI accounts to label themselves, because we think it’s important that people know if what they’re looking at is real or not real.” He also acknowledged there are fears around AI, including from creators:

I think people are concerned about a lot of things. I think they’re concerned about owning their content and models getting trained on their content. I think they’re concerned— there’s people who are concerned about safety. They’re concerned about job displacement, having AI displace whatever it is they do, if you’re a content creator or an engineer or a mathematician. I think there’s concerns about— I mean, fundamentally what I think it comes down to when you really have, like, a heart-to-heart with someone is a fear of being left behind in some way.

#484. The Milwaukee Brewers win the first World Series title in franchise history

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Our live coverage of Game 5 of the World Series has ended. For a full recap of the Brewers’ World Series-clinching victory, read below.

The Milwaukee Brewers put on a masterclass in every facet of the game as they beat the Tampa Bay Rays 4-1 to win their first World Series title in franchise history in five games on Wednesday at Tropicana Field in St. Petersburg, Florida.

In a game that saw both teams’ aces go head-to-head, it was the Brewers’ Cy Young candidate Jacob Misiorowski besting Rays ace Drew Rasmussen.

The NL Cy Young frontrunner went five-and-two-thirds innings, striking out two while only allowing two hits before exiting the game with an apparent cramp in his leg. Rasmussen was pulled after four innings with him being on short rest and a reported pitch count of 60.

The real story was the Brewers offense – especially rookie shortstop Cooper Pratt.

Pratt got the scoring started in the second inning with an RBI single, scoring Brice Turang. And Milwaukee never looked back.

That wasn’t the only notable thing Pratt did – he made a little World Series history with his single. It was his sixth straight at-bat with a hit, the most for a rookie in Fall Classic history.

But it was the sixth inning when things went up a notch. William Contreras’ RBI double scored a run and then he would score on Pratt’s sacrifice fly to build the lead to 4-0.

The Rays bullpen was able to settle in, using seven total pitchers, but the Tampa offense was sound asleep until Liam Hicks and Chandler Simpson both doubled in the ninth for the Rays, making the score 4-1.

Contreras was named the World Series MVP after batting .381 with four RBI.

The Brewers will now bring a World Series title back to Wisconsin.

For the first time in franchise history, the Milwaukee Brewers are World Series champions.

The Brew Crew dominated the Tampa Bay Rays in Game 5, winning 4-1 in St. Petersburg, Florida.

It’s the last party of the season in a long line of them.

Every major US sport has multi-round postseason schedules, but none of them party like MLB teams. Locker room celebrations in the NFL tend to be subdued until the Super Bowl, and since hockey players are famously superstitious, popping bottles early is practically verboten in the NHL; the Stanley Cup is all that really matters.

Boston Red Sox clubhouse manager Tom McLaughlin has spent over 40 years with the Red Sox, and he says he’s lost track of how many celebrations he’s been involved in, but he counts them in the dozens. He told CNN Sports it was “pretty tame” when he started in the ‘80s, when the postseason was much shorter, but the back end of the season now can feel like one enormous bender for the most successful teams.

Florida’s Natural, the juice giant from the Sunshine State, is about to pay up.

Florida’s Natural and Johnsonville, the famous sausage company from Wisconsin, made a bet before the World Series. If the Rays won, Johnsonville would be sending 400,000 bratwursts to Tampa Bay for victory grilling.

But the Rays came up short. So instead, Florida’s Natural will be sending north enough orange juice to make 200,000 mimosas, according to a news release from Johnsonville.

Milwaukee has waited 57 years for a World Series title. Cooper Pratt had to wait about four months.

Pratt, the Brewers shortstop who caught fire in this World Series at the plate, was called up to the Brewers in June. He found himself starting at shortstop in the Fall Classic just a few short months later and is now a champion.

“This is one of the greatest moments of my life. Oh my gosh,” he said, overwhelmed by the moment. “It doesn’t even feel real. Like, I don’t know. This is this is surreal. Only took four months!”

#367. The 2,000-Nit OLED in Apple’s New MacBook Is a Big Deal

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Laptops with OLED screens aren’t particularly special. They’ve been around for a decade. You can currently buy a touchscreen OLED machine for as little as $700, and even the iPad Pro has had an OLED screen since 2024.

So, why did Apple wait over a decade to release its own touchscreen OLED MacBook Pro? The simple answer for the long wait is that the company wanted to do it “the Apple way.” But in this case, “the Apple way” involves somehow cranking up the display to 2,000 nits, solving both the brightness and glare problems that OLED laptops have always suffered from.

Having seen the thing with my own eyes at a media event today in Los Angeles, I can tell you that this screen is the real deal.

Apple is using a tandem OLED panel, the same layer-stacking technology used in the OLED iPad Pro. The real innovation here, however, is that the panel has been optimized to hit a new peak brightness of 2,000 nits, and that’s a result that matters.

That big number is meaningless on its own, but here’s the context. Nits are the standard measure of screen brightness used across the consumer tech industry. A cheap OLED laptop maxes out around 300 nits. A higher-end OLED laptop might get you up to 1,000 nits of brightness—but only in brighter and more colorful High Dynamic Range content like specific streaming apps, YouTube videos, and games. In other words, the vast majority of the time you’re using an OLED, it’ll feel like a dimmer screen than normal. The iPad Pro can get up to 1,600 nits for HDR content, and 1,000 nits for the less bright, less colorful Standard Dynamic Range content. That’s closer.

But this MacBook Pro’s screen is on an entirely different level. It’s a ground-up redesign of the tandem OLED layer arrangement in the iPad Pro, even down to the manufacturing process. And the result is that it can blast your eyes with 2,000 nits of brightness in both SDR and HDR. That means whether you’re looking at some generic wallpaper, a website, or an HDR-compatible movie, you’ll feel that extra brightness. That’s why, I assume, Apple gave it the name PROLED XDR. In a world where the words “OLED” and “HDR” don’t necessarily indicate how good the experience is, more weighty jargon is needed.

As with all tech, bigger numbers don’t always add up to a noticeable difference for the user. But when it comes to HDR performance, we’ve been waiting a long time for OLED to catch up.

HDR, or High Dynamic Range, serves as a measure of color depth, brightness, and contrast—and in the past, this has always involved a trade-off. An OLED screen can turn each individual pixel any color, and it can turn off individual pixels in the display. This allows OLED to have great color control and unbeatable contrast, since when you turn a pixel off, it renders absolutely black.

But OLEDs couldn’t get super bright, and other screen tech like mini-LED could get much brighter, which is why Apple stuck with mini-LED for years, using it on the MacBook Pro and Studio Display XDR. This technology allowed Apple to have the laptops with the best HDR performance out there.

With the 2026 MacBook Pro, that compromise no longer exists. This OLED screen has both the brightness and contrast in its pocket, and you can see it.

An OLED panel has lots of more benefits over conventional backlighting beyond just contrast. Mini-LED struggles with an effect called blooming, where bright parts of images bleed light to the surrounding area, which is where lots of detail is lost in the image. OLED completely fixes that, giving each pixel a much wider range of control (up to completely turning off). As part of the Apple event I attended, we were shown a selection of HDR images with lots of contrast, a number of which had fields of darkness at the edges that made it look like the image simply disappeared into the bezel. Very pretty. We also watched a darker scene in the show Widow’s Bay in HDR, which was an excellent showcase of the range of detail this screen can show.

The new screen helps this MacBook stay exceedingly light, as well. Much of the savings in weight comes from the OLED design, since the screen uses thinner and lighter materials than screens past. Despite the computer using the same aluminum chassis as the previous MacBook Pro, the new 14-inch MacBook Pro weighs just 3 pounds, which is 12 percent lighter than before.

To put the new screen into context, we were also shown a comparison between Apple’s OLED and the OLED of a high-end competitive Windows laptop. The difference was striking, especially in off-axis viewing. At its worst, there were visible rainbow effects on the Windows machine’s screen.

There’s a catch, though. While Apple’s screen can get up to 2,000 nits, it’s normally capped at 1,000 nits. When the laptop’s built-in adaptive brightness sensor thinks you need a boost, the screen’s brightens increases up to 2,000 nits. So the brightness only hits the high end when you’re outside or working under really harsh lighting conditions. The previous-gen MacBook Pros also did this, but to a lesser degree. With this machine, you’re getting 400 more nits of SDR brightness right out of the box, with the ability to crank it up to twice as much.

Glare is another issue with conventional, low-brightness OLED laptops that the MacBook Pro improves. When deeper blacks are rendered on the screen, it often results in harsher reflections. Sometimes it can get so bad, it feels like you’re looking into a mirror. The brightness alone on the MacBook Pro goes a long way toward overpowering glare and reflections, but Apple is also using what it claims is a “state-of-the-art” anti-reflective coating. Almost every laptop screen uses some kind anti-reflective coating, so one can hope that Apple’s “state-of-the-art” coating is at least on par with those, but I can attest that this is the very best I’ve seen.

The ultimate solution to cutting glare and reflections is still the option for a nano-texture display, where the anti-reflective pattern is actually etched into the screen itself rather than layered on top. That’s available as an upgrade on both the 14-inch and 16-inch models.

The other major innovation happening with the MacBook Pro’s screen is the introduction of touch. Like OLED itself, this is a feature we’ve seen on many other laptops for well over a decade. I came in hoping Apple would swing for the fences here, but its use of touch interaction feels much more modest. (Let us quickly forget the failure of the Touch Bar from 2016.)

The implementation itself is impressive. Interacting with elements on the screen feels every bit as fluid as it does on an iPad. The screen also supports many of the familiar trackpad gestures, such as pinch to zoom or a three-fingered swipe to switch Spaces.

The most natural feeling touch optimizations have been lifted directly from iPadOS. If you tap in the top left window controls, they zoom in to let you select more precisely. You can even grab a window and flick it to the side to quickly get into Split View. My favorite optimization is the ability to tap, hold, and slide down to select an item within the Menu Bar. Very clever. And yet, I was still hoping for a stronger commitment on the software side. You can’t use an Apple Pencil on this screen, and nothing in the hardware of the MacBook Pro has been adjusted to make touching the screen feel any more ergonomic.

The bigger issue is with third-party apps, though. While touch-to-click will work on all apps automatically, it’s up to developers to do any more than that. Even the third-party apps we were shown during demos had varying degrees of touch optimization.

The 3D modeling application ZBrush was a good example. While certain aspects were touch-enabled, none of the controls or menus had the useful optimizations that Apple’s first-party apps had. Hopefully this touch option will become more widely adopted, but I can foresee a future in which these fingers-first interactions are mostly forgotten outside of occasionally scrolling down a webpage with your thumb. And let me be clear: All of this is more than what Microsoft has ever done to optimize Windows 11 for touch. (And Microsoft even has Windows 2-in-1 tablets to support!)

With the 2026 MacBook Pro, Apple is doubling down on what’s working well for it: leading the way in high-end hardware. There’s plenty more testing to do on the MacBook Pro in the coming weeks, but this OLED screen already looks like the one the entire industry will be chasing for years to come.

#45. Judge denies the Justice Department's bid to stop processing California ballots

People extract ballots from their envelopes and prepare them to be scanned for counting in the Vote-By-Mail Processing Boards area at the Orange County Registrar of Voters Facility in Santa Ana, Calif., on Oct. 28. Apu Gomes/Getty Images hide caption

A federal judge has denied an unprecedented, emergency request by the Department of Justice to segregate thousands of California ballots in the midterm elections.

The Justice Department wants California election officials to stop processing ballots pertaining to 20,000 individuals that federal officials say are "likely" noncitizens.

State and local officials in California have countered that the DOJ's list of "likely" noncitizens is unreliable, with a preliminary analysis by Los Angeles County showing that many on the list do not have active voter registrations. The officials also claim that setting aside ballots just days before Election Day, when voting and processing mailed ballots are already underway, would be unlawful and infeasible and would disenfranchise eligible voters.

In his 26-page ruling issued Wednesday evening, U.S. District Judge David Carter, a Clinton appointee, denied the Justice Department's request for a temporary restraining order to stop processing the ballots in question.

"Never before in American history has the federal government, on the eve of an election, sought to coerce a state and local government to abandon their own Constitutional elections procedures and bend to the will of the Executive Branch," he wrote.

Carter also expressed concern about taking action that could disenfranchise eligible voters and create chaos.

"Once a vote is segregated, lasting harm has occurred," Carter wrote at the conclusion of his order. "If the Executive can discount votes and threaten an ongoing election due to unproven allegations, then our democracy is in danger. This Court declines to be a party to such process."

The judge said that the federal government can address the issue by continuing to indict people for the crime of illegally voting as noncitizens. DOJ officials say in the last two months they have charged dozens of people for registering or voting without being a U.S. citizen.

The DOJ's lawsuit in California, which was filed Oct. 15, is among a flurry of unprecedented actions the department has waged in states close to Election Day, raising alarms about federal interference in state election procedures.

Like in California, on Monday federal prosecutors asked Washington state election officials to review a list of 400 registered voters the DOJ believes are likely noncitizens. And last week, the DOJ filed an unusual lawsuit in Alaska that seeks to stop the state from certifying the results in its Senate race if the Republican incumbent loses due to votes for a longshot candidate with a similar name who is accused of being a spoiler candidate.

In California's most recent motion to the court, state Attorney General Rob Bonta, a Democrat, asked the judge to take into account the circumstances of DOJ's demand. "This litigation is a precursor to further interference here in California," Bonta wrote. "The Court must not be drawn into this chilling degradation of democratic values and constitutional principles."

The Department of Justice quickly filed a notice that it is appealing Carter's denial. The matter could ultimately wind up before the U.S. Supreme Court.

"Every day, more ballots are processed by election officials. This ongoing emergency disenfranchises American citizens through dilution," Bill Essayli, the top federal prosecutor in Los Angeles, wrote on X, adding that he would be seeking an emergency order from the 9th Circuit Court of Appeals.

The DOJ has argued the issue is urgent since once signatures are verified on returned mail ballot envelopes, the ballots are separated from their envelopes to retain voter confidentiality, making it impossible to retrieve an ineligible ballot.

Voting by noncitizens has been shown by audits and investigations to be extremely rare.

"Tonight's decision makes clear that the federal government's actions are election interference," said Jonathan Miller, chief program officer at Public Rights Project, which is counsel for Los Angeles County.

"The district court unequivocally found that DOJ's claims are unsubstantiated, that it has no basis to bring its lawsuit, and it has presented no evidence to justify its actions. The administration claims to be protecting election integrity while, at every turn, undermining the systems and federal support that safeguard our elections," Miller said.

Bonta, California's attorney general, said in a statement the ruling is an important victory, "But this fight is not over. We will keep fighting to protect California's elections from unlawful federal interference and get this baseless lawsuit dismissed."

The accuracy of the DOJ's list of "likely" noncitizens is still an ongoing matter of dispute.

A Los Angeles County analysis of a small sample of people on the DOJ's list found that a significant portion had said in records they were born in the U.S. But U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services senior official Nicolas Bartell contested that in recent court filings, writing that his office's review of those individuals found none were U.S.-born.

Bartell did acknowledge the review found four of those people "might have a claim to U.S. citizenship."

He also provided the court additional details about the federal government's methodology in creating the list in recent court filings.

But University of Delaware political scientist Kassra Oskooi, who has been tapped as an expert witness by voting rights groups who are intervening in the case, responded in his own declaration to the court that he believes USCIS's matching protocol "is likely to be unreliable."

Carter wrote in his order the federal government's protocol "raises significant concerns" and that he could not conclude its process is credible.
