#595. Mbappe, Real Madrid and La Liga face long road to redemption in China

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Kylian Mbappe, Real Madrid and La Liga have apologised for the superstar striker's comments about Mao Zedong, but the damage to their reputation in China won't be easily or quickly repaired, experts say.

The France captain, one of the best footballers on the planet, is at the centre of a major row after a video emerged this week of him appearing to make fun of former leader Mao.

Mbappe talked about being amused by an internet meme of his face photoshopped onto "some guy, think he's a Chinese dictator".

Mao remains officially revered as the hero of the communist revolution and founder of the People's Republic of China.

He is blamed by historians for millions of deaths, but publicly mocking him is taboo, and Mbappe's remarks saw him become the latest foreign sports figure or entity to infuriate Beijing.

Major Chinese brands cut ties with the 27-year-old, and Spanish top-flight matches will be pulled from state television broadcaster CCTV.

Chinese state media and social media users lined up to lambast Mbappe, although they were left in the dark about what exactly he said.

China has the world's second-biggest economy and can be hugely lucrative, and within two days the striker, his club Real Madrid, and La Liga all apologised.

A spokesman for China's foreign ministry said the Beijing government took Mbappe's apology "seriously".

"Viewed from a calm, objective and emotion-free standpoint, this is clearly a very damaging incident," Yan Qiang, a veteran sports commentator, told AFP.

"It causes serious damage to the image of La Liga and Real Madrid in China.

"In the age of the mobile internet things blow up on social media quickly and emotions run high.

"An apology does not necessarily mean the end of the story.

"It's better to have an apology than no apology at all, but it's definitely not the end of the story."

The Mbappe furore and fallout is not an unfamiliar tale.

Various overseas sports leagues or players have found themselves in hot water for comments on topics that are politically sensitive to China.

China effectively cut ties with the NBA in 2019 after then-Houston Rockets general manager Daryl Morey tweeted his support for Hong Kong protesters.

NBA executives said Morey had the right to freedom of expression. In contrast, La Liga immediately distanced itself from what Mbappe said.

The NBA, which along with football is the most popular sport in China, was wiped from CCTV for more than a year.

NBA pre-season matches only returned to China last year.

Australian National University lecturer Debby Chan called the NBA case -- and now Mbappe -- an illustration of China's consumer nationalism, where online sentiment and state media converge into "a spiral of outrage".

NBA commissioner Adam Silver said the league lost "hundreds of millions of dollars" over the split.

A source within La Liga told AFP that China and the United States alone finance the "majority" of international broadcasting rights, which were valued at 748 million euros ($834 million) for the 2024-25 season.

According to the Real Madrid-affiliated newspaper Marca, the Madrid club generates between 10 and 15 percent of its sales of jerseys and merchandise in China.

Real Madrid also counts Chinese companies among its main sponsors, with Hisense, sports betting firm Kaiyun Sports, and Linglong Tire all featuring prominently.

Adam Zhang, founder of the Beijing-based Key-Solution Sports Consulting, said showing fast and heartfelt remorse was the first step.

"This will influence the decision on whether to continue the broadcast ban and, if so, for how long," he told AFP.

Sports commentator Yan cast doubt on whether the reputation of Mbappe, whose face has adorned drinks bottles and advertising campaigns in China, can ever fully recover.

"Judging by commercial sponsorships and big ad campaigns he appeared in during the World Cup, he has a huge influence in China," he said.

"If that influence can't be sustained there can be no talk of commercial interests, and La Liga and Real Madrid will definitely be badly affected."

Yan said it was "genuinely hard to predict" what happens next.

"In the short term, I'm certainly not optimistic," he added.

"If they want to repair their image and the relationship, their future actions and behaviour will be key."

The LSU Tigers are going to make the statement "the rich get richer" look silly. That is, if what Lane Kiffin just said while speaking to Barstool Sports' Brandon Walker about the future of his roster holds true. His message was straightforward: He believes LSU's current personnel represent a starting point rather than the ceiling.

That is a bold assessment eight games into his tenure. Kiffin is not simply suggesting the Tigers can improve their execution or become more comfortable with his system. He expects the rosters to get better, too.

For LSU fans, that is the appeal of his comments. The program's potential was part of the attraction when he arrived. Now, Kiffin is explaining why his experience in Baton Rouge has strengthened that belief.

Asked about his decision, Kiffin contrasted his confidence in choosing LSU with his lingering frustration over a decision against Kentucky. His answer moved beyond football facilities or a recruiting pitch. He pointed toward feeling comfortable in the place and the support surrounding the program.

"Yeah, and even more, and I don't just say these things, as you know," Kiffin said. "Unlike that Kentucky game, where every day it haunts me, a decision in that game. I've never thought twice about this decision, just from getting here and being at home here. The stadium, the environment, the state, the people and how much support there is for football."

That matters because building a roster requires more than identifying talented players. A coach needs an environment that supports retaining, developing and adding them. Kiffin's response suggests he believes LSU offers that foundation, although his confidence alone does not guarantee future results.

His statement came when discussing what the Tigers have already shown. Kiffin described stretches of play that, in his assessment, compare with anyone nationally. He then connected those flashes to an offseason he portrayed as unusually complicated.

"We are eight games in, and to see that we have played already as well as anybody in the country at times," Kiffin said. The final sentence is the headline within the answer. Kiffin is presenting this season's roster as something future teams should surpass. That creates an ambitious standard for his staff's recruiting and development.
