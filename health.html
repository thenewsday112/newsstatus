#70. RFK Jr. to ease access to unapproved peptides, draft policy states

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

October 28, 2026 / 9:20 PM EDT / CBS News

Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. took steps this week that could open up access to certain unapproved peptide drugs that are being promoted for wellness.

In a draft policy filed Wednesday, Kennedy said he had determined that compounding pharmacies could prepare six specific peptides — BPC-157, Epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, TB-500 — even though they do not have approval from the Food and Drug Administration.

"By allowing limited compounding of the substances addressed in this guidance under appropriate safeguards, the policy provides patients with a more controlled alternative," Kennedy wrote.

Compounding pharmacies mix ingredients to prepare customized medications for individual patients.

Although they are not FDA approved, the six peptides in Kennedy's new policy are often still marketed with health claims.

The Department of Health and Human Services "did not intend to take action" against licensed pharmacies or physicians that compound "a drug product using a peptide bulk drug substance that is not a component of an FDA-approved drug product," Kennedy's policy said.

Peptides are small proteins that can have powerful effects. There are several, such as insulin, that are FDA approved as being safe and effective. GLP-1 medications used for weight loss and to treat diabetes also contain FDA approved peptides.

In July, an FDA advisory panel voted to recommend allowing the six unapproved peptides to be used in compounding, although that decision did not constitute FDA approval. That vote, however, went against the recommendations of the FDA's own scientists, who said there isn't enough evidence of safety and effectiveness for each of the six peptides to make such a determination.

Kennedy cited that panel's recommendation in his decision, saying he had determined the policy "advances patient choice and freedom while appropriately protecting patient safety."

In a statement provided to CBS News, an HHS spokesperson said that pharmacies will have to do quality testing and safety reporting, and that should help patients know what is in these peptide drugs that are currently being sold by unregulated sources.

"The policy does not grant FDA approval or establish clinical safety and effectiveness," the HHS spokesperson said. "It requires patient-specific prescriptions, traceable ingredients, quality testing, and safety reporting to hold compounders accountable and give patients an alternative to unregulated sellers."

The draft policy requires an FDA-registered ingredient maker, a lab certificate for the raw ingredients, and reports of serious side effects.

In a CBS News investigation published last week, a laboratory test was conducted on an experimental peptide weight-loss drug known as retatrutide that was purchased from a bodega in Brooklyn, New York.

The lab test, however, found that the sample of retatrutide purchased from the bodega did not contain a peptide at all.

A CBS News investigation last summer found that dozens of clinics across the country, staffed by licensed physicians and nurse practitioners, have been openly advertising retatrutide even though it has not been reviewed by the FDA and remains in clinical trials by drug giant Eli Lilly.

Kennedy's draft policy will be open to public comment beginning Friday through Nov. 13. The policy will remain in place until the FDA finalizes a rule regarding compounded peptides. However, at this point, the FDA has not proposed one.

Dr. Céline Gounder, Adam Yamaguchi and Kerry Breen contributed to this report.

#497. Clare Crawley ‘Literally Almost Died’ in 2025 and Lost 20 Lbs

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Former Bachelorette Clare Crawley is opening up about her health journey.

“Last December, I literally almost died. I had lost 20 lbs. I did not have that weight to lose and I was super sick and just, frankly, wanted to die,” Crawley, 45, said on the Tuesday, October 27, episode of the “She’s All Bach” podcast. “I was feeling so horrible and just felt like I couldn’t go on how life was for me health wise.”

She continued, “It was years of going through this that I was like, ‘I don’t know how much longer I can do this.’ So much pain. But I know what it feels like to be at low, low, with my health and emotionally because I had just lost my mom at the time, too.”

In 2025, Crawley shared her journey with Topical Steroid Withdrawal (TSW) after undergoing breast explant surgery. TSW is a severe reaction that occurs when moderate- or high-potency topical steroids are used on skin for a prolonged period, according to the National Eczema Society.

Now, Crawley — who shares a daughter with husband Ryan Dawkins — shared that she wants to “help other women fight for their health and to be seen within the health care industry.” (Crawley and Dawkins welcomed their daughter, Rowen, via surrogate in 2024.)

“I had a lot of doctors steering me in the wrong way and postponing my healing, delaying my healing and giving me the wrong diagnosis, the wrong treatments,” Crawley said. “So it’s kind of empowered me so deeply and embedded in my soul that this fire in me has come back of, like, ‘I’m going to heal. I’m not only going to heal for myself but for my daughter and for my husband and for the life I have now.’”

She continued, “So to wrap it up in a little bow, it’s like, I really deeply appreciate even the smallest moments now because I know what it’s like to not be able to get out of my bed and to not be able to look in a mirror for months at a time. To hate what I see. To not see myself and to see a shell of a human being.”

With this perspective, Crawley also has a newfound appreciation for her hair.

“I had lost all of my hair twice in the last couple of years. Like, 90 percent of it,” she said. “I was just like, had shreds and wore hats all of them and hid. But now I tell Ryan every night, I’m like, ‘She back. She back.’ I’m like, ‘Oh my God, I would do anything for this hair.’ I see my gray hair come in and I’m like, ‘I f***ing have hair.’ But it’s hair, you know. Like, I love it. I love the things that I never appreciated before.”

Crawley shared that she used to look at her husband and question why he was married to her, adding that she felt like she looked “like a troll.”

“I don’t even see myself. But I look at my hair and every day … I’m just so thankful. I’m thankful that I have it. I’m thankful that I don’t have to hide,” she said. “I don’t even like wearing makeup anymore because — I haven’t been able to wear makeup for a couple years now because my skin was so bad — but I’m like, ‘I don’t even want to wear it because I love feeling my skin and seeing how soft it is.’ I see my wrinkles and I’m like, ‘But it’s good skin. But it’s not inflamed and it’s healthy skin.’ I’ll take a wrinkle any day.”

She continued, “It’s because I’ve been laughing and smiling and feeling alive again. That’s why we get those wrinkles. If I was to be a corpsey, like I want my daughter to see me … I want her to see all these lines because it means I’m living and laughing, you know, I just have a very deep appreciation for life now when I’ve been so sick.”

Savannah Chrisley’s fiancé Jason Galanis’ ex-wife Monet Berger claimed she was a “victim” of his fraud when she filed for divorce in 2017 after nine years of marriage.

“[Berger] is not aware of the extent of the communities’ liability for [Galanis’] conduct during the marriage. She was a victim of his crimes and received compensation from the Victim’s Defense Fund,” her divorce petition obtained by Us read.

In September 2015, Galanis was arrested and charged in connection with a multimillion-dollar investment scheme. In May 2016, he was arrested again while on bail and after being indicted in another fraud scheme that targeted a Native American tribal entity.

Berger also told the court she was applying for “innocent spouse relief” for their joint tax returns, both filed and unfiled, during their marriage.

She claimed she was “unaware” that her husband was “underpaying [or] failing to pay” Uncle Sam. Berger also alleged Galanis, 56, “forged” her name “on more than one tax document” during their union.

Berger filed her dissolution of marriage in Los Angeles Superior Court on August 16, 2017, citing “irreconcilable differences” as the reason for their split.

She listed their marriage date as October 21, 2006, and their separation date as May 23, 2016. The former couple shared no children.

She also claimed that because of his “current criminal conviction,” the Department of Justice seized their community property for restitution, including their shared vehicles, checking and savings accounts, retirement accounts, and more.

“[Berger] is unaware of the extent of the property taken by the Department of Justice, as [Galanis] was responsible for the parties’ assets, debts, income and expenses,” the documents read.

Berger asked for spousal support and for the court to terminate Jason’s ability to seek spousal support from her. She agreed they would pay their own attorney’s fees and asked the court to treat their debts as separate, including any accrued by his businesses, affiliates, partnerships, etc.

Berger also demanded possession of the couple’s Yorkshire Terrier, her Bloomingdale’s and Barney’s credit cards, a 2014 Mercedes-Benz M-Class and all her jewelry.

Galanis never responded to her divorce filing, so the judge issued a default judgment on April 26, 2018.

The judgment also revealed more alleged deceptions from Chrisley’s now-fiancé during his marriage to Berger.

While going through boxes of personal items in 2016, Berger allegedly found a letter from the L.A. County Recorder’s Office advising Galanis to return a form within a certain timeframe to complete their marriage process.

“[Galanis] never told [Berger] that he received the letter from the Recorder’s Office, advising him that the marriage license could not be completed‚ nor that he did not return the requested information,” the judgment read.

She had her legal team investigate, and it turned out their marriage was not registered with the Department of Health.

“Nonetheless, because [Berger] believed that she and [Galanis] were married, because they held themselves out as a married couple, including filing joint tax returns, it is necessary to dissolve the putative marriage,” the judge noted.

The court granted Berger’s request that Galanis could never request spousal support from her, but reserved jurisdiction to award her support in the future.

Galanis’ ex was awarded all items in her possession, her “premarital and post-separation earnings,” Social Security benefits, and any assets she acquired from and after the date of separation, as well as any gifts she received from her husband before or during their marriage.

The judgment also revealed Berger donated several of Galanis’ personal items, including his clothes, books and more, to charity.

“I’ve prayed for this life. I’ve cried for this life. And there were moments I truly wondered if this life would ever be mine,” the reality star wrote in part. She also included a sweet message about her baby daddy, only referring to him as “J.”

Galanis also has ties to Hunter Biden, and previously worked with the former First Son on investment ventures.

“Yes, there is a huge age gap there, OK?” she said on her “Unlocked” podcast. “And that’s probably part of the reason why I kept a lot of it quiet, because I know how brutal the public can be. But I genuinely do not care.”

For reference, Chrisley’s famous father is 57, making Todd just one year older than her fiancé.

#88. New book revisits the 2023 killing of an unhoused man in crisis on the NYC subway

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Jordan Neely was shouting threats on the NYC subway. Fellow passenger Daniel Penny put Neely into a chokehold and killed him. Journalist Anand Giridharadas believes both men felt abandoned. Above, protestors rally in support of Neely in New York City on May 24, 2023. Michael M. Santiago/Getty Images hide caption

On May 1, 2023, an unhoused Black man named Jordan Neely boarded the New York City subway. For years, Neely had drawn crowds with his Michael Jackson impersonation, but on that day he was in crisis, shouting threats and frightening fellow passengers.

A white former Marine named Daniel Penny stepped in, putting Neely into a chokehold for six minutes, killing him. Later, a video of the encounter divided the country. To some, Penny was doing what the police weren't there to do. To others, Neely was a victim, another Black man whose life had been treated as disposable.

Journalist Anand Giridharadas spent more than two years investigating what the video couldn't show. He wanted to write a story "that would take us to the heart of America and what it means to live in this country, in this moment." When tragic stories become social media fodder, Giridharadas says, "human beings become caricatures."

In December 2024, Penny was acquitted of criminally negligent homicide after a jury hung on a second degree manslaughter charge and the prosecution dismissed it. Within days of the verdict, Vice President-elect JD Vance invited Penny to the Army-Navy game alongside President-elect Trump. Soon afterward, a Silicon Valley venture capital firm gave him a job.

In his new book, Man in the Mirror: Hope, Struggle, and Belonging in an American City, Giridharadas posits that both men felt let down by the systems that should have protected them.

"I think Jordan Neely had felt abandoned for a very long time, and had reason to feel that way," Giridharadas says. "Daniel Penny would claim that he felt abandoned in terms of law enforcement not being there, and in terms of all the systems that had allowed Jordan Neely to be in that subway car in that condition instead of all of the other conditions that he might have been in had there been a better system of care."

Giridharadas says it's that sense of abandonment that leads people to take matters into their own hands, often with tragic results: "I think it behooves us to think about the condition in which we've left people, this sense of abandonment that we've allowed to be so pervasive that decent people feel like maybe there's no other route for folks besides just DIYing it."

On Jordan Neely's criminal record and the city's attempt at rehabilitation

He had a record of interactions with the police that went back more than a decade. But it was very trivial stuff at the beginning. It was essentially the stuff of what it takes to be homeless in New York, jumping turnstiles, sleeping rough. And then in more recent years, it had gotten more serious and he'd committed a couple of violent crimes, assaults, punching a woman in the face, things like that. And he had gone to Rikers. He'd been charged. And then as part of this alternative to incarceration program — and there are programs like this popping up around the country, where folks get an alternative to going to prison that is considered more restorative, more in line with actually helping people who have substance issues or other issues — he gets to avail of one of these programs. Prosecution, defense, they all agree. And he gets sent to this kind of rehab facility in the Bronx instead of prison. And one day, he just walks out of that facility.

And then, in April 2023, just a few weeks before he died, he is [at] Coney Island, one of the terminal points on the subway system, and he's acting erratically on the platform. Outreach workers try to deal with him to no avail. The cops have some interaction with him, but they don't actually realize that it is the guy who is out with a warrant for his arrest because he walked out of this program. And the outreach workers who were there that night don't quite realize that he's a guy that their agency knows very well, just maybe not those individual workers. And he sort of runs off into the night. And a few weeks later, he's dead on the floor of a different subway car.

On Daniel Penny's origins in West Islip, Long Island

These towns in central Long Island have this very complicated relationship [with New York City]. … On the one hand, it's very out of the city, but it's right there. A lot of people commute in every day for work. Some don't. These are towns that were largely populated by white flight in the last several decades. … In many ways, these are towns that are almost like middle-class refugee camps from the squalor and terror of New York City.

Maybe even understanding immigrant stories is a more helpful framework for understanding these places. … Immigrants have very complicated relationships with the places they leave. We don't think about that with places that are 45 minutes away from a metropolis, but I actually think that is the way to understand the town in which Daniel Penny grew up. … These are communities where there's often a contempt, often barely concealed, for all the people one wanted to get away from, when one left.

I can't know what's in his heart. I didn't sense that kind of sadness or remorse. And not only did I not necessarily pick that up in that brief conversation we had at his victory party a year and a half after the incident, but one of the things that's striking in both the bodycam video on the scene when they first get there, as well as the interrogation ... is that even in those moments, forget remorse, he doesn't even seem to have curiosity about what may have happened to that guy.

He sort of gave the impression of a kind of blankness, ideologically. And that was one of the things that then became very strange, that all these people really wanted him to be their symbol and projected onto him all these ideas. And then he, of course, willingly embraced it. He embraced the money that came from it. And then, most dramatically still, at the end of the case, not only was he content to appear with JD Vance and Donald Trump at the Army-Navy game, but shortly thereafter, he got a job offer that gobsmacked many people. This is someone who, I think, has not graduated from college. He was offered a job as a partner at the venture capital firm in Silicon Valley called Andreessen Horowitz. ... He was made partner on their American dynamism team, which is a team that invests in startups that do weapons systems and drones and other things supposedly in the national interest.

On the politicization of the case and parallels with Luigi Mangione

I had spent the last year and a half hearing Daniel Penny's people make an argument that was basically like, "The system wasn't there, the system wasn't responsive. Daniel Penny stepped into a void and did what he had to do, took matters into his own hands because of this void." And then in the jury deliberations during that moment was the Mangione assassination of Brian Thompson, of United Health Care. And as that case started to become better known, a similar argument started to develop around Luigi Mangione, this time on the parts of the left instead of parts of right that Luigi Mangione was stepping into this void of a cruel healthcare system and stepping in because so many people were abandoned and he felt abandoned. …

And then Hawk Newsome, Black Lives Matter activist in New York, says, yes ... if they're gonna have vigilantes, we need Black vigilantes. But we also need to make our own schools, we need to have our own media, we have to have our own everything.

And I just thought to myself, gosh, is this the last kind of common thread? The idea that you could hear on the far right, the far left, and elsewhere that we're abandoned, the system's rigged, no one's listening, take matters into your own hands. The sense of, somebody step up. Somebody do something.

#535. Flu Season Is Back: How to Protect Your Family and Prevent Spread at Home

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Flu season is back, and families want practical ways to limit the spread at home. Here is what to know about timing, symptoms, prevention and medical care.

Flu season in the northern hemisphere typically runs October through March and can stretch into May, according to Mayo Clinic. Southern hemisphere flu usually falls between April and September. Outbreaks often begin in classrooms, where younger children with developing immune systems are most vulnerable.

Common flu symptoms include fever or chills, cough, sore throat, runny or stuffy nose, muscle aches, headaches and fatigue, according to the CDC. Vomiting and diarrhea can also occur, more often in children. Most people recover within two weeks, but complications such as pneumonia can be life-threatening, and flu can worsen conditions like asthma and chronic heart disease.

Flu viruses travel in droplets when an infected person coughs, sneezes or talks, and can also spread through contaminated surfaces, Mayo Clinic explains. A person is typically contagious from about a day before symptoms appear until roughly five to seven days afterward. David A. Cabezas, a family medicine specialist at UF Health Urgent Care, recommends isolating the sick person, wearing masks, washing hands often and improving ventilation to limit household spread.

Clean high-touch surfaces regularly with EPA-approved disinfectants, focusing on doorknobs, light switches, countertops, phones and remotes, according to University of Rochester Medicine. The American Lung Association advises carrying hand sanitizer with at least 60 percent alcohol when soap and water are not available. Amy Pridemore, assistant clinical professor at Auburn University College of Nursing, told Campus Insights Media keeping sick children home until they are fever-free for 24 hours helps stop germs from moving through schools. “The younger they are, the more likely they are to get sick because they don’t have that strong immune system yet. That’s why you see a lot of illnesses start in the kindergarten classrooms and in the pre-K classrooms before they get a little older and develop that immune system a little more,” Pridemore explained.

Higher-risk groups include young children, adults over 65, pregnant people and those with weakened immune systems or chronic conditions such as asthma, diabetes and heart disease, according to Mayo Clinic.

Everyone 6 months of age and older should get a flu vaccine every season, especially people at high risk, according to the American Red Cross. The CDC maintains a list of who should and shouldn’t get the shot. Flu vaccination during the 2019-2020 season prevented an estimated 7 million illnesses, 3 million medical visits, 100,000 hospitalizations and 7,000 deaths in the United States, the CDC reports. Pridemore said the shot helps the body recognize and fight the virus, making illness less severe even for people who still get sick.

Antiviral drugs are the primary treatment and require a prescription, according to the CDC. They work best when started within two days of symptoms and can shorten illness by about a day. Otherwise healthy people can often recover at home with rest, fluids and over-the-counter remedies. Seek emergency care for difficulty breathing, chest pain, persistent dizziness, seizures or severe weakness. In children, warning signs also include rapid breathing, bluish lips or nail beds and signs of dehydration such as no tears when crying.
