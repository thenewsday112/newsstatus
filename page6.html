#8. A top general pushed out at Pentagon's Joint Staff

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia is the latest senior military officer to be sidelined.

A top general at the Pentagon was suddenly removed from his senior post earlier this week and has now retired, according to two sources familiar with the situation.

Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia served in the Joint Staff as the Joint Staff's Director for Strategy, Plans & Policy, an important office, that handles long-term strategic planning and is known in military jargon as the J-5.

Sylvia was removed from the position for a lack of confidence, according to one of the sources.  With his removal Sylvia retired from the U.S. Army after having served in the role for 11 months.

Sylvia becomes the latest senior military officer to be either fired or sidelined during Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's tenure.  However, one source said it was unclear whether Sylvia's removal was ordered by Hegseth or Gen. Dan Caine, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff.

"The Chairman, the Joint Staff, and the Joint Force extend our deepest gratitude to Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia and his family for their 32 years of distinguished service to our nation," said a Joint Staff spokesperson.

"We wish him and his family the best as they graduate to new opportunities and the next chapters of their lives," the spokesman added.

Earlier this week, Sylvia was listed as still being the director of the planning office on the Joint Staff's website, but the website now lists only the name of Vice Director Maj. Gen. Mark H. Slocum.

The J-5 is recognized as one of the Joint Staff's most important directorates in the Joint Staff along with the directorate for Operations, the J-3, that is responsible for the planning and execution of military operations.

Last year, two prominent Army generals serving on the Joint Staff were sidelined and had to retire early, including Sylvia's immediate predecessor as the J-5, Lt. Gen. Joseph "JP" McGee, who retired last October.

McGee had been nominated under the Biden administration to become the director of the Joint Staff, but he was not renominated by the Trump administration.  With no other slots available to him as a three or four star general he had to retire.

Also retiring early last year was Lt. Gen. Douglas Sims, the director of the Joint Staff, who was widely expected to be on track for a fourth star.

#357. Bluesky Is Blocking Links to a Major Leak of DHS Employee Records

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bluesky is blocking users from opening links to a newly leaked archive of records on more than 41,000 Department of Homeland Security employees and contractors, and labels the pages “malicious.”

The files include people’s email addresses and phone numbers, “shared without their authorization,” Bluesky tells WIRED, in violation of its community guidelines. The company says it chose to block the links, rather than remove individual posts, “to avoid limiting public discussion or restricting broader access to reporting and commentary.”

The DHS records, published Wednesday by the transparency nonprofit Distributed Denial of Secrets, list employees' and contractors’ names, contact information, and membership in internal groups, tied in some cases to surveillance and undercover operations. A WIRED investigation found that the files lay out in detail how DHS is organized, from senior leadership to individual immigration agents, and in some cases appear to show who has access to certain technologies.

Users who try to open the archive through Bluesky’s website or app land on a page labeled "Blocked Link," which says the URL has been identified as “malicious” and blocked for their safety. Bluesky did not respond to questions about whether the block was imposed automatically or whether any government agency had asked the company to restrict access to the data.

The DDoSecrets homepage loads normally from Bluesky. At least one third-party app, Klearsky, opens links to the DHS archive with no warning.

Bluesky promotes a decentralized approach to content moderation that lets users pick independent services that label or filter posts. Its current community guidelines bar sharing “home addresses, personal contact info, financial or medical data, or identification documents.” (A preview of the DHS leak analyzed by WIRED apparently contained work-related contact information one expert described as details that would be printed “on a business card.”) The same guidelines explicitly protect “factual reporting” on matters of public interest that are “related to government transparency and public officials.”

Bluesky started in 2019 as a Twitter research project, later became independent, and opened to the public in February 2024. By August of this year, it counted 45 million registered accounts, and an estimated 10.4 million people used its mobile app in June. Toni Schneider, the founding CEO of Automattic, has run the company since cofounder Jay Graber stepped down in March. Because the network runs on an open protocol, outside developers can build their own Bluesky apps and decide which of the company’s moderation labels to apply.

Emma Best, cofounder of DDoSecrets, says this is not the first time a social media platform has wrongly labeled the organization's website malicious or tried to restrict access to its publications. Twitter suspended DDoSecrets’ account in 2020, prior to the Elon Musk acquisition and X rebrand, and blocked links to its website after the group published BlueLeaks, a 269-gigabyte trove of internal records from hundreds of police agencies and fusion centers. Best says tech platforms often give in to government pressure but insists they cannot prevent the information from spreading. “They can't stop the signal,” they say.

A group calling itself the Department of Peace gave the archive, dubbed “Icepick,” to DDoSecrets and journalist maia arson crimew. crimew also built a website for searching the records, and Bluesky blocks that site in its website and app as well.

At least one of Bluesky’s competitors has taken a different tack. On X, the Grok chatbot built by X’s parent company pointed curious users to the Icepick website, describing it as a “cleaned searchable web tool” and saying that the complete dataset can be downloaded via DDoSecrets’ website or by torrent. X did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

#20. Hackers expose the identities of more than 40,000 DHS employees

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The U.S. Department of Homeland Security emblem on the U.S.Immigration and Customs Enforcement building as seen on January 07, 2026 in Washington, D.C. Heather Diehl/Getty Images North America hide caption

A hacking group cracked the U.S. Department of Homeland Security's personnel files, exposing the names, phone numbers and addresses of more than 40,000 DHS agents, operatives, and associates, on Wednesday, including those involved in undercover operations.

"On July 17th, Markwayne Mullin said he was 'turning up the heat.' This is what happens to ICE when you do that," a spokesperson for the hacktivist group behind the breach, the Department of Peace, said in an online statement.

The hackers said they stole the data by subverting DHS's single sign on system, according to an online post. It said it distributed that material to the group Distributed Denial of Secrets, which posted it online in a database called Icepick. The Department of Peace also shared the trove of data with Ryan Ottignon, a U.S.-based journalist, and a Swiss hacker known as maia arson crimew. Together, the two created a searchable online database, with crimew doing all of the technical work, Ottignon told NPR.

DDoSecrets cofounder Emma Best called the leak "extraordinary but deceptive in its simplicity," because it makes explicit connections between "officials responsible for overseeing covert and undercover operations …along with key personnel in DHS's ever-escalating rendition and deportation programs."

Other activists say the information will be a boon to those investigating DHS and filing freedom of information requests, which, due to a lack of specific information, like names, email addresses, can often yield useless results.

In a statement, DHS said it was "aware of a potential breach and is actively working to address the situation swiftly and effectively." The department added that it is taking necessary steps to "ensure all are properly protected."

More than 21,000 records identify employees with U.S. Customs and Border Protection. Another 10,000 relate to Immigration and Customs Enforcement employees and its Homeland Security Investigations unit.

It is an unprecedented breach of intelligence, coming on the heels of similar hacking attacks at the FBI. However, the database is far from comprehensive and represents about a fifth of Homeland Security's workforce, which staffs a little more than 220,000 people.

Ottignon, who has worked on similar data leak projects in the past, rejected suggestions that he and others connected to the Department of Peace or Distribution Denials of Secrets were doxing government employees.

"We are in a situation in which a group of public officials go around into cities and states and all around the country, and they seek to hide who they are. Very frequently using masks, wearing plain clothes. And they do universally unspeakable things to these communities," Ottignon said. He added that he believed that kind of behavior is unacceptable in a free society.

"We need oversight for useful government, if it exists at all. So this is just one step in that oversight process," he added.

In a note attached to the files, DDoSecrets wrote, "They have our information, so it's only fair that we have theirs."

#85. California judge denies Trump DOJ request to segregate alleged noncitizen ballots

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

A federal judge in California on Wednesday refused the Trump administration’s request to segregate 20,000 mail-in ballots from individuals it alleges are noncitizens.

“This request violates longstanding constitutional principles mandating that states have control over their elections,” U.S. District Judge David Carter wrote. “If Plaintiff’s last-minute request for relief were granted, either party could create chaos for their own political purposes to disrupt the election process.”

What the administration was asking for, he wrote, “would wreak havoc and disrupt the election that is already underway, create a chilling effect on election workers and voters alike, and discount the votes of United States citizens.”

He also questioned whether the government’s claims of over 20,000 noncitizen voters were accurate.

“Given the vagueness of Plaintiff’s descriptions of its procedures and the unreliability of its similar claims in other states, the Court cannot conclude that Plaintiff’s process is credible,” Carter wrote, pointing to similar allegations the administration had made in a neighboring state.

“In Nevada, DHS initially identified up to 15,903 registered voters as noncitizens based on ‘high-confidence’ matches, but later reduced that number to 6,218, and then to 185 after conducting its manual review. Nevada officials later confirmed that all 185 individuals were citizens,” the judge wrote.

“Had Nevada officials accepted Plaintiff’s allegations there as true, 185 citizens would have been deprived of their vote. This Court will not deprive Californians who have met the requirements for voter registration of their fundamental right because of Plaintiff’s unfounded allegations.”

The Justice Department filed the suit Oct. 15, seeking a court order that the state “set aside vote-by-mail ballots from persons identified by the Federal Government likely to be noncitizens” and not count them until their citizenship is confirmed.

The department said it had compiled “a list of over 20,000 names of people” the government “believes are likely non-citizens.”

Attorneys for the state countered that the government’s numbers are unreliable. In a July letter, it put the number of suspect voters at 190,000 and has refused to release information about how it has generated its lists.

“Where is the evidence?” an attorney representing California, Christina McCall, said at the hearing Tuesday, calling the government’s allegations “vague” and “not reliable.”

Another attorney for the state, Anne Bellows, told the judge “the stakes here are enormous,” arguing the government was trying to force California to initiate an unclear investigation into the citizenship of tens of thousands of people without any clear standards shortly before an election.

It “is a recipe for disenfranchisement,” Bellows said.

First Assistant U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli told Carter: “We’re not telling California what they have to do. We’re telling them what they can’t do.”

Essayli said that once the state is made aware of potential noncitizens on its voter rolls, “they need to do something.”

“What they cannot do is do nothing and proceed to count the votes,” he said.

The judge said that if the government believes there is noncitizen voting, which is a crime, it should bring charges.

“Don’t you have the power of indictments?” Carter asked Essayli. “You brought 28 indictments,” he said, referring to a recent announcement by prosecutors of people who have been found to have voted illegally in California in recent years. “You may find others. You have the right to bring that indictment against them.”

“You’ve got a remedy, for goodness’ sakes,” Carter said. “Go out and indict.”

Essayli on Wednesday bashed the judge’s ruling and said an appeal is underway.

“Every day, more ballots are processed by election officials. This ongoing emergency disenfranchises American citizens through dilution,” he said in a statement. “The Solicitor General authorized an immediate appeal. We filed our Notice of Appeal and will immediately seek an emergency order from the Ninth Circuit.”

The Justice Department has already been to a federal appeals court once in the case. It filed a motion to expedite the case to the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals last week, after Carter did not act on its request to rule in the matter by Thursday.

The appeals court held an emergency hearing Sunday and ruled against the administration 2-1 on Monday.

The majority found it did not have jurisdiction because Carter had not yet ruled, but it found the government’s “claimed emergency” was at least partly “a self-constructed one.”

“At least part of the delay here is attributable to the United States’s own delay in filing suit,” Circuit Judge Mark Bennett wrote in the ruling.

“The United States has taken issue with California’s voter rolls since at least July 16, when it sent a letter to California’s secretary of state citing its ‘[p]reliminary’ analysis of California’s voter rolls. And neither California’s law permitting the processing of ballots twenty-nine days before an election, nor its law providing for the issuance of mail-in ballots to registered voters, is new,” he wrote.

Circuit Judge Gabriel Sanchez wrote in a concurring opinion that the government was asking the “court to dictate to a state legislature a matter squarely within the state’s traditional domain, upending the state’s policies in this regard and inviting disorder and confusion for voters and election officials alike.”

The dissenting judge, Trump appointee Patrick Bumatay, wrote, “We should’ve granted the federal government relief — federal law precludes California from commingling ballots likely cast by aliens with legitimate ballots, because it encourages and induces illegal voting.”

#46. Judge rejects DOJ's push to force California to set aside ballots

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Updated on: October 28, 2026 / 10:39 PM EDT / CBS News

Washington — A federal judge on Wednesday rejected the Trump administration's bid to require California election officials to separate out mail ballots received from roughly 20,000 people it claims are not U.S. citizens.

The decision from U.S. District Judge David Carter is the latest setback for the Trump administration in its efforts to exert more federal control over election administration just ahead of the Nov. 3 midterm elections, when voters will decide which party controls the House and Senate.

Carter said that the Justice Department's request for California to segregate and exclude more than 20,000 ballots violates "longstanding constitutional principles" giving states the authority to administer their own elections.

"Never before in American history has the federal government, on the eve of an election, sought to coerce a state and local government to abandon their own Constitutional elections procedures and bend to the will of the Executive Branch," the judge wrote in a 26-page decision. "In bringing this lawsuit, the Executive would have this Court believe that it reigns as a sole sovereign. But California and its people are sovereign, too."

If he granted the Trump administration the relief it sought, either political party could "create chaos for their own political purpose to disrupt the election process," Carter wrote.

"Over 20,000 registered Californians would potentially lose the right that generations of Americans have fought to preserve," he said.

Carter echoed concerns raised by lawyers for California and Los Angeles County about the accuracy of the list of people that the Justice Department said are not U.S. citizens, writing that its protocol for verifying citizenship "raises significant concerns."

The judge called the Trump administration's methodology for creating the list "vague" and said he "cannot conclude that plaintiffs' process is credible."

Carter also warned that requiring California election officials to set aside mail ballots received from those alleged noncitizens would likely lead to disenfranchisement.

"Constitutional rights and our democracy are at stake. The public interest favors their protection," he said. "Individuals who have met the requirements of California's voter registration laws must not be deprived of a core right merely because of unfounded allegations stemming from a procedure rife with uncertainty and errors."

The Justice Department plans to appeal Carter's decision and will seek an emergency order from the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit.

"Every day, more ballots are processed by election officials. This ongoing emergency disenfranchises American citizens through dilution," Bill Essayli, the top federal prosecutor in Los Angeles, said in a statement.

During a hearing a day earlier, Carter appeared skeptical of the government's arguments that thousands of ballots should be set aside and not counted while election workers verify they were cast by U.S. citizens.

"I think there's always been an encouragement of people's right to vote, we the people, because when we exercise our right to vote, we exercise equality," Carter said.

The judge pressed the Justice Department as to why, rather than risk disenfranchising lawful voters, prosecutors can't instead pursue indictments after the election of those they believe voted illegally. He noted the Justice Department can rely on the envelopes from returned ballots, which have identifying information, to show a noncitizen voted in violation of federal and state law.

"The remedy as a good prosecutor is to make sure these people are prosecuted," he told Justice Department lawyers.

Carter said the Justice Department's request to separate out ballots from people the government believes are noncitizens until their citizenship can be verified would be a "monumental change" to election procedures while voting is taking place.

The decision from Carter, appointed by former President Bill Clinton, came after the Justice Department went to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit on Friday and asked that court to require California election workers to set apart ballots from voters on a government list of 20,000 people it claims are noncitizens.

But the appeals court ruled 2-1 on Monday to dismiss the appeal, finding that the Trump administration failed to give the district judge "adequate opportunity" to consider whether to grant temporary relief.

The Justice Department filed its lawsuit against California, its secretary of state Shirley Weber and Los Angeles County registrar-recorder and county clerk Dean Logan earlier this month after it sent them letters claiming that roughly 20,000 suspected noncitizens were registered to vote and had received mail ballots. There are more than 23 million registered voters in California.

The government gave the elections officials 48 hours to review its list of people it said are ineligible to vote and asked California to ensure ballots received from them are not tabulated until their citizenship can be confirmed.

Shortly after California officials raised concerns with the accuracy of the list and the Justice Department's demand, the government sued and asked the court to issue a temporary order requiring state election workers to sequester ballots from the suspected noncitizen voters.

The Justice Department has argued that California's refusal to segregate the ballots risks diluting Americans' votes. Additionally, because California is potentially commingling lawful ballots with those cast by alleged noncitizens, the state is thwarting Congress's efforts to protect election integrity, Bill Essayli, the top federal prosecutor in Los Angeles, said during a hearing.

Federal and California law prohibit noncitizens from voting.

"There's an interest in the American public having trust and confidence in their elections," Essayli said.

He stressed that the Trump administration is not asking to void or cancel any ballots.

But California officials have denounced the Justice Department's lawsuit as a "reckless political stunt" designed to cast doubt on its election results.

Anne Bellows, a deputy attorney general for California, told the court during the hearing that the relief sought by the Trump administration — an order prohibiting California from tabulating ballots from the voters it identified as "likely" noncitizens — would be "incredibly disruptive" for election officials and a "recipe for disenfranchisement."

Lawyers for the state also said the Trump administration has not been transparent about how or when it received California's publicly available voter registration list, raising concerns the government used outdated information to come up with its list of the 20,000 alleged noncitizens on the state's voter rolls. The Trump administration said that it "enhances" that voter data using a third-party service.

Lawyers for Los Angeles County, meanwhile, warned that it would be "impossible" for election workers there to segregate ballots returned by alleged noncitizens by the end of the election period, since they would have to review ballot envelopes by hand to identify those received from voters on the Trump administration's list.

And in court filings, Los Angeles County argued the Justice Department's data is "unreliable." Of the 20,000 records on the government's list, only just over 7,000 had addresses in Los Angeles County, lawyers said.

Additionally, a review of a sample of 200 records from the list found that more than half corresponded to voter registrations that were canceled or inactive, so no ballot was mailed. For the sample of 200 voters who reported their birthplace, just over 38% indicated they were born in the U.S., Los Angeles County officials said.

But an official with U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services said in a declaration filed with the district court that the agency conducted its own review of immigration records for the 200 people in the county's sample and determined none were born in the U.S.

The official, Nicolas Bartell, separately acknowledged that as part of an "ongoing" analysis of voter data, USCIS identified roughly 189 records "with minor data discrepancies" that were on the list of 20,000 registered voters sent to California.

Voting in California has been underway since early October, when mail ballots were sent out to registered voters. In Los Angeles County, more than 550,000 ballots have already been processed, lawyers for Logan, the registrar-recorder and county clerk, said. More than 3.5 million mail ballots have been returned statewide.

President Trump has for months claimed that Democratic-led states had voters on their rolls who are not U.S. citizens, though instances of noncitizen voting are rare. The president's claims are part of a broader effort by his administration to assert more control over federal elections.

Beyond the effort in California, federal prosecutors in Washington state asked election officials there to set aside ballots received from more than 400 people they allege are not U.S. citizens and warned legal action could follow. The Trump administration has also filed a lawsuit against Alaska election officials seeking to block certification of its Senate results if the incumbent, GOP Sen. Dan Sullivan, loses.
