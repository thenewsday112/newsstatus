#582. Emacs Turns 50

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

How the MIT AI Lab’s text editor gave birth to the free software movement.

Programming used to be a very different experience from the one we know today. In fact, the first programmers didn’t write code at all; the six women who worked on the ENIAC, one of the world’s first computers, at the University of Pennsylvania in 1946 did it by physically configuring switches, cables, and plugboards.

A few years later, MIT engineers programmed the Whirlwind by punching code onto paper tape that was then loaded into the computer’s memory—making it among the first “stored-program” machines. Later, they and others moved on to punch cards, one for each line of code.

By the early 1960s, programmers with interactive computers could use a “line editor” to type their code on teletypes—large, noisy keyboard-and-printer machines. They’d type one line of code at a time, and their work would be saved on a spinning hard disk the size of a modern washing machine. But there was still no such thing as the now-familiar full-screen text editors that allow programmers to see and modify many lines at a time on a display.

One of the first such tools, EMACS, turns 50 this year, and it is still widely used.

EMACS first ran on the early video terminals at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory in 1976, but its core concepts were so powerful that it was rewritten for many operating systems in the decades that followed. Apple even distributed it with Mac OS X. Beyond that, the software-­sharing community that started around EMACS helped give rise to the modern-day free software movement and the GNU/Linux operating system.

EMACS’s roots begin with the TECO text editor (originally the “Tape Editor and Corrector”) that Dan Murphy ’65 created in 1962 for the pioneering PDP-1 computer at MIT. The program’s commands were uppercase letters: A user would type “I” to insert text, “D” to delete a character, and so on. When tape gave way as a storage medium to files on a huge shared disk, the “T” in “TECO” came to stand for “Text.” And as years passed, TECO became a full language that programmers could use to create new commands, called macros, for editing their code.

In the fall of 1971, Carl Mikkelsen ’76 created Control-R, an add-on program for TECO that took advantage of “glass teletype” terminals with video displays. Control-R immediately displayed changes to the file being edited on screen; the programmer no longer needed to reprint each line to see the changes. The drawback: The display could show only a few lines of code at a time.

“Experienced and sophisticated users found EMACS to be eminently more usable than any of the previous forms of editing.”

Then, in 1976, Richard M. Stallman, a researcher at the MIT AI Lab, saw a full-screen editor—called E—on a visit to Stanford’s AI Lab. Inspired, he returned to MIT and set out to improve Control-R. The most important change to the program’s underlying structure: Every key ran its own macro when pressed, making Control-R far easier to customize. The lab’s hackers took to Stallman’s code with gusto and started changing and adding commands.

That created an issue for Guy Steele Jr., SM ’77, PhD ’80. A graduate student in the lab, Steele frequently needed to help other lab members debug their programs. “I noticed that it was much harder for me to help someone who was using one of these packages rather than straight TECO,” he recalls. “Eventually, it occurred to me to try [to] broker consensus on a standard set of Control-R commands that everyone could agree to use.”

David Moon ’75, another member of the lab, helped out. Stallman jumped in as well, and eventually he took over. Together, the group assigned a standard set of commands that made use of the Meta key found on many of the lab’s early keyboards: Meta-F would move the cursor forward a word, Meta-B would move it back a word, and so on. They called the result EMACS, short for “Editor Macros” or “Editing Macros.” “My goal was not to construct a great new text editor,” says Steele. “I was trying to address a social problem.”

Nevertheless, EMACS was a great new text editor. Whereas Control-R could be tricky, EMACS users with “no technical experience at all” could learn to edit letters, emails, and even programs “within minutes or an hour,” according to a history by Bernard S. Greenberg, SM ’74. Meanwhile, “experienced and sophisticated users found EMACS to be eminently more usable than any of the previous forms of editing.”

Stallman started distributing EMACS to anyone who sent him a magnetic tape and a postage-paid return envelope. “EMACS does not cost anything; instead, you are joining the EMACS software-sharing commune,” he wrote in the 1981 manual. “The conditions of membership are that you must send back any improvements you make to EMACS.”

Greenberg, meanwhile, decided to write his own version for Multics, an early operating system by that time owned by his employer, Honeywell. Greenberg’s EMACS won first prize at the International Honeywell Software Conference in 1980, and Honeywell started selling it. Honeywell wasn’t alone in marketing a version of EMACS: James Arthur Gosling, a Carnegie Mellon graduate who went on to lead the development of the Java programming language, made a version that he eventually sold as a commercial product.

This transition from free to paid software ultimately spurred Stallman to launch a project to create an operating system that could be freely shared and improved by its users. He modeled this effort, called the GNU Project, after the increasingly popular Unix operating system from Bell Labs. “GNU” was short for “GNU’s Not Unix,” alluding to the fact that, unlike Unix, it would be free.

The project’s first major application was GNU Emacs (now restyled in lowercase). The GNU/Linux operating system was still years away, so, despite the project’s aim to create an entirely free operating system, GNU Emacs initally ran on Unix. It would eventually run on a range of operating systems, including Linux but also other Unix-based systems. When Apple launched Mac OS X, the first release of its Unix-based operating system, in 2001, GNU Emacs was included.

Apple stopped shipping GNU Emacs in 2019, but the company still has a program called mg (“Micro GNU Emacs”). Though it supports most EMACS commands, it lacks an integrated programming language, making it what some call an “ersatz EMACS.”

Meanwhile, much of Apple’s OS still has hidden support for EMACS commands. The keyboard has no Meta key, but Control-Option-B will move back one word, and Control-Option-F will move one forward.

#328. Serial killer Ted Bundy linked to another Utah girl's cold case murder

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Updated on: October 28, 2026 / 9:20 AM EDT / CBS/AP

DNA connects serial killer Ted Bundy to the death of a Utah teenager, authorities announced Tuesday, more than 50 years after her body was discovered by a hunter.

Bundy has been dead since 1989, but the tally of his confirmed victims continues to grow due to advances in DNA testing. He killed at least 30 women and girls in multiple states in the 1970s, and many more escaped or survived with injuries.

Investigators are satisfied that Bundy was responsible for killing 17-year-old Melissa Ann Smith in 1974, a death that had long been listed as a cold case, Summit County Attorney Margaret Olson said at a news conference.

Besides DNA evidence, Smith's death had the hallmarks of Bundy's "pattern, motive, means and opportunity," Olson said.

Investigators were able to close the cold case by making crucial DNA connections between Smith and Laura Aime, another Bundy victim whose case was resolved last April in Utah County.

A highly similar DNA profile linked to Bundy was found in evidence obtained from Smith's autopsy, Utah County Sgt. Mike Reynolds said.

"This case demonstrates the importance of preserving evidence," said Salt Lake County Sheriff Rosie Rivera.

Officials have been in touch with Smith's next of kin, none of whom still live in the area.

"With every case closure, may we stomp the memory of Ted Bundy out," Reynolds said.

Bundy was executed in 1989 by electric chair in Florida for the 1978 murders of two women at the Chi Omega sorority house at Florida State University.

Bundy killed at least 30 women and girls in multiple states in the 1970s, and many more escaped or survived with injuries.

Bundy was one of the nation's most prolific serial killers, with at least 30 women and girls' deaths linked to him in several states in the 1970s. In 2011, a vial of Bundy's blood — drawn in 1978 when Bundy was arrested in the death of a 12-year-old girl — was found in Florida. The full DNA profile was entered into the FBI's national database, giving investigators a shot at solving potential cases linked to Bundy.

His murders — which occurred in sorority houses, parks and elsewhere — set the nation on edge. Bundy's arrest drew widespread fascination, in part because many considered him to be charming and handsome.

Bundy gave a series of confessions in his final days, including to some crimes that were previously unknown to police. Not all of those cases have been confirmed.

It's not known when Bundy first began his attacks, but by 1974, young women — many of them college students — began disappearing in Washington state. Authorities were still investigating those cases when Bundy moved to Salt Lake City in September and began killing people in Utah, Idaho and Colorado.

Nancy Wilcox, a 16-year-old cheerleader in Salt Lake County, was believed to be one of Bundy's first victims in Utah, according to historian Linda Sillitoe. Wilcox disappeared on Oct. 2. High school senior Melissa Smith disappeared near the end of the same month. Deer hunters found her bludgeoned body, Sillitoe said.

#410. FCC clears Amazon Leo to acquire mobile-satellite operator Globalstar

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The deal is designed to allow Leo to to deliver direct-to-device service.

Amazon Leo's $11.5 billion acquisition of mobile-satellite operator Globalstar has been approved by the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC), the regulator announced. The deal is designed to expand Leo's space-based footprint and allow direct-to-device service for its growing satellite network, Amazon previously said.

"The companies' complementary assets promise to enhance Amazon Leo's entry into the direct-to-device segment and create a stronger satellite operator capable of offering fixed and mobile services to residential, business, and government customers," the FCC wrote in a press release that echoed Amazon's own talking points. The regulator said that the deal is likely to benefit the public by enhancing data coverage in underserved areas.

You may not know Globalstar but have possibly used its services, as the company provides Apple's emergency satellite connectivity for compatible iPhones and Watches. Interestingly, Apple also held a 20 percent stake in Globalstar prior to its takeover. As part of the deal, Leo will power satellite services for supported iPhone and Apple Watch models.

Leo, a rival to SpaceX's Starlink, has been active of late. The company recently announced that it has 400 satellites in orbit and revealed last month that it had launched high-quality satellite internet service in Uzbekistan. It's teaming up with Delta to eventually bring satellite internet to flights, as well. Amazon recently added six new Arianespace launches and aims to have more than 3,000 low-earth orbit satellites up in the near future, still well short of SpaceX's 11,000 satellites.

#460. Amazon Kindle Paperwhite (2026) and Colorsoft (2026) Review: Best for Accessories

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It's a good day to be a Kindle fan. Amazon announced the arrival of a new generation of devices earlier this month, and the new Kindle Paperwhite and Kindle Colorsoft are available today. A question now presents itself: Should you upgrade?

The most intriguing reason to upgrade isn't the devices themselves—the Paperwhite and Colorsoft both have base plastic versions with 16 GB of storage and aluminum-backed Signature Editions with 32 GB of storage and extra features—but the little extras could go a long way for certain readers. Namely, the ability to add page-turn buttons with the new Kindle Page Turn Cover ($80)—which is compatible only with the new Signature Editions of the Paperwhite and Colorsoft.

Color also plays a big role in the Signature Editions compared to the new base models. Not a color screen, to be clear—that's still Colorsoft only—but the color of the device itself, which Amazon has said was a huge driver for Kindle users to upgrade to a new model after the brand released colors like Matcha and Raspberry in 2024. The 2026 line has one color for each of these 7-inch e-readers that's only available with the Signature Editions.

It's worth noting that while the price of these new models is the same as the 2024 editions, it's because Kindle raised the prices of its 2024 versions back in August. At first, it seemed like this was intended to make Amazon sales look better than they actually were heading into Prime Day and Black Friday, but now it's clear it was probably a way to make these new models feel worth buying, since you won't find a cheaper model as easily. And it's a valid move: These models will be my new picks, because if you're spending the same amount, why wouldn't you want the latest model?

But that doesn't necessarily help you decide if it's worth upgrading. It might be worth it if you're desperate for page-turner buttons, which is easily my favorite part of this new line of Kindles. If you don't have a Kindle already and are on a mission to select your first one, this latest version of the Paperwhite remains a good choice unless you're determined to splurge on a color screen. The warm light is a big benefit for anyone who reads in the evening, and you'll have to choose one of these two models from Kindle's lineup to get it anyway.

The 2026 Kindle Paperwhite (left) and 2026 Kindle Colorsoft (right).

The Kindle Paperwhite and Kindle Colorsoft are, to some degree, the same device. You'll see a similar laundry list of features: a 7-inch screen, a front light with adjustable brightness and warmth, and 16 GB of storage in the base models. Both have the same new look of a flush-front screen and ever-so-slightly-smaller body when compared to the 2024 Paperwhite and 2025 Colorsoft (the first Colorsoft in 2024 was later renamed the Signature Edition, and a cheaper base model came out in 2025).

Kindle Paperwhite (2026, 13th Gen) and Kindle Colorsoft (2026, 2nd Gen)

Amazon touts these devices as being thinner and lighter than the previous models, but the difference is minimal. And most of the specs have stayed the same: You still get up to 12 weeks of battery life on the Paperwhite and eight on the Colorsoft, according to Amazon. The resolution is still 300 pixels per inch (ppi) for black-and-white content and 150 ppi for color, and both remain waterproof. The screens are obviously different, with the Colorsoft using a color display that has a slight blue tinge to it when compared side by side with the new Paperwhite models at the same brightness level, with the warm-light settings turned off.

Kindle Paperwhite (2026, 13th Gen) and Kindle Colorsoft (2026, 2nd Gen)

The 2024 Kindle Paperwhite (in raspberry) and 2026 Kindle Paperwhite (black) side by side.

The 2024 (left) and 2026 Kindle Paperwhites, stacked. The 2024 model looks thinner due to the rounded edge, but the 2026 model is actually slightly slimmer.

The biggest change is in weight, which is still minimal—both 2026 versions now weigh 7.2 ounces, while the older Paperwhite was 7.4 ounces and the Colorsoft was 7.6 ounces. You can feel the difference, even if it is slight. But it's not so much lighter that you should switch to this model if you have wrist problems or mobility issues; if this is you, I'd recommend getting the new Kindle Click ($35) page-turner remote (which also launched today) and a stand of some kind. This Bluetooth remote will pair with Kindles from 2024 onward to turn pages for you without needing a secondary device to sit on the Kindle itself, which is what you've needed up to now.

Performance-wise, I found the experience to be very similar across both models, and similar to my older devices as well. Both the 2026 Paperwhite and Colorsoft are snappy with opening books and turning pages, and I was impressed by how well each model could quickly recall where I'd left off on another device; something my past Kindles never seemed to do. Compared with my 2024 Kindle Paperwhite, I didn't see a direct speed improvement in opening my books like the new Kindle Aluminum had over the 2024 Kindle.

Kindle Paperwhite (2026, 13th Gen) and Kindle Colorsoft (2026, 2nd Gen)

The 2026 Kindle Colorsoft and 2024 Kindle Colorsoft.

When I put the new Colorsoft side by side with the old Colorsoft, I did see a slightly more yellow undertone to the base screen, but otherwise the colors looked identical to my naked eye. I didn't expect to see a huge change here; E Ink did just announce a new version of its color e-paper screen, but I don't expect to see it arrive on devices until next year at the earliest. The performance didn't seem to change between the first and second generations, either.

These new models of the Colorsoft and Paperwhite alike also promise two AI-based features. One is available now, and Kindle calls it Story So Far, but you'll find it in your Kindle's menus as “Recap.” (It can also sometimes pop up when you open a book, asking if you'd like a recap before you start reading.) Kindle will analyze how far you've read into the story and give you a spoiler-free summary based on your spot in the book. There's also a feature called Ask This Book that will let you ask questions about the story (and also promises to be spoiler-free), but Amazon only says this is coming soon. I don't love AI features for my books and can't see myself using this, but maybe you're reading a ton of books at once and need reminders of what's going on in each story.

Kindle Paperwhite (2026, 13th Gen) and Kindle Colorsoft (2026, 2nd Gen)

The 2026 Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition in Seaglass Green.

The back of the 2026 Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition and the new pogo pins.

The Signature Editions have always had a little more to offer than the base models, and while these new ones cost the same as the previous models, the 2026 Signature Edition versions of the Paperwhite and Colorsoft come with a couple of other extras. I tested just the Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition, but besides the color screen and battery life, the rest of the benefits remain the same across both models.

Kindle Paperwhite (2026, 13th Gen) and Kindle Colorsoft (2026, 2nd Gen)

The previous Signature Editions of both the Kindle Paperwhite and Colorsoft already offered auto-adjustment of their front lights, wireless charging, and more storage, and that remains true with the 2026 version. But the 2026 Signature Editions also pack a new, aluminum-backed frame that gives them a more premium feel and shiny look, plus pogo pins on the back to pair them with new Kindle accessories. That aluminum form makes it heavier, weighing 8.1 ounces instead of 7.2 ounces. You can feel the difference, but the taller form factor makes the weight feel better distributed than on the shorter (though lighter) Kindle Aluminum.

Colorful finishes are essentially locked into the Signature Editions only now with these new models, at least if you want one of the 7-inch ones. While the base, 6-inch plastic Kindle has a purple color option, the base Kindle Paperwhite and Kindle Colorsoft will only come in black. The Signature Editions are where you'll find the fun colors: I tested the Paperwhite Signature Edition’s Seaglass Green color, while the Colorsoft Signature has a deep-purple Fig option. I am disappointed it's only one color per model, given how expensive these are and the fact that the Aluminum version has two colors.

But the biggest appeal to me for choosing a Signature Edition isn't the color but the compatibility with a new accessory made by Amazon for these new Kindles: the Kindle Page Turn Cover ($80).

Amazon used to make an e-reader with page-turn buttons, the Kindle Oasis, but hasn’t for years. It’s a feature we’ve been waiting to see come back, and instead of baking it into one of its e-readers, Amazon has chosen to make it an add-on. Amazon says it's a vocal minority of about 5 percent of Kindle users who want page-turn buttons, and I think this is a smart move to make it an option for those who want it without risking a potentially unpopular device.

The cover works well, easily popping onto my Paperwhite Signature Edition and quickly working to turn the pages. You can use it to move up and down on pages, too, but the experience feels backward—the higher-up forward button will move you down a page, when visually you expect it to move you upward. It's a lightweight, sleek cover otherwise, and feels identical to the new slim covers Amazon has out for its aluminum models. It’s sleek and discreet, and the buttons are in the perfect spot to start using them as soon as I open the book. I love that you don’t need to worry about anything besides popping the case on.

The downside is the cost. The Page Turn Cover will add another $80 to your purchase after already needing to upgrade to a Signature Edition, which costs $40 to $50 more than the base models. The cheapest combination is with the Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition, but you'll end up paying over $300, which is how much you'd spend to get the Kobo Libra Colour (my favorite e-reader that isn't a Kindle) with page-turn buttons built in and a stylus to have it double as a digital notebook. So while this Signature/Page Turn Cover combo works well, the price hurts when you look at the competition. Plus, if you aren’t picky about your page-turning accessory, the Bluetooth Kindle Click ($35) remote is significantly cheaper and works with older models.

It's hard for me to say you need any of these devices if you're already a Kindle user. The older models are already disappearing from Amazon’s website, though, so if you’re in the market for your first Kindle, I’d still recommend choosing a Paperwhite, and you might as well choose the newest model since the older one isn’t any cheaper.

A group of investors led by Nelson Peltz's Trian Fund Management has offered $1.4 billion to buy Wendy's, according to a regulatory filing Wednesday.

The group has offered $7.35 per share in cash to take the burger chain private, paying a 20% premium to Tuesday's closing price. In addition to Trian, it includes investment firms BlueFive Capital and Sixth Street Partners, plus Flynn Group, one of Wendy's largest franchisees.

Wendy's shares spiked about 15% in morning trading following the offer. The Wall Street Journal first reported Peltz's proposal.

Wendy's did not immediately respond to a request for comment from CNBC. Trian declined to comment beyond the filing.

"In our view, a take private makes sense as Wendy's turnaround is likely to be prolonged given the challenged state of the lower income consumer and soft state of the quick service burger category," TD Cowen analyst Andrew Charles wrote in a note to clients on Wednesday.

In May, former Potbelly CEO Bob Wright joined Wendy's as its latest chief executive, after his former employer went private. In his first earnings conference call with analysts in August, he was candid about what he views as the chain's biggest issues, like muddled value offerings, inconsistent execution at its restaurants and too many one-off promotions.

Trian previously explored a takeover of Wendy's in 2022, but later decided against it. The firm and affiliated parties own a 16.25% stake in Wendy's, according to the regulatory filing, making them the company's largest shareholder.

#430. Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition (2026) review: Updated in all the right places

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Are you willing to pay $50 more for the upgrades?

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

There's no denying the chokehold Amazon has on the ereader industry. Its library is massive and its entire lineup of Kindles are consistently reliable devices that meet readers with varying budgets and needs. As part of the company's 2026 lineup, the Kindle Paperwhite Signature Edition ($250) sits squarely in the middle in terms of both capabilities and price. Amazon saw fit to refine an already compelling mix of design and performance, with key changes to how you use this Paperwhite as well. But as we noted with the 2024 Signature Edition, the best premium ereader may not offer any exclusive features that you can't live without.

Amazon didn't try to reinvent the wheel with its updates to the more premium Paperwhite. There's now an aluminum back, but overall, it's still an IPX8 waterproof design with a high-contrast, 300 dpi display (more on the screen changes later) and 32GB of storage. The company did color match the front bezels on the Seaglass Green version so it matches the back panel. This is certainly a welcome change as it definitely looks better than previous models that had colorful cases but with gray fronts. The 2026 model is also one millimeter thinner than its predecessor — a variation you're unlikely to notice, but one Amazon will gladly tout.

Amazon also moved the power button to the top right edge, a location that will be more familiar for anyone who uses a smartphone. I honestly prefer it there, because even though I've been using a regular Kindle Paperwhite since 2024, I never got used to the power button on the bottom edge. Now, the USB-C port is the only component that still resides there.

Perhaps the biggest change on the 2026 Paperwhite Signature Edition is the introduction of pogo pins on the back cover. Amazon ditched the 2024 model's standard wireless charging (via Qi-compatible and Amazon accessories) in favor of this arrangement. The four pins accommodate new accessories, most notably the Page Turn Cover ($80) with physical page-turn buttons, and there's also a $60 charging stand with an unknown shipping date. I should note that both of these accessories also work with the Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition, which has the requisite pogo pins onboard as well.

In my opinion, the Paperwhite line offers the best reading experience on a Kindle. I don't need a color screen, but I do prefer the adjustable warm light and larger display over the standard Kindle. Of course, the IPX8 waterproofing is also nice to have, as I like to take the Paperwhite to the beach, and you can certainly feel safe using it during a bath or at the pool.

To further enhance the overall experience, Amazon employs an oxide-thin film transistor backplane and offers a contrast ratio that it says is its highest yet. The company promises deeper blacks and crisper text on the 2026 Signature Edition compared to the previous version, although my eyes are unable to discern the difference in either of those. I couldn't distinguish between the sharpness on my 2024 Paperwhite and this new Signature model either, but I could tell them apart by the slightly warmer overall glow of the latest release, even when the Warmth slider on both Paperwhites was set to 0. Still, the Paperwhite display continues to offer the best ereader performance among the company's lineup for my money.

The auto-adjusting front light feature returns, using an ambient sensor to increase the brightness in daylight or dim it at night without you having to do so yourself. Of course, you can disable this feature and swipe the slider as you see fit. There's also a new double tap gesture that allows you to turn pages by double tapping on the left and right sides. Two taps on the right edge to go to the next page, or two taps on the left to go back. I didn't use this very much, probably because I'm conditioned to just tap once on the sides of the display to turn pages (which still works here). I'll also mention there's an accelerometer in this 2026 version for automatic screen rotation, but I don't have much use for it, so I left rotation locked during the bulk of my testing.

One area I wasn't able to test in the two weeks I've been using the new Paperwhite Signature Edition is battery life. Amazon says you can expect up to 12 weeks of battery life on a charge, and with past Kindle devices, I've left them unused for months and come back to enough juice for multiple reading sessions. #467. Amazon Adds an Alexa Photo Frame and a Bedside Clock to Its Echo Family

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Panos Panay, Amazon’s senior vice president of Devices and Alexa, says a couple hundred million people now use Alexa+, the revamped version of the decade-old voice assistant, on its Echo devices. He says people talk to Alexa+ two to three times more than before its AI glow-up, cook with it five times more, listen to music three times more, and create routines twice as much.

To keep that momentum going, Amazon has new Echo products to deliver a richer Alexa+ experience, including minor refreshes on existing hardware and entirely new categories. Announced Wednesday and available for purchase now, they include the Echo Dot ($100), Echo Studio With Home Camera ($250), Echo Show 16 ($500), Echo Time ($200), and the first Alexa Photo Frame ($200).

“I don’t think anyone’s really doing AI at all in the home, and if they are, they aren’t doing it well,” Panay says at an intimate media-only event in New York City. As people have “deeper” conversations with Alexa+, Panay says devices need to be in premium shape—a strategy Amazon has been focusing on after announcing more premium Kindles and Alexa Tablets in early October.

You can see that premiumization in the hardware pricing: The Echo Dot—once the affordable, entry-level $50 smart speaker—now starts at $100. That’s higher than the marked-up prices on existing Amazon devices from this summer, which the company blamed on the memory shortage.

The new Echo Dot costs $100 and comes in four colors.

Panay says Amazon has sold more than 100 million Echo Dots, and in 2026 alone, people spent more than a trillion minutes listening to music on the diminutive orb. So, the main goal for the new Dot? Boost the sound. The full-range driver reportedly delivers 50 percent stronger bass performance and crisper vocals.

With four beamforming microphones (up from three) and Amazon’s AZ3 chip, Alexa can isolate your voice in a noisy environment and track your commands more effectively. It also has Omnisense like last year’s Echo Dot Max, which can sense the environment to, say, detect when you walk into the home and turn on the lights.

The Dot now supports the Matter standard and works as a Thread border router, turning it into a hub for setting up and controlling smart home devices. If you have an Eero router, it can also turn into a Wi-Fi extender.

It comes in four colors, three of which are calming pastels. Amazon’s design head Chris Green tells WIRED that’s intentional, to evoke a calm, softer aesthetic, citing recent data showing nearly half of US adults are stressed once a week. Alternatively, you can buy the Kids Echo Dot, which has the same hardware but in fun designs, plus Amazon Kids+ for age-appropriate content.

Amazon’s senior vice president of Devices and Alexa, Panos Panay, video calls his dad with the camera in the new Echo Studio at an intimate media briefing in New York City.

The new Echo Studio is the first Amazon smart speaker with a camera—a feature once limited to Amazon’s Echo Show smart displays—allowing Alexa to identify the person making the command (along with voice recognition) for more personalized responses. That’s especially important these days as Alexa+ can connect to email or calendar apps and needs to verify who’s speaking before divulging personal information.

It also lets you use Live View to check in on your home and monitor pets, or video call friends and family. With no display on the Echo Studio, you’ll need to pair it with a Fire TV or use your smartphone to view the camera feed of the person you’re calling—as seen in the picture above when Panay used the Studio to Drop In on his dad. This “Drop In” feature will arrive later through a software update.

The camera enables what Amazon calls “Advanced Room Adaptation,” which tunes the sound to the room. Even if you move around the space, the volume will stay consistent. And it can also be used for visual intelligence—just like how you might use your phone to identify an object with Google Lens, you can pick up an object and show it to Alexa through the camera, and it can identify it and answer questions. For example, show a board game you’ve decided to play and ask Alexa to explain the rules.

While no privacy shutter physically blocks the camera, you can press a button to turn off the mics and camera, and an LED lights up when the camera is on and in use. If you don’t want yet another camera in your home, Amazon says you can buy the standard Echo Studio. The speaker is otherwise very similar to the 2025 model and is meant to be the top-end option for folks who want even better audio quality.

Digital photo frames are a well-established category now, with companies like Aura and Skylight leading the market. Now Amazon’s entering the fray with the Alexa Photo Frame. Yes, you can still talk to Alexa just like any other Echo device, but the 10-inch display will always show your photos—nothing else. The bottom edge lights up when Alexa activates, and you can ask it the weather or to show you pictures of your dog last Halloween.

The caveat is that you need to use Amazon Photos. Once your images are in Amazon Photos, you can choose who and what appears on the frame—even newly captured photos so Mom can see the latest pics from your vacation before she even asks. The display is a semi-matte, anti-glare touchscreen that adjusts brightness based on the room’s ambient light, and you can swipe the screen to see more images. Tap an image to play a voice note tied to the picture—that’s something you can add in the Amazon Photos app, but a future update will let users add voice notes to images straight on the Alexa Photo Frame.

Smart curation happens in the background to choose the best photos. For example, if you have a lot of vertical images, it will stack them side by side and group familiar faces together. Maybe that means a pic of your kid when they were 8 years old, and the pic on the right of when they’re 14. (Though Google has been doing this for years on its smart displays.) Alexa knows to choose pictures of people smiling, and understands timing—you’ll be served last year’s Halloween pictures around this time of year.

Amazon says it’s easy to set up for someone else—like your grandparents—and the frame comes in three styles: black, birch, or walnut.

The other entirely new product is the Echo Time. It’s similar in size and sound to the Echo Dot, but with a digital clock screen. No cameras here—it’s designed for the bedside table, after all—and the 3.2-inch circular display can dim as low as 0.3 nits (the same as Apple’s new HomeView).

It’s hardly the first time Amazon has dabbled with clocks on an Echo (see the Echo Spot), and Amazon even sold a bedside sleep tracker before discontinuing it barely a year later.

The Echo Time doesn’t track your sleep, but it can help you start a bedtime routine as you wind down. The embedded ultrasonic transducer can detect your breathing to identify when you’re in a light sleep stage and gently wake you up within 10 to 60 minutes of your alarm, rather than forcing you out of deep sleep. These “Smart Alarms” are increasingly common on fitness trackers, like Google’s Fitbit Air, but the pitch here is that you don’t need a gadget on your wrist. Sadly, these features work only for the person closest to the Echo Time; with two people in bed, you’ll need two Echo Time units—one on each nightstand.

You can choose from hundreds of clock faces, set up alarms with Alexa+, and ask the assistant to remind you about certain things when it wakes you up—like grabbing lunch from the fridge before heading out. Alexa can play soothing soundscapes to lull you to sleep like a sound machine. And when you wake up, you can tap the top of the device to snooze it (up to four times).

There’s a Kids Echo Time with wacky designs and more limited features constrained to Amazon’s Kids+ service. Kids can set their own alarms, ask for the weather forecast, and customize clock faces, but the main highlight is Sleep Studio. This connects to content from apps like Calm, Headspace, and Moshi to help kids wind down and fall asleep.

Finally, there’s the Echo Show 16. Yes, it’s a bigger smart display, but Panay says Amazon has redesigned the entire experience from the ground up. This specific model is designed to be wall-mounted, though you can buy a kickstand if you’d prefer. It starts at $500, but Amazon has introductory pricing of $400; the version with the Nanomatte anti-glare display (like the one on the Alexa Tablet Pro 12) costs $550.

It has slimmer bezels, a thinner frame, and an entirely new audio system with eight woofers and two tweeters. The 13-megapixel camera can supposedly identify people from up to 17 feet away, so you won’t have to walk really close to the screen to see your personalized information. It also supports night vision, so you can use Live View to check in on your home and still see in the dark.

A full built-in Fire TV experience lets you watch as much content as you want while cooking, or follow along with recipes with Alexa. One big focus of the redesign was letting users prioritize the features they use the most. Want to use it as a smart home controller? There’s a dashboard that shows all your controls as your primary “Screen Style.” Parents who prefer to use it as a calendar can set the “Family Organizer” screen as the main display. Or just let it turn into a digital photo frame with the Photos Screen Style.

More unique is the AI-generated interface Amazon is debuting with Alexa+ called “Neo.” Instead of a basic chat transcript like most other chatbots, Alexa+ will create charts, generate images, and offer other visuals to zhuzh up its responses during more complex conversations. (That’s something Google and OpenAI have also played up with recent updates to Search and ChatGPT.) These “Cards" will also appear in your Alexa app so you can revisit them at any time.

A Reuters report earlier this year revealed details that Amazon was working on a smartphone, codenamed Transformer, with Alexa+ as the focus of the experience and a generative user interface. “Neo” on the Echo Show 16 gives us a first glimpse of what that might look like, and paired with many of the Alexa+ features introduced on the recent Alexa Tablet Pro 12, it feels like the Amazon smartphone is closer than ever.

I asked Panay point-blank whether Amazon was making a phone, and while he said there was “no intent," he pointed out that all of the new software experiences are “scalable.” He then asked to look at the iPhone Duo I was holding and remarked that it was similar in size to the Surface Duo, which Panay helmed at Microsoft. There may be “no intent” on paper, but it sure seems like he wants to make a phone again.
