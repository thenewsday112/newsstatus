#94. CEO says plan is to file for bankruptcy after ending luxury never-ending cruise

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

October 28, 2026 / 8:31 PM EDT / CBS News

More than 200 passengers, mostly Americans, are stranded in Cambodia after the operator of a luxury U.S. cruise ship that was on a continuous around-the-world voyage ran out of money due to skyrocketing fuel costs.

Villa Vie Residences, the Florida-based operator of the MV Odyssey, said the war with Iran quadrupled fuel costs and forced it to suspend operations on Tuesday.

CEO Mikael Petterson told CBS News in a phone interview Wednesday that Villa Vie Residences was paying an average of $700 a ton for fuel before the war broke out in late February. Since then, it has been paying anywhere from $2,000 to $3,000 per ton.

The Odyssey uses between 700 and 800 tons per month in fuel, Petterson disclosed.

"When you divide it over 300 people on board, it's just backbreaking," Petterson told CBS News. "And this has been going on for nine months. We've tried to do everything we can do."

In a statement, Villa Vie Residences said the per cabin fuel costs rose from an average of $1,800 per month before the war to between $6,000 and $8,000.

The company was unwilling to pass those rising costs on to its passengers, Petterson said.

The Odyssey launched in October 2024 for a continuous around-the-world journey. At the time, passengers could become residents of the ship by purchasing a permanent cabin for several hundred thousand dollars. Some sold their homes to do so. At the time, they were given a 15-year minimum guarantee of residency aboard the Odyssey.

The Odyssey attempted to counter the spiking fuel prices by slowing down its itinerary and spending five or six days in each port "hoping that things would come to a close in the Middle East," Petterson said, but it wasn't enough.

"For us, when our monthly expenses go up from $400,000 to $2 million, where are we going to get the $1.6 million?" Petterson asked.

The Villa Vie Residences CEO said that the ship ran out of money as it searched for a financial partner, and the plan is now to file for Chapter 11 bankruptcy.

"We've borrowed money, we've stretched our payrolls as far as we can, and we're at a point where it's just at a breaking point where we need capitalization," Petterson said.

Passenger Benjamin Schatz thought the MV Odyssey would be his forever home.

"Honestly, right now, I'm in denial and in shock, and I'm heartbroken," Schatz, a retiree, told CBS News.

CBS News met Schatz in April 2025 for a story featuring Villa Vie Residences. At that point, he had been living on the ship for about one year. On Wednesday, he was given just hours to pack up his life and leave.

Said Schatz: "There are people now who are homeless and who have very little money and don't know what they're going to do."

#37. Dolores Huerta, civil rights icon, dies at 96

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Huerta co-founded the United Farm Workers.

Labor icon Dolores Huerta, who co-founded the United Farm Workers, has died at 96, her family said.

"We are deeply saddened to announce that our beloved mother, Dolores Huerta, has passed away today at the age of 96," her family said in a statement on her official Instagram account. "As you can imagine, this is an incredibly difficult time for us, and we ask for privacy as we grieve this incredible loss to our family. We know that many of you around the world share in our mourning, and we will be following up on plans to recognize and celebrate her incredible life and legacy."

A spokesperson for Huerta's family confirmed her cause of death was cancer.

Huerta co-founded the National Farm Workers Association (NFWA) with fellow activists Cesar Chavez and Gilbert Padilla, which would eventually become the United Farm Workers of America. The union's work led to the first farmworker union contracts in U.S. history.

The union highlighted Huerta's coining of the popular labor motto "Si, Se Puede" ("Yes, we can") and her advocacy for farmworkers, women, immigrants, the LGBTQ+ community and "all marginalized people."

"Her life's work was to empower her community to face and overcome injustices, and she has left farm workers and the entire Latino community stronger and more organized than she found it," said UFW President Teresa Romero.

Huerta was born on April 10, 1930, in Dawson, a small mining town in northern New Mexico to Mexican parents involved in union activism. She grew up in Stockton, California, and became active in community and labor organizations as an adult.

Huerta became a teacher in rural California in the 1950s and said life wasn’t always easy for Mexican-Americans, witnessing mistreatment, extreme poverty and starving children.

"We still have that racism that exists that I had to confront when I was a teenager growing up," Huerta told "Good Morning America" in 2020.

She began seeing how horrendous life was for farm workers in California.

"They were only earning like 50 cents an hour," Huerta said. "They had no unemployment insurance, farmworkers, because they migrated from, you know, from county to county. They weren't even able to have any kind of access to the food banks ... no bathrooms in the field, especially for the women. That was really horrible."

She said she knew it was her calling to fight discrimination and help improve the lives of farmworkers. First, she joined a grassroots group called the Community Service Organization. That’s when she met Chavez and her mentor, Fred Ross Sr., who she calls the godfather of the farmworkers' movement.

She founded the Agricultural Workers Association in 1960 before joining Chavez and Padilla to form the NFWA two years later.

Huerta played a pivotal role in the Delano grape strike of 1965 and led the subsequent national boycott of table grapes, which successfully pressured growers to improve wages and working conditions.

Huerta founded the Dolores Huerta Foundation in 2002, a civic advocacy organization supporting marginalized, low-income and immigrant communities

She was also a strong advocate for women, campaigning for reproductive rights and serving as an honorary co-chair of the 2017 Women's March in Washington, D.C.

In 2012, she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom for her decades of advocacy in civil rights and women's equality.

In 2026, Huerta spoke exclusively to ABC's John Quiñones in response to a New York Times article detailing allegations that Chavez abused women and minors. Chavez died in 1993.

Huerta said that she was "manipulated and pressured into having sex" with Chavez She detailed two alleged separate sexual encounters with him in the 1960s -- one in which she said she was pressured to have sex with him, and another in which she said she was forced against her will.

"And to think that somebody that we looked at as our hero and our leader -- you know, it's pretty horrible," Huerta said.

Huerta said both alleged encounters she had with Chavez led to pregnancies that she kept secret, later arranging for the children to be raised by other families.

"I thought that abortion was a sin," Huerta told Quiñones. "I have since changed my mind on those issues because I now realize that women have to have a right to abortion. And by the way, abortion was also illegal at that time."

She said that one of the children was raised by her brother and the other was raised by a family friend.

Huerta said at the time that she remained committed to her work, focusing on current threats to labor rights and the treatment of immigrants in detention centers.

"My intention is just to do the work, make lives better for women, make lives better for working people," Huerta said. "We know that the job isn't finished yet."

"At my age, 96, as long as God gives me strength and the little energy that I have left, I want to just continue doing the work to make life better for women, for children, and of course, for farmworkers and workers in general," she said.

#444. Senators Demand to Know if Dr. Oz Collaborated With Election Denial Groups

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

With a week to go before the midterms, a group of Democratic senators are demanding that Mehmet Oz, the administrator for the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS), share communications his department may have had with far-right groups promoting a baseless conspiracy theory about left-wing groups stealing votes from residents of nursing homes.

In July, Oz’s agency issued a memo rescinding previous guidance on how staff at nursing homes could help residents to vote by assisting with mail-in voting or transporting residents to polling locations. The memo replaced that guidance with threats of prosecution if staff “engage in inappropriate coercion or other violations of residents’ rights.” Referring to this memo, election denial groups last month began to boost claims that voter fraud was happening inside care facilities.

That campaign was spearheaded by the Election Integrity Network, a group led by Cleta Mitchell, a former adviser to president Donald Trump. “These left-wing groups go into the nursing homes, and they go into the group homes, and they basically steal the votes of these most vulnerable,” Mitchell said in a recent appearance on Steve Bannon’s War Room show.

Now, citing WIRED’s reporting on the nursing home conspiracy theories, a group of senators that includes Elizabeth Warren, Richard Blumenthal, Alex Padilla, Mazie Hirono, Ron Wyden, Kirsten Gillibrand, Jeff Merkley, and Cory Booker wrote in a letter to CMS to ”urgently request that CMS rescind its July 2026 memorandum and reinstate its 2020 and 2024 nursing home voting guidance.”

“This is as low as it gets,” Warren tells WIRED. “The Trump administration’s unfounded allegations of election fraud are inspiring election denial groups to harass nursing home residents and staff. With less than a week until Election Day, every lawmaker in America should be standing up for fair elections and pushing back on this attack on seniors’ voting rights.”

#420. Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition 2026 review: Nice, but doesn't justify the price

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

$320 is a lot to pay for a well-balanced device in an aluminum case with pogo pins.

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

I'll be honest. I don't have much to say about the new Kindle Colorsoft. It doesn't feel like that long ago that Amazon introduced its first-ever color ereader, but that was in October 2024. At the time, the product was novel for the company, as it presented a technical challenge. How was Amazon going to offer a paper–like color reading experience while preserving the quality of a black-and-white ereader? The company used nitride LEDs and custom algorithms to finetune how lights were beamed through individual pixels to maintain contrast levels, and also made sure that pages turned faster than previous devices. The original Colorsoft only came in one color, which felt like a staid container for its relatively vibrant contents.

Cut to October 2026 and Amazon has a new Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition for the world. It's a bit thinner than before, with edge-to-edge color and a new color option: Fig. With this variant, the Colorsoft's front bezels also match the rest of the case, so you won't see unsightly white or black bars like on previous models. This Kindle Colorsoft generation starts at $290, but if you pay $30 more, you can get the Signature Edition, which is what I'm reviewing here.

Everything else about the Kindle Colorsoft is basically the same as its predecessor. You're still getting a screen with 300 pixels per inch (ppi) for black-and-white reading, or 150 ppi for color. Nothing has changed about the color representation here – you still get the "vibrant" mode in addition to the standard rendering to get a bit more saturation out of your pages.

I'm not a huge graphic novel reader, and most of the pops of color in my Kindle Colorsoft experience are in the book covers in my Library or when I highlight particular sentences or passages. They were pleasant little touches, but for me, the novelty of paperlike color E Ink displays has largely worn off. If I'm not reading something where the colors are actually integral to the storytelling, I don't need an ereader to deliver a broad spectrum of shades.

All that is to say – the reading experience on the Kindle Colorsoft this year is largely the same as last year, minus the early troubles our reviewer (and some other people online) encountered with yellow bands on their devices. Since Amazon resolved to fix the issue, it should not impact current and new Colorsoft models, and I did not notice this during my testing. I didn't experience any obvious issues when reading on the Colorsoft either.

Pricing is a bit confusing to compare, so bear with me. At launch in 2024, Amazon only made a "Signature Edition" of the Colorsoft available, and it started at $280. This year, the Colorsoft starts at $290 for the base configuration with 16GB of RAM and goes up to $320. That additional spend will get you the Signature Edition's aluminum case, pogo pins on the back, twice the storage and the choice of black or maroon. So you can say the Colorsoft costs between $10 and $40 more than before, and for that money you're mostly getting a slightly more refined flush-front design and thinner chassis.

In real life, much as I appreciated the aesthetic tweak, it didn't have a huge impact on my reading experience. I never struggled to hold the previous Colorsoft (or many other recent Kindles) so the thinner body, while nice, wasn't a huge change. The new Fig finish was a nearly identical match for my Burgundy iPhone 18 Pro, though, so if you love matching your gadgets to each other, this might be worth considering.

More importantly, I liked the sturdiness and premium vibe of the aluminum housing. My Kindles live in my cluttered purses, and their ability to withstand scratches is crucial. In my two weeks with the new Kindle Colorsoft, I've thrown it in my backpack with no regard for its safety, and it's still managed to remain pristine. I did decide to put a case on it midway through my testing so as not to tempt the review Gods, though, to ensure I could get a good photo of it before my recklessness marred it forever.

I haven't had a chance to connect the Colorsoft to any new accessory that's compatible with its new pogo pins in the real world yet. But I can at least say that based on a short demo ahead of Amazon's launch, I enjoyed how easy it was to snap the Signature Edition Colorsoft onto a magnetic case and use page turning buttons on a cover that connected via the pogo pins. I'll need more time to judge the responsiveness, consistency and ease of use, but for now, color me intrigued. I do wish Amazon hadn't removed the ability to wirelessly charge the Colorsoft. It's so handy to just drop the tablet onto a charging stand, and those who have already bought those accessories might be miffed at the omission.

That said, I never worried about charging the Kindle Colorsoft, because like most other Kindles, its battery lasts forever. I mean quite genuinely, because every time I pick up one of the many abandoned Kindles lying around in my house, they always still have juice left. I was slightly concerned when, after a day or two of focused reading, the Colorsoft's battery dipped by 10 percent to 90 percent. But then I remembered I had the same experience with the larger, more energy intensive Kindle Scribe Colorsoft. And that thing remains alive on my couch without me even thinking about plugging it in.

I won't say that I've had enough time to properly evaluate the Kindle Colorsoft's battery performance, but suffice it to say that nothing in my two weeks with it has been a real cause for concern so far.

Like I said, I don't read a lot of stuff that's better in color. But I can tell you that the Kindle Colorsoft is not your only option out there – Signature Edition or no Signature Edition. Our favorite ereader overall is the Kobo Clara Colour, which costs just $150. It has a slightly smaller 6-inch screen, so if that's too small and you have more money to spare, the Kobo Libra Colour is also a solid offering. It has a 7-inch screen that's a respectable color E Ink display, and costs $260 on Kobo's website. As a bonus, it has physical page-turn buttons onboard so you won't have to spend even more money on a case for that feature. However, it does only come in black or white options. At that price, the Kobo Libra Colour comes in at $30 under the Kindle Colorsoft's starting fee, and still manages to offer 32GB of storage and stylus support.

The Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition feels like an unnecessary splurge for me. But for anyone who regularly reads color ebooks, the Colorsoft might be a treat. Plus, if you are also a fan of the Fig color option, are locked into the Amazon ecosystem and might want to use your Kindle Colorsoft with the page-turn cover one day, the Signature Edition is a capable ereader that won't disappoint.
