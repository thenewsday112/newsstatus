#73. Death of US actress Hayden Panettiere ruled accidental, ending local police probe

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Police in South Carolina, where Hayden Panettiere died in August, say they have concluded their investigation into her death

The death of US actress Hayden Panettiere has been ruled accidental, ending a local probe into her death, police in South Carolina have said.

The Heroes and Nashville star died in August from the toxic effects of fentanyl, Xanax and several other drugs in her system, at the age of 36.

Citing the medical examiner's report last month that found Panettiere died from a drug overdose, the Greenville Police Department said it had "concluded its investigation" into her death.

The US Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) reportedly joined a local police probe. It is unclear if that investigation has also concluded. The BBC has contacted the DEA for comment.

"Based on the Medical Examiner's determination and the findings of the investigation, Hayden Panettiere's death has been ruled accidental," police said in a statement.

Authorities, including the DEA, were reportedly investigating the circumstances of Panettiere's death, according to the BBC's US media partner CBS News.

Taking on Kindle is hard. Beating Goodreads might be really easy.﻿

Taking on Kindle is hard. Beating Goodreads might be really easy.﻿

For the last several years, Readwise has been building tools for power readers. There’s the eponymous product, Readwise, which stores and categorizes your Kindle highlights to help you remember what you’ve read. There’s Readwise Reader, an app for reading newsletters, PDFs, and web articles you’ve saved for later. And now there’s Readwise Books, a full-fledged platform for buying, reading, and talking about books.

“Strava for reading” wasn’t the original goal of Readwise Books, which has been part of the Readwise plan since the beginning. “Literally, Dan and I talked about this when we first met,” Homsi says. “We feel like Kindle could be so much better. We really didn’t think it would take nine-plus years to be able to get to this point.” But it turns out, Doyon adds, that for all their ideas about innovation in books, “no one sits around wanting a better Kindle.” But there is one thing they do wish for: a better Goodreads. “Everybody hates Goodreads.”

In a sense, then, the plan for Readwise Books is to take on Kindle by first taking on Goodreads. The Readwise Books app offers a nice way to track books you want to read, are reading, and have read; it even syncs with your Kindle account to let you track books you’ve bought from Amazon by taking pictures of your starting and ending pages. You can rate and review books and see what others have said. It’s a little Strava, a little Letterboxd, and a lot nicer to look at than Goodreads.

The long-term goal, of course, is to get people to stop buying Kindle books and start buying from Readwise. Homsi and Doyon have spent the last several years working to reach deals with all five of the major US publishers in order to have a competitive library of books available. That meant endless negotiations, security reviews, DRM debates, and more — both cofounders seem exhausted just talking about the process. But eventually, by several small miracles, they got the deals done. And since those deals govern the price of books, and many things about how they’re sold and presented, they bring Readwise to a nearly even playing field almost immediately.

It all took a lot longer than expected, but Doyon and Homsi think their timing might actually be pretty good. Digital book reading continues to grow, particularly among young people; a huge amount of that reading happens on phones and tablets. Meanwhile, a new crop of E Ink devices like the tiny Xteink and the Boox Palma line have expanded the universe of available hardware for a company like Readwise.

Not long ago, Doyon says, they would have had no choice but to build an e-reader of their own. (And he says they still might.) Now, they can just make sure it runs on an E Ink-friendly flavor of Android.

After a couple of days using Readwise Books, I can say confidently I already like it better than practically all of its competition. It’s a much nicer-looking app than anything you’ll get from Kindle or Kobo; it has lots of ways to customize how books look; and at least for now, I’m finding the tracking features compel me to read more instead of annoying me. (Homsi says some early versions of the gamification features were substantially more annoying.) I’ve found a few bugs, and the half-integration with Kindle is a little confusing in spots, but it’s already a really nice place to read.

Of course, I, like millions of other people, already own a Kindle and lots of Kindle books. Switching ebook platforms is hard, and Doyon says he’s not counting on everyone to do it right away. He says he’s hopeful Readwise can persuade existing users to switch, in order to sync their notes and progress more seamlessly. He’s also hoping that the advent of AI, and the ways in which AI reliance may rot our brains, will get a lot of people back into the idea of reading more deeply again. And that a bunch of those people might be looking for a nicer, more social place to do so.

#168. Albertsons won't insure employees' spouses if they're eligible for their own health coverage

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Albertsons is curbing health care benefits for employees due to rising healthcare costs.

Starting next year, the grocery giant will no longer offer medical insurance plans to employees' spouses and domestic partners who have jobs that provide such coverage. The grocery giant detailed the change in an open enrollment guide posted to its website.

Albertsons said it expects its healthcare costs to increase 9.5% next year due to inflation, higher prescription drug costs, provider consolidation, and shortages in the healthcare workforce.

"Like employers across the country, Albertsons Companies continues to navigate rising health care costs while working to provide associates with access to quality, competitive benefits," a spokesperson said in a statement to Business Insider.

The spokesperson also said spouses and partners who don't have access to employer-based coverage will remain eligible for coverage on applicable health plans.

Other changes this year include higher weekly paycheck contributions from employees, the elimination of two network plans, along with higher deductibles and out-of-pocket maximums for some other plans.

Large companies Disney, Starbucks, and Deloitte have also introduced cost-saving changes to their benefits packages for 2027. Disney is also dropping coverage for partners and spouses who can get insurance from their own jobs.

"We've seen employers reducing their contribution toward the spouse's coverage, but not eliminating the coverage option for those people," Capitol Benefits CEO Joshua Lavine told Business Insider in August, regarding Disney's decision.

#225. Federal Reserve holds interest rates steady as midterm elections loom

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Fed raised interest rates a quarter of a percentage point last month.

The Federal Reserve held interest rates steady on Wednesday as the economy faces a monthslong surge of inflation ahead of the midterm elections.

The Fed's benchmark interest rate currently stands between 3.75% and 4%. That level marks a significant drop from a recent peak attained in 2023, but rates stand well above a 0% level established at the outset of the COVID-19 pandemic.

An acceleration of price increases this year has posed a challenge for Fed officials tasked with containing inflation. The central bank raised interest rates a quarter of a percentage point last month in an effort to battle back price woes. The move marked the first rate increase in three years.

That rate hike should help cool off inflation by slowing the economy and eating away at demand, though such a policy move typically yields results after a lag time lasting several months.

Prices increased 3.7% in September compared to a year earlier, putting the inflation rate more than a percentage point above the Fed's target level of 2%, data showed.

Speaking at a press conference in Washington, D.C., on Wednesday, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said policymakers had opted to leave rates unchanged as they await further economic data.

"The committee believes the accumulation of more information will help it determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the 2% goal," Warsh said.

Warsh voiced optimism about U.S. economic performance, even as he acknowledged the challenge posed by elevated inflation.

"Economic activity is expanding at a strong pace. While uncertainty remains, owing in part to geopolitical developments, domestic spending has been resilient," Warsh said.

Policymakers may lose patience as they weigh whether the current level of interest rates is high enough to ease price hikes. The odds of a quarter-point rate hike at the Fed's next meeting in December are pegged at nearly 80%, according to CME Group's FedWatch Tool, a measure of market sentiment.

In the meantime, price increases are accelerating. Inflation rose to a four-month high in September as fuel prices climbed in response to unrest in the Middle East, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS).

The rise of inflation may put pressure on the Fed to ratchet up interest rates in an escalation of its fight against price hikes. That policy would mean pressing the brakes harder on the economy -- risking sluggish growth and job cuts.

Warsh also announced plans for the Fed to issue just seven rate decisions in 2027 compared to eight this year.

In 2028, the Fed plans to reduce the number of rate decisions to six, Warsh said.

"Getting policy right involves assessing near-term developments at timely, regular and information-rich intervals. But it’s equally important we reserve time and space as an intellectually diverse group for strategic deliberations," he added.

A weak jobs report this month complicated the economic picture, suggesting a resilient labor market may be wobbling more than previously thought.

Employers added 29,000 jobs in September, a sharp slowdown from the 162,000 jobs added in August, the BLS said. The job gains came in well below economists' expectations.

Economic conditions pose a quandary for the Fed, Bank of America Global Research said in a memo to clients last week.

"If the Fed hikes, investors might worry about overtightening. If the Fed holds, markets could question the Fed's inflation-fighting credibility," the memo said.

The rate decision may also thrust the central bank into a political maelstrom in the final sprint to Election Day.

President Donald Trump has repeatedly criticized the Fed for raising interest rates over across both terms in office, including a reprimand leveled at the Fed soon after its rate increase last month.

"LOWER THE INTEREST RATES FOR THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA, AND FAST!" Trump said in a social media post hours after the announcement.

The Fed is an independent institution established by Congress. The central bank is typically insulated from political interference, but several past presidents have sought to influence the Fed’s interest rate policy, including Trump.

The rate decision may also draw attention to the challenge posed by inflation, a top issue among voters.

In a memo to clients before the rate announcement, Deutsche Bank Research said the upcoming midterms could factor into a potential decision to hold interest rates steady, though in their view the election would not be a "primary consideration."

"We could imagine that all else equal the Fed might prefer to stay out of the headlines for raising rates just ahead of a midterm election where affordability is the principal concern," the memo said.

#76. Trump administration wants to remove regulations on a super planet-heating gas

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Flares burn off methane and other hydrocarbons at an oil and gas facility in Lenorah, Texas, Oct. 15, 2021. David Goldman/AP hide caption

The Trump administration is proposing to dramatically scale back a Biden-era rule that aimed to cut methane emissions from the oil and gas industry at drilling sites nationwide.

Methane is a major contributor to climate change and is estimated to be 80 times more planet-warming than carbon dioxide in a twenty-year time frame. In 2023, the Biden administration finalized rules that were meant to rein in methane emissions from the oil and gas sector.

President Trump made the announcement while campaigning in Lubbock, Texas, Tuesday, saying his administration's revision, under the Clean Air Act, would save the industry billions in production costs and spur more domestic drilling. The proposal comes as Republicans face a backlash from voters over high gas prices heading into the midterms.

"Behind every barrel of oil and every cubic foot of natural gas produced in the U.S. are millions of workers, businesses, and the heartbeat of this nation's economy. The Biden EPA destructively targeted all this," EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin said in a press release.

The oil and gas industry, which vigorously opposed the 2023 rules, said EPA's proposed rollback will address some of the challenges companies have faced in trying to comply with the regulations.

"We look forward to working with the Trump administration on a more workable approach that supports American energy production, provides greater regulatory certainty and advances continued emissions reductions," Dustin Meyer, vice president at the American Petroleum Institute, a trade organization for the U.S. oil and gas industry, said in a statement.

But environmentalists say this may end up actually costing the industry more.

"Methane is natural gas, or natural gas is methane," says Jon Goldstein, associate vice president at the Environmental Defense Fund. "And so if you're capturing more natural gas, you're not allowing it to leak out, you're not allowing it to be flared, you have more product that you can sell. And so that offsets a lot of the costs."

"That is harmful to our energy security, and it's harmful to taxpayers that ought to be seeing royalty and tax revenue collected off of this gas that's going to waste," Goldstein adds.

One of the regulations the EPA wants to cut is the "Methane Super Emitter Program." Across the U.S., oil and gas pipelines, refineries, and other energy facilities sometimes have huge leaks of methane. But methane is invisible and difficult, not to mention expensive, to detect. The super emitter program is supposed to take effect in January 2027. It would allow the government to delegate monitoring for big methane leaks to certified third parties, including private and nonprofit methane satellite programs.

"The easiest way to think about it is that if the government is alerted to a big leak, then if it's reported by certified third parties, then they could use that information," says Paasha Mahdavi, a political science professor at UC Santa Barbara who studies the oil industry.

"[The government] needs help from other people to say, 'Hey! You're breaking the law!' so they're able to prosecute," Mahdavi adds.

If that program goes away, Mahdavi says it will be harder for the government to spot large leaks of the planet-heating gas from oil and gas facilities. "And we need that because it's really hard to see this stuff," he says.

The EPA's announcement would also loosen regulations on methane leaks coming from low-volume "marginal" oil wells that are considered "marginally economic," meaning they don't make much money.

"People assume marginal well must equal small emissions," Goldstein says. But he says satellites and new technology find that's not true. "These old sort of end-of-life wells nearing the end of their production life are actually responsible for the lion's share of methane pollution from the [oil and gas] production sector," Goldstein says.

The EPA plans to hold at least a 45-day public comment period, including a public hearing, once the proposed rule changes are published in the Federal Register.

#260. CoreWeave and other neocloud stocks drop after Microsoft capex comes in below expectations

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Shares of neoclouds like CoreWeave and Nebius dropped in extended trading as Microsoft's lower-than-expected capital expenditures figure points to infrastructure partners taking longer to deliver data center capacity.

Microsoft on Wednesday reported $45.2 billion worth of capex and finance leases during the quarter, up about 30% from a year earlier, but below the $50.7 billion average analyst estimate, according to StreetAccount.

Microsoft is particularly reliant on neoclouds for capacity as it pours money into its artificial intelligence infrastructure. Microsoft inked a nearly $20 billion deal with Nebius at the tail end of last year for compute through the 2030s. It's also a top customer for CoreWeave.

Those companies and their peers have experienced a rocky few weeks on the market as rising interest rates led to concerns that future debt raises will be too expensive and lenders will start retreating.

In its earnings report, Microsoft said Azure revenue jumped 48% from a year earlier, far outpacing the increase in capex.

"Growth was higher than capital expenditures driven by timing differences between receipt of goods and payment and a lower mix of finance leases," Microsoft said its earnings deck.

CoreWeave and Nebius slid almost 5% after Microsoft's report. IREN fell about 3%.

Alphabet on Wednesday also reported capex numbers that fell short of estimates. The Google parent's capex totaled $51.36 billion, while analysts expected $55.09 billion.

SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, whose company rents AI infrastructure to Anthropic and Google, predicted over the weekend that more than 200 neocloud companies will collapse, posting on X that "debt markets have almost entirely shut off access to money for any company that doesn't have substantial revenue independent of AI data centers."

And last Monday, analysts at Gartner shared with CNBC its prediction that, by 2030, 80% of neocloud companies will either be absorbed or completely cease operations.

After a steep plunge this month, the neoclouds had been enjoying a modest recovery this week, with CoreWeave up 18% in the past three trading days to pace the gains. Analysts at Wolfe Research wrote in a report on Tuesday that they see the stock rebounding into the mid-$70s. It closed Wednesday at $63.93.

The infrastructure story gained some strength earlier on Wednesday as Oracle issued a strong long-term revenue forecast and said it will return to being free cash flow positive in the second half of next fiscal year. The stock jumped 11% in regular trading before retreating 3% following Microsoft's report.

#371. A Health Update

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I'm Craig Hockenberry and this is where I write for the web. I make apps and run websites.

I’m writing this post from a hospital bed. And I couldn’t be happier.

You hear folks say chemotherapy sucks, but until you go through it yourself, you don’t really know how bad it can be. It remains a popular activity only because it’s better than the alternative.

My first exposure to chemotherapy was with this contraption: a fluoroscope.

Every week, they used this machine to tap into my spine, extract 5 mg of fluid for analysis, and replace it with 5 mg of methotrexate. There was only a local anesthetic during the procedure and the hardest part was knowing that there was a needle, turned up to eleven, going into my back.

It was afterwards that things got dicey: the nerves in your spine float in a thecal sac filled with spinal fluid that acts as a kind of insulator. Since we were exchanging the fluids, it was easy for the levels to get out of balance and for the nerves to react.

The result were the worst headaches I’ve ever experienced. The good news is that the remedy was simple: to lie flat on my back to ease the pressure gravity was putting on my spine.

That was also the bad news: many days were spent lying prone and trying to avoid boredom. Watching shows and scrolling timelines just doesn’t cut it when that’s all you can do.

Thankfully, the spinal headaches subsided quickly when the six-week course of lumbar punctures finished at the end of January. But my body began to face a new challenge: chemotherapy infusions of Bendamustine and Rituximab.

Everyone experiences chemotherapy differently. Just as every body is different, so is the reaction to the chemicals that are killing the cancer.

The first effect I noticed was in my tastebuds: everything I put in my mouth had a slightly metalic taste, so my eating habits changed. My beloved morning coffee was replaced by tea, food needed a lot less salt, and I started eating a blander diet (cottage cheese with berries remains a favorite).

Some people experience nausea and other gastric issues: luckily, I avoided that. I also didn’t have any hair loss, but my normally thick and curly hair lost its body and became lifeless: an external expression of what was happening to me internally.

As someone who’s always been active and strong, the biggest overall effect of chemo was losing muscle mass and suffering from overall fatigue. It felt very strange to be weak.

I was on a 28 day cycle for my infusions: during the first week I got pumped full of chemicals and then spent three weeks letting my body process them. One of the things they gave me with the infusion on a Tuesday was a dose of steroids that gives the body a boost during the initial onslaught. But by the weekend, that wore off and I felt like shit. It usually took until the middle of the second week before I started feeling human again.

I didn’t lose my appetite, but I wasn’t eating more than normal. The problem was my metabolism was working overtime and my calorie intake didn’t adjust. I lost 50 pounds over the course of a couple of months and eventually got to the point where I was having five meals a day. Too bad it all tasted bad.

One thing that I did to fight my diminished capacity was a daily walk. Anyone who exercises regularly knows that there are always plenty of reasons to avoid it: you didn’t sleep well, you have an important project to finish, or you’re sore from a previous workout. When you’re doing chemo, add feeling like absolute shit to that list.

I still had nerve damage in my spine, so my “walk” was more like a shuffle. Thankfully my wife found some awesome walking sticks to aid with my balance. They are clearly labelled “Not A Medical Device” but they are my most important medical device.

My daily exercise was just like any gym workout: dread at the start, joy at the end. Sometimes there wasn’t much behind that joy: at my lowest point, a walk around the block on flat ground got my heart rate up to 160 bpm. Breathtaking, literally.

That low point was in March. In the week following my infusion, where I was already feeling like crap, I came down with a type of pneumonia that affects people with weakened immune systems. I had no strength and my blood oxygen level plummeted below 90%. Getting from the living room to the kitchen for a drink of water felt like running a marathon, and then my Apple Watch pinged to inform me that I was having an episode of atrial fibrillation. It was a lot and I honestly had thoughts of “is this how it ends?”

Thankfully, antibiotics battled the pneumonia quickly and the chemo effects subsided. My doctor offered to skip the next cycle of chemotherapy, but I decided to push on. There is only one way to be done with a thing.

Throughout all of the infusions, the nerve damage that occurred before the lymphoma discovery didn’t improve. As I said above, my gait and balance were severely compromised: going up and down stairs was particularly challenging. The tingling sensations on my backside and numbness near my abdomen became a bigger concern with each passing month. It wasn’t getting better and I feared it was a permanent condition.

I finished the last of my infusions in May and my body slowly started to recover. Food started tasting better, and my strength and stamina started to return. My daily walks got longer: first a quarter of a mile, then half a mile, then a mile, and then something fantastic happened.

Something was changing in my spine: and after a couple of weeks it was clear that this was a sign that the nerves were fixing themselves.

Our guess is that the chemotherapy infusions, which attack white blood cells and other parts of the blood that aid in recovery, had retarded the healing in my nervous system. Now that my body had a chance to repair itself, it was.

I can now go up and down stairs without hanging on for dear life. I’m able to walk without aid, but I still like my walking sticks for longer outings since they let me push myself harder. A group of Democratic candidates on the ballot next week are hoping to start a new conversation around guns as part of a strategy to win back rural voters in red-leaning areas.

Wisconsin’s 1st District: Democrat Mitchell Berman’s ads have portrayed him as a proud gun owner and hunter. His race against GOP Rep. Bryan Steil was not expected to be close. President Donald Trump carried the district in 2020 and 2024, and Steil won re-election two years ago by 10 points. But polling has shown the race is within reach for Democrats.

Texas’ 15th District: Bobby Pulido, a Tejano singer-turned-Democratic congressional candidate, is not shy about his love for guns and hunting. Pulido is also running in an area that Trump won in the last two presidential elections. The district was redrawn earlier this year to make it more favorable for Republicans, but a shift in the Latino vote away from Trump mixed with a struggling economy has put Republican Rep. Monica De La Cruz at risk of losing her bid for re-election.

On the campaign trail, President Donald Trump has been confidently promising that oil prices will drop like a rock after the midterms — especially skyrocketing diesel fuel critical in farm states that could decide which party wins the Senate. Implicit is that he will attempt to do something decisive after next week to end the war against Iran and fully reopen the Strait of Hormuz.

His not-so-subtle hints are backed up by new reporting in The Wall Street Journal and other publications that the U.S. has told Gulf partners it will resume intense, brief strikes against Iran after the midterms. And Israel’s hawkish Trump ally Benjamin Netanyahu has at least temporarily retained a caretaker role as prime minister while he and his rival, former Army Chief of Staff Gadi Eisenkot, jockey for a majority. In Israel’s complex parliamentary system, a decisive outcome could take weeks or months, or even lead to another election.

That places Netanyahu in control of a potential resumption of the war, a position he had advocated for before Israelis went to the polls, according to The New York Times and other outlets. According to those reports, Israel’s military and intelligence leaders effectively vetoed that option, saying it would politicize their decision-making.

As flawed as Israel’s democracy has been in many respects, according to many of its own citizens and even many longtime U.S. supporters, it still has the virtue of a largely independent military. But in the U.S., critics fear that may not be the case with the civilian leadership of the military in the second Trump administration.

In past administrations in both political parties, secretaries of defense and their top deputies were usually experienced foreign policy or former military leaders, willing to offer independent opinions to the presidents they served in a formalized National Security Council interagency system. Conflicting options were debated in a deliberative process before they were presented to the commander in chief.

Yes, on occasion, chief executives were misled by false narratives, like those delivered during the Vietnam War by Defense Secretary Robert McNamara. Or, during the Iraq quagmire, when the chain of command was short-circuited by a powerful personality like President George W. Bush’s defense secretary, Donald Rumsfeld.
