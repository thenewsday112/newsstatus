#389. ‘The Mosquito Bowl’ Review: Football Heroes Take to the Battlefield in Peter Berg’s Emptily Macho WWII Epic

This pummelling Netflix production seeks pathos amid the gunfire as it tells the true story of four college football stars joining the Marines in the wake of Pearl Harbor, but not one of these noble warriors feels like a real person.

No one familiar with the oeuvre of Peter Berg — the action merchant behind the Afghanistan war drama “Lone Survivor” and the improbably pumped-up boardgame adaptation “Battleship” — should approach “The Mosquito Bowl” expecting great restraint or good taste. Ostensibly, Berg’s Netflix-backed combat epic is fighting the same fight as Terrence Malick’s “The Thin Red Line,” likewise charting the physical and psychological torment of American soldiers in Guadalcanal during the Pacific Theater of the Second World War. But they are two very different history lessons. There’s not a hint of Malick’s poetic anti-war melancholy in Berg’s brawny, cacophonous Semper Fi salute to American military brotherhood, which certainly doesn’t stray from standard war-is-hell sentiment — rarely, in fact, has war been depicted as more loudly ruinous — but positively revels in onscreen carnage, as if to underline the mettle of its Marine heroes. Not that said mettle is in any danger of going unnoticed. For the four men at the pretty hollow center of “The Mosquito Bowl” aren’t just soldiers but sportsmen — stars of the college football circuit who bring their strength and smarts from the field to the battlefield. And if that comparison sounds glib to you, then buckle up, because the film has plenty more: “The Marine Corps is the greatest offensive line in the history of football,” says one character, a sentiment that sounds sort of rousing without making much sense. That goes for much of the script by Berg and Mark L. Smith, which is drawn from a popular 2022 historical novel by Buzz Bissinger — the journalist whose 1990 bestseller “Friday Night Lights” became the basis of Berg’s sturdy 2004 football drama and the more popular TV series spun off from it.

Berg and Bissinger are cousins; the screen adaptation of “The Mosquito Bowl” was likely a fait accompli all along. Yet while the book’s ennobling mixture of Greatest Generation portraiture and brutal evocation of the rigors of combat is perfectly tailored to the filmmaker’s interests, it also hands the screenwriters a bit of a structural red herring. The title refers to a football game that the principals were tasked with organizing on Guadalcanal; it was played on Christmas Eve 1944 between the 4th and 29th regiments of the 6th Marine Division, and featured a wealth of drafted football talents who had set aside their sporting careers to serve their country. Outwardly, the book and film both seek to imbue this event with the significance of the famed Christmas truce soccer matches between British and German forces in the no-man’s-land of the First World War.

When push comes to shove, however, the game simply isn’t all that consequential. It’s skipped over surprisingly swiftly on the page, and despite much anticipatory buildup, the film, too, struggles to make a climactic set-piece of an event that has considerably less visceral impact than the various juddering, blood-on-the-lens battle scenes on either side of it. Nor does it work as a human interlude, because the human dimension of “The Mosquito Bowl” is so flimsy. Its four principals are colorless, interchangeable alpha males, uniformly stout of build and square of jaw; they’re granted mildly differing character attributes by the script, but even as the closing credits roll, you’d be hard pressed to remember who was who.

John McLaughry (Nicholas Galitzine), a graduate (and future football coach) of Brown University, is given the leading perspective — which is to say he occupies the framing scenes that have him visiting a college football field years after the war with a stoic, misty gaze, and reflecting vaguely on all that has been won and lost. We rewind to 1941, when the earnest young man’s budding future with the New York Giants is put on ice by a surge of patriotic duty in the wake of Pearl Harbor; the same goes for jocular Purdue grad Tony Butkovich (Ray Nicholson, appropriating his father Jack’s rakish perma-grin), soulfully withdrawn Wisconsin Badgers star Dave Schreiner (stage star Tom Francis, a Tony nominee for “Sunset Blvd.”), and fellow Wisconsin grad Bob Baumann (Bill Skarsgård), whose personality lands somewhere between those last two — the writers having run out of all-American archetypes quite early on.

Historical dates are lightly fudged to give the four men a shared soldierly narrative, and so they collectively endure the grueling trials of Parris Island basic training, horrible but familiar from many a military drama, before shipping out to the Pacific, where the shattering, deafening mayhem that awaits them is horrible but familiar from many a war movie. Knowing that he has nothing new to show us, Berg’s solution is to show us ever more of the same, ladling on the frank images of death and devastating injury with relentless rat-a-tat-tat sound design to the point that the film does attain a blunt sensory force.

The aesthetic, with DP Jacques Jouffret’s aggressively desaturated palette and nauseous POV shooting coupled with antsy, abrasive editing, is ripped from “Saving Private Ryan,” but cranked up to video game levels, with a grinding, guitar-heavy score by post-rock band Explosions in the Sky; the film’s technical proficiency so overwhelms its slender character-building that it begins to feel almost heartless.

The actors, all talented, are given no scope to be any more than capable. There’s scant room for inner life here, nor for reflection on anything more specific than war’s general cruelty — though, needless to say, the enemy gets even shorter shrift. There’s a jingoistic undertow to “The Mosquito Bowl,” with its flag-waving celebration of football and Marine fortitude, that sits queasily with decontextualized shots of Japanese soldiers committing ritual seppuku or being summarily executed by American captors, or the main characters’ repeated gung-ho statements about “killing the Japanese.” The film strains to say something about the morally corrupting effects of warfare, but doesn’t challenge its heroic leads on the racist invective they parrot, or circle back to any regret they may feel deeper into their service. Trivial and shoehorned in as it seems, when the titular football game rolls along, you can practically feel the film’s sigh of relief: at last, a blessed few minutes of macho combat without consequences — or politics.

#105. Experts react to new findings on CTE in former NFL players

Updated on: October 28, 2026 / 8:27 PM EDT / CBS News

A new study from Boston University estimating that nearly 75% of NFL players who died over a 15-year period had signs of CTE in their brains is raising concern, and experts say it demonstrates the need to collect more data on the condition.

CTE, or chronic traumatic encephalopathy, is a degenerative brain disease found in people who have suffered repeated blows to the head. It can only be definitively diagnosed after death. It has also been found in the brains of other athletes, first responders and military members, but is rarely seen in the general population.

The Boston University research builds on data published in The British Medical Journal in August. That data estimated that anywhere from 25% to 98% of ex-NFL players who died between 2016 and 2021 had CTE. The broad estimate reflected uncertainty about how representative brain donors studied were of the overall NFL population, since brain donors are more likely to have symptoms believed to be related to CTE.

The Boston researchers looked for signs of the condition in brains donated by 387 players who died between 2008 and 2023 — a subset of the 2,362 former NFL players who died during that period. Ninety-four percent of the donors had some form of CTE. The researchers estimated that former NFL players with cognitive symptoms were 5 times more likely to have their brains donated to science than others, and estimated from there that 74.5% of players had some form of CTE. The research is published in JAMA Network Open.

CBS News chief medical correspondent Dr. Jon LaPook cautioned that that figure may be an overestimate. He noted that the research looked at how many former players had signs of CTE in their brain — not how many showed actual symptoms of CTE.

Dr. Kristen Dams-O'Connor, director of the Brain Injury Research Center at Mount Sinai, said the results of the paper "cannot and should not be interpreted as being representative of current living athletes." She pointed out that of those whose brains were studied, "many ... ended their careers long ago." In recent years, the NFL has implemented rule changes that are designed to reduce concussions on the field, and introduced more protective equipment, including Guardian Caps.

"This should translate to lower CTE risk in recent generations of players, compared to those who played decades ago," Dams-O'Connor said.

The NFL told CBS News in a statement that it "continuously strives to make the game of football safer, including by implementing strategies to reduce concussions and head impacts."

Dams-O'Connor said that while Boston University used "strong methods" to adjust for selection bias in the data, there are still other gaps, such as accounting for the approximately 20,000 NFL players who could have died during the study's 15-year timeframe but did not. She also said the results could not be applied to the "vast majority of people who played youth football" or other non-professional contact sports.

"It's a helpful study in trying to look at the bigger picture, recognizing that those who donate their brain are more likely to have cognitive symptoms, and often are more likely to have had changes," said Dr. Jeremy Tanner, assistant professor of neurology at The Glenn Biggs Institute for Alzheimer's and Neurological Diseases at the UT San Antonio Health Science Center. "Even taking that selection bias into account, when you look more broadly, it's at a minimum 20 to 25%, which is eye-opening and really shows the need to both advance the research."

Dr. Jay Alberts, director of the Cleveland Clinic Concussion Center, said the data shows the need to look for early warning signs of CTE.

"We often think about neurological disease, including CTE, as having a silent phase. These things are changing, and we can't detect them," Alberts said. "But I don't think it's silent, per se. I think what we need to do is listen more carefully and use better measurements to understand what those changes are."

Those measurements could include studying a person's "instrumental activities of daily living," or IADLs, Alberts said. That includes skills like driving a car or balancing a checkbook, where a person must use both cognitive function and motor function simultaneously. In patients with Alzheimer's or Parkinson's disease, IADLs start to change between five and seven years before a diagnosis is made. He said CTE may be similar.

"Those types of activities are really insights into brain function, and I would argue those are things that would help us to listen more effectively," he said.

Understanding how a person's brain function may change during the early stages of CTE could make it easier to diagnose, Alberts said. That could also lead to interventions that might "slow it or even potentially reverse it."

Alberts and Tanner also highlighted research focused on determining if there are biomarker changes that could be connected to CTE. Ongoing studies, including one that has a study site at UT Health San Antonio, follow current or former players throughout their life to see if there are any abnormal changes to the body or brain over the years. Alberts called for even more long-term research, and Tanner said that such studies can benefit more than just former NFL players.

"This has really been a rapid area of scientific advancement over the last 10 to 20 years, and has led to breakthroughs in the new therapies for Alzheimer's disease, and hopefully (will) for many other neurodegenerative diseases like CTE, Lewy body disease, and others in the years to come," Tanner said.

"Hopefully we can develop tools and biomarkers to detect these brain changes during life, which will enable us to study these diseases during life, and in the best case, learn ways to better prevent or treat these diseases."
