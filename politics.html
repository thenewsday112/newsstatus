#8. A top general pushed out at Pentagon's Joint Staff

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia is the latest senior military officer to be sidelined.

A top general at the Pentagon was suddenly removed from his senior post earlier this week and has now retired, according to two sources familiar with the situation.

Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia served in the Joint Staff as the Joint Staff's Director for Strategy, Plans & Policy, an important office, that handles long-term strategic planning and is known in military jargon as the J-5.

Sylvia was removed from the position for a lack of confidence, according to one of the sources.  With his removal Sylvia retired from the U.S. Army after having served in the role for 11 months.

Sylvia becomes the latest senior military officer to be either fired or sidelined during Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's tenure.  However, one source said it was unclear whether Sylvia's removal was ordered by Hegseth or Gen. Dan Caine, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff.

"The Chairman, the Joint Staff, and the Joint Force extend our deepest gratitude to Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia and his family for their 32 years of distinguished service to our nation," said a Joint Staff spokesperson.

"We wish him and his family the best as they graduate to new opportunities and the next chapters of their lives," the spokesman added.

Earlier this week, Sylvia was listed as still being the director of the planning office on the Joint Staff's website, but the website now lists only the name of Vice Director Maj. Gen. Mark H. Slocum.

The J-5 is recognized as one of the Joint Staff's most important directorates in the Joint Staff along with the directorate for Operations, the J-3, that is responsible for the planning and execution of military operations.

Last year, two prominent Army generals serving on the Joint Staff were sidelined and had to retire early, including Sylvia's immediate predecessor as the J-5, Lt. Gen. Joseph "JP" McGee, who retired last October.

McGee had been nominated under the Biden administration to become the director of the Joint Staff, but he was not renominated by the Trump administration.  With no other slots available to him as a three or four star general he had to retire.

Also retiring early last year was Lt. Gen. Douglas Sims, the director of the Joint Staff, who was widely expected to be on track for a fourth star.

#85. California judge denies Trump DOJ request to segregate alleged noncitizen ballots

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

A federal judge in California on Wednesday refused the Trump administration’s request to segregate 20,000 mail-in ballots from individuals it alleges are noncitizens.

“This request violates longstanding constitutional principles mandating that states have control over their elections,” U.S. District Judge David Carter wrote. “If Plaintiff’s last-minute request for relief were granted, either party could create chaos for their own political purposes to disrupt the election process.”

What the administration was asking for, he wrote, “would wreak havoc and disrupt the election that is already underway, create a chilling effect on election workers and voters alike, and discount the votes of United States citizens.”

He also questioned whether the government’s claims of over 20,000 noncitizen voters were accurate.

“Given the vagueness of Plaintiff’s descriptions of its procedures and the unreliability of its similar claims in other states, the Court cannot conclude that Plaintiff’s process is credible,” Carter wrote, pointing to similar allegations the administration had made in a neighboring state.

“In Nevada, DHS initially identified up to 15,903 registered voters as noncitizens based on ‘high-confidence’ matches, but later reduced that number to 6,218, and then to 185 after conducting its manual review. Nevada officials later confirmed that all 185 individuals were citizens,” the judge wrote.

“Had Nevada officials accepted Plaintiff’s allegations there as true, 185 citizens would have been deprived of their vote. This Court will not deprive Californians who have met the requirements for voter registration of their fundamental right because of Plaintiff’s unfounded allegations.”

The Justice Department filed the suit Oct. 15, seeking a court order that the state “set aside vote-by-mail ballots from persons identified by the Federal Government likely to be noncitizens” and not count them until their citizenship is confirmed.

The department said it had compiled “a list of over 20,000 names of people” the government “believes are likely non-citizens.”

Attorneys for the state countered that the government’s numbers are unreliable. In a July letter, it put the number of suspect voters at 190,000 and has refused to release information about how it has generated its lists.

“Where is the evidence?” an attorney representing California, Christina McCall, said at the hearing Tuesday, calling the government’s allegations “vague” and “not reliable.”

Another attorney for the state, Anne Bellows, told the judge “the stakes here are enormous,” arguing the government was trying to force California to initiate an unclear investigation into the citizenship of tens of thousands of people without any clear standards shortly before an election.

It “is a recipe for disenfranchisement,” Bellows said.

First Assistant U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli told Carter: “We’re not telling California what they have to do. We’re telling them what they can’t do.”

Essayli said that once the state is made aware of potential noncitizens on its voter rolls, “they need to do something.”

“What they cannot do is do nothing and proceed to count the votes,” he said.

The judge said that if the government believes there is noncitizen voting, which is a crime, it should bring charges.

“Don’t you have the power of indictments?” Carter asked Essayli. “You brought 28 indictments,” he said, referring to a recent announcement by prosecutors of people who have been found to have voted illegally in California in recent years. “You may find others. You have the right to bring that indictment against them.”

“You’ve got a remedy, for goodness’ sakes,” Carter said. “Go out and indict.”

Essayli on Wednesday bashed the judge’s ruling and said an appeal is underway.

“Every day, more ballots are processed by election officials. This ongoing emergency disenfranchises American citizens through dilution,” he said in a statement. “The Solicitor General authorized an immediate appeal. We filed our Notice of Appeal and will immediately seek an emergency order from the Ninth Circuit.”

The Justice Department has already been to a federal appeals court once in the case. It filed a motion to expedite the case to the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals last week, after Carter did not act on its request to rule in the matter by Thursday.

The appeals court held an emergency hearing Sunday and ruled against the administration 2-1 on Monday.

The majority found it did not have jurisdiction because Carter had not yet ruled, but it found the government’s “claimed emergency” was at least partly “a self-constructed one.”

“At least part of the delay here is attributable to the United States’s own delay in filing suit,” Circuit Judge Mark Bennett wrote in the ruling.

“The United States has taken issue with California’s voter rolls since at least July 16, when it sent a letter to California’s secretary of state citing its ‘[p]reliminary’ analysis of California’s voter rolls. And neither California’s law permitting the processing of ballots twenty-nine days before an election, nor its law providing for the issuance of mail-in ballots to registered voters, is new,” he wrote.

Circuit Judge Gabriel Sanchez wrote in a concurring opinion that the government was asking the “court to dictate to a state legislature a matter squarely within the state’s traditional domain, upending the state’s policies in this regard and inviting disorder and confusion for voters and election officials alike.”

The dissenting judge, Trump appointee Patrick Bumatay, wrote, “We should’ve granted the federal government relief — federal law precludes California from commingling ballots likely cast by aliens with legitimate ballots, because it encourages and induces illegal voting.”

Gary Grumbach is an NBC News legal affairs reporter, based in Washington, D.C.

Dareh Gregorian is a politics reporter for NBC News.

#183. Peltola campaign under fire after report it recruited 2nd Dan Sullivan in Senate race

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Alaskans for Mary said it "has no involvement with either Sullivan campaign."

Alaska Democratic Senate candidate Mary Peltola's campaign is under fire after a New York Times report Wednesday that it played a role in recruiting Dan J. Sullivan -- a Republican who almost has the same name as incumbent GOP Sen. Dan Sullivan -- as part of an effort to sow confusion and ultimately benefit Peltola's campaign.

The Times report said it relied on 12 sources, text messages and documents that allegedly showed members of Peltola's campaign, lawyers for Peltola and senior aides at the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee contributed to pushing Dan J. Sullivan, a Republican on Alaska's four-way ballot, to run.

The Times said the effort to find a spoiler candidate -- who could potentially siphon votes from Sen. Sullivan and benefit Peltola -- was internally referred to as the ballot composition project.

Alaskans for Mary, the campaign committee for Peltola, said through a spokesperson on Wednesday that it "has no involvement with either Sullivan campaign."

The DSCC told ABC News in a statement that it was "not involved with Dan J. Sullivan."

"Republicans have a long history of recruiting and propping up candidates in an attempt to boost their own campaigns," a DSCC spokesperson wrote in the statement, detailing an instance when Peltola ran for reelection in the House in 2024 and the state Republican Party supported an effort by a Democratic House candidate and inmate in New York federal prison to stay on the ballot, challenging Peltola.

Peltola finished first in Alaska's August primary with 49.5% of the vote and Sen. Sullivan was second with 41.4%. Dan J. Sullivan was third with 2.5% of the vote and Democrat David Leslie finished with 1.1%. Leslie later dropped out of the race and Republican Gerald Heikes, who finished fifth in the primary with 1%, moved into the fourth spot on the November ballot.

On the general election ballot, Sen. Sullivan is listed as Dan S. Sullivan, a registered Republican and it is noted that he is the incumbent. The other Sullivan is listed as Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. with no party affiliation.

In response to the Times report, President Donald Trump said in a social media post Wednesday afternoon that Peltola should "suffer the Legal and Political consequences for what she has done," and that she and Dan J. Sullivan should be kicked off the ballot. The president also said Peltola engaged in "election fraud."

"Dan Sullivan should be allowed to run without either Mary Peltola or FAKE Dan Sullivan on the Ballot," Trump wrote.

ABC News has reached out to Dan J. Sullivan's campaign for comment on the New York Times' report. ABC News also reached out to the Alaska Department of Elections for comment.

Alaska courts evaluated the dispute over Dan J. Sullivan's candidacy and found nothing illegal about his bid to be on the ballot -- even if it was not entirely in "good faith" or an attempt to "confuse and mislead" as the Trump administration and Republicans contend.

Ballot-stacking tactics are common in politics and are widely considered legal, the New York Times reported.

Former Peltola staffer Elisa Rios told the Times about the reported recruitment effort, saying Peltola wasn't directly involved but that she was "definitely aware."

However, the Times also reported that Rios later requested a retraction of the statements she gave the newspaper, though she did not disclose a reason.

Rios was also reportedly fired from the campaign this past summer, just days before the primary. At the time, the Peltola campaign told ABC News the "campaign has undergone staffing updates as we transition into the general election" but did not comment on Rios specifically.

For months, Republicans have claimed that Dan J. Sullivan was pursued as a candidate in order to confuse Alaskan voters and potentially peel away voters from the incumbent GOP senator.

In a statement on Wednesday, Nate Adams, campaign spokesperson for incumbent Sen. Sullivan said that "Alaskans deserve the truth" and claimed that Peltola and her campaign "have spent months gaslighting Alaskans" in an effort to "deceive" voters.

The Department of Justice has an active criminal investigation into the circumstances surrounding Dan J. Sullivan's candidacy dating back several months, ABC News has reported.

In a statement on Wednesday, the Alaska Department of Law said the Times' story confirmed its initial concerns about Dan J. Sullivan's candidacy, but it had no impact on the DOJ litigation.

“Placing candidates on the ballot is a matter of state law, not federal law," it said. "The Alaska Supreme Court ordered the Division to place Daniel J. on the primary election ballot and he advanced to the general election under Alaska law as the third-place candidate."

"The Division is bound by the Alaska Supreme Court’s order and is committed to running a fair election and assisting voters as needed,” it said.

The Trump administration preemptively filed a lawsuit last week to block certification of the Alaska Senate race if Sen. Sullivan narrowly loses, citing voter confusion with two Dan Sullivans on the ballot.

The federal judge handling that case signaled considerable skepticism of DOJ's legal strategy on Monday.

District Judge Sharon Gleason highlighted the last-minute nature of the government's actions, especially as Alaskans have already begun casting their ballots.

#174. Early vote one week out shows Democratic enthusiasm in Texas, Georgia: Analysis

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Democrats are getting a higher share of that group, an ABC News analysis shows.

With less than a week to the midterms elections, an ABC News analysis of the early vote in the key states of Texas and Georgia shows signs of more enthusiasm among Democrats, who are getting slightly higher turnout among those who don't vote regularly -- particularly in Texas -- compared to what they got in 2022.

More than 39 million Americans have voted early as of Wednesday afternoon, according to the Election Lab at the University of Florida.

Much of the apparent surge in turnout is among voters who likely would have voted later in the pre-election period or on Election Day, the analysis shows. Less-habitual voters who are voting early, while a much smaller number compared to regular voters, appear to be more likely to vote for the Democrat.

The data from voter records firm L2 that ABC News' analysis is based on does not indicate who someone might have voted for and does not say anything about who might yet turn out on Election Day.

The analysis also uses L2's models for whether a voter is likely to vote Democratic or Republican, as neither Texas nor Georgia register voters by party. The modeling uses available information like turnout in primaries and other factors to predict which party people are likely to support.

More than 5 million voters had cast early ballots in Texas as of Wednesday, according to the Election Lab at the University of Florida, more than 20% of the state’s registered voters.

There are closely watched races for Senate and governor in the Lone Star state; Democrats hope to flip both despite not having won a statewide race since 1994. President Donald Trump rallied there twice on Tuesday and Republicans say the president’s swings through red-leaning states are meant to turn out his base and motivate Republicans who otherwise might have sat out this midterm.

As of Monday, 51% of the early vote in Texas is from voters modeled as likely to vote for Democrats while 45% is from voters modeled as likely to vote for Republicans, the analysis shows -- a 14 percentage point swing for Democrats from 2022 when they were behind Republicans by around 8 percentage points when early voting was finished. Donald Trump won Texas by 14 points in 2024.

In Texas, regular voters who voted in four out of the last four federal elections made up a greater share of the turnout as of Monday than they did in the complete early vote in 2024 and 2022, according to the analysis. This doesn’t necessarily mean that more habitual voters have shown up, just that fewer less-regular voters have. The habitual voters make up 60% of the early vote this year, as of Monday; this group made up 47% of the total early vote in 2024 and 44% of the total early vote in 2022

The early vote data shows that Democrats are getting far higher turnout among the less frequent voters in Texas who likely prefer Democrats, in comparison to the less frequent voters who likely prefer Republicans. Whether it be during the remaining days of early voting or on Election Day, Republicans will need to mobilize their less enthusiastic supporters.

Among voters who did not vote in any of the last four federal elections but who have voted early in Texas as of Monday, 64% of them are modeled as likely to vote for a Democrat and 21% as likely to vote for a Republican. In the total early vote in 2022, 50% of these voters were modeled as likely Democratic voters and 39% were estimated to be likely Republican voters.

In the early vote to date, there are far fewer less-habitual voters compared to high-turnout ones and they take up a smaller share of the early vote overall. Still, close elections are decided at the margins and polling in Texas has shown the Senate and governor’s races being close.

More than 2.1 million voters had cast early ballots in Georgia as of Wednesday morning, according to the Election Lab at the University of Florida, where the Senate and governor's races are also being closely watched.

In the Senate race, GOP Rep. Mike Collins is trying to unseat incumbent Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff. Democrats are trying to flip the governorship as Democratic former Atlanta Mayor Keisha Lance Bottoms faces Republican businessman Rick Jackson in a race both parties see as competitive.

According to ABC News' analysis, 54% of the early vote in Georgia is from voters modeled as likely to vote for Democrats while 38% is from voters modeled as likely to vote for Republicans.

In Georgia, habitual voters who voted in four out of the last four federal elections comprised a greater share of the turnout as of Monday than they did in the early vote one week out from the 2022 midterms.

Habitual voters made up 67% of the early vote in 2026 as of Monday while they comprised 55% of the total early vote one week from Election Day 2022.

Among voters who did not vote in any of the last four federal elections but who have voted early in Georgia as of Monday, 52% of them are modeled as likely to vote for Democrats and 12% are modeled as likely to vote for Republicans. In the early vote at this time in 2022, 51% of those voters were modeled Democratic and 27% were modeled Republican

Meanwhile, among voters who voted in two out of the last four federal elections who had voted early in Georgia as of Monday, 56% are modeled Democratic and 24% Republican. In the early vote at this point in 2022, 54% of those voters were modeled Democratic and 35% Republican.

Early voting ends in both states on Friday.

#108. Meet the Democrats trying to flip the script on gun rights: From the Politics Desk

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Welcome to From the Politics Desk, a daily newsletter that brings you the NBC News Politics team’s latest reporting and analysis from the White House, Capitol Hill and the campaign trail.

In today’s edition, Frank Thorp V and Ryan Nobles speak with Democratic candidates who are touting their gun credentials. Plus, Andrea Mitchell digs into President Donald Trump’s potential next moves on Iran.

A group of Democratic candidates on the ballot next week are hoping to start a new conversation around guns as part of a strategy to win back rural voters in red-leaning areas.

Wisconsin’s 1st District: Democrat Mitchell Berman’s ads have portrayed him as a proud gun owner and hunter. One shows him fishing while wearing a handgun in a holster; another shows him skeet shooting with clay pigeons labeled “Data Centers.” After the target explodes in the air, his 6-year-old daughter yells, “Great shot, Dad!”

“That’s who I am,” Berman told NBC News. “When I go fishing up north — where I’m from we have wolves. We have — in spring trout season, we have bears that are coming out of their dens with cubs. We’ve even had a mountain lion on our trail camera. So, you know, when I go out into the woods, I carry a sidearm with me for protection.”

His race against GOP Rep. Bryan Steil was not expected to be close. President Donald Trump carried the district in 2020 and 2024, and Steil won re-election two years ago by 10 points. But polling has shown the race is within reach for Democrats.

Texas’ 15th District: Bobby Pulido, a Tejano singer-turned-Democratic congressional candidate, is not shy about his love for guns and hunting. He’s posted videos of him shooting a target a mile away, and his rec room is lined with the trophies he’s gathered on hunts. He even named his oldest son Remington.

“Listen, in the rural areas, firearms are a way of life,” Pulido told NBC News, saying that fundraisers in the Rio Grande Valley are more common at skeet shooting events than at golf courses.

Pulido is also running in an area that Trump won in the last two presidential elections. The district was redrawn earlier this year to make it more favorable for Republicans, but a shift in the Latino vote away from Trump mixed with a struggling economy has put Republican Rep. Monica De La Cruz at risk of losing her bid for re-election.

On the campaign trail, President Donald Trump has been confidently promising that oil prices will drop like a rock after the midterms — especially skyrocketing diesel fuel critical in farm states that could decide which party wins the Senate. Implicit is that he will attempt to do something decisive after next week to end the war against Iran and fully reopen the Strait of Hormuz.

His not-so-subtle hints are backed up by new reporting in The Wall Street Journal and other publications that the U.S. has told Gulf partners it will resume intense, brief strikes against Iran after the midterms. And Israel’s hawkish Trump ally Benjamin Netanyahu has at least temporarily retained a caretaker role as prime minister while he and his rival, former Army Chief of Staff Gadi Eisenkot, jockey for a majority. In Israel’s complex parliamentary system, a decisive outcome could take weeks or months, or even lead to another election.

That places Netanyahu in control of a potential resumption of the war, a position he had advocated for before Israelis went to the polls, according to The New York Times and other outlets. According to those reports, Israel’s military and intelligence leaders effectively vetoed that option, saying it would politicize their decision-making.

As flawed as Israel’s democracy has been in many respects, according to many of its own citizens and even many longtime U.S. supporters, it still has the virtue of a largely independent military. But in the U.S., critics fear that may not be the case with the civilian leadership of the military in the second Trump administration.

In past administrations in both political parties, secretaries of defense and their top deputies were usually experienced foreign policy or former military leaders, willing to offer independent opinions to the presidents they served in a formalized National Security Council interagency system. Conflicting options were debated in a deliberative process before they were presented to the commander in chief.

Yes, on occasion, chief executives were misled by false narratives, like those delivered during the Vietnam War by Defense Secretary Robert McNamara. Or, during the Iraq quagmire, when the chain of command was short-circuited by a powerful personality like President George W. Bush’s defense secretary, Donald Rumsfeld. Notably, Rumsfeld was forced out of his role the morning after Bush’s party got hammered in the 2006 midterms.

Now, whether his party wins or loses in next week’s midterms, Trump faces what could be the most consequential decision of his second term: whether to “finish the job” and resume attacking Iran.

Such a decision would fly in the faces of European leaders who counsel that renewed strikes would lead to higher oil prices and a potential economic downturn and not produce a more compliant regime in Tehran. At the same time, there is now a growing consensus in Riyadh and other Gulf capitals that they need to find some accommodation with Iran to get the regime to try to restrain its Houthi proxies, who threaten to become an existential threat to their economies.

The question for Trump is whether to follow the advice of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, the former Fox News personality who has purged many of the Pentagon’s most experienced top generals and has privately advocated for resuming military action against Iran.

The hawks could be right that a weakened Iran could be felled by a decisive blow, though skeptics note that such a strategy has not yet proved successful. The U.S. and global economy — to say nothing of Trump’s legacy — may well depend on what he decides in the next few weeks.
