In other words, most of Oklahoma City's core rotation from a year ago, aside from Shai Gilgeous-Alexander, is not available right now. Inevitably, that has sparked quite a bit of speculation. The Thunder are as asset-rich as any team in the NBA. They have the picks to go trade for a difference-maker and a roster spot to add a free agent. The question here is who, and on Wednesday, ESPN's Brian Windhorst stirred the pot with an intentionally cryptic rumor.

"And this is one thing that I've talked to executives about, and I don't mean to be cryptic, but I'm being cryptic," he said on his Hoop Collective podcast. "If you're going to, if they're going to acquire a player, it would not be a surprise if they acquire a player they've already had."

This makes plenty of sense on paper. The Thunder are very particular about their culture. Bringing in someone new is far riskier than reacquiring someone they already know, and given their asset surplus, they can afford to be picky. Given Windhorst's track record of cryptic reporting leading to seismic transactions, let's take a look around the league to see which former Thunder players, if any, make sense as reacquisition targets.

The Thunder traded Dort, Joe and Wiggins last offseason. Given how things have played out, Oklahoma City may regret doing so, and given this rumor cycle, fans will inevitably call for them to try to reacquire one of last season's core pieces. Alas, Dort, Joe and Wiggins are functionally not obtainable to the Thunder. The NBA does not allow teams to reacquire players they have traded until the following season. That means Dort, Joe and Wiggins are off limits for now.

There is a very narrow exception to this rule, but it is not likely to come up. If the player is traded a second time and then gets waived, the original team can re-sign him upon clearing waivers. This happened with Mike Conley and the Minnesota Timberwolves last season. Of course, no team is likely to waive Dort, Joe or Wiggins, so we should assume for the sake of argument that they are off the table.

Microsoft generated $10 billion in revenue from commercial arrangements with OpenAI, including revenue-sharing payments, in the quarter ended September 30, the company said in a regulatory filing on Wednesday. That represents nearly 11% of Microsoft’s total quarterly revenue of $92.3 billion, highlighting the enormous financial ties that remain between the two companies even as they increasingly compete for the same customers.

(Microsoft’s financial year begins in July, making the quarter ended September 30, 2026 the first quarter of its fiscal 2027.)

For the full 2026 fiscal year, revenue from OpenAI hit $24.1 billion. At its latest quarterly pace, Microsoft would pull in $40 billion annually from OpenAI-related arrangements.

It’s not clear how much of Microsoft’s revenue from OpenAI came from revenue-sharing payments or Azure cloud computing services. TechCrunch has reached out for more information. In exchange, OpenAI committed to buying an additional $250 billion in Azure services as part of an October 2025 agreement.

Microsoft’s ownership stake in OpenAI has also declined slightly from about 25% in June to 24% in September. That continues a gradual decline from roughly 27% following the October 2025 recapitalization. Microsoft did not specify what caused the dilution.

#307. Fed holds interest rates steady even as inflation remains high

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Updated on: October 28, 2026 / 5:34 PM EDT / CBS News

The Federal Reserve held interest rates steady on Wednesday after hiking borrowing costs in September to clamp down on inflation.

The central bank left the federal funds rate at a target range of 3.75% to 4%, keeping it unchanged after raising rates last month for the first time in three years.

In a statement, the Fed said economic activity is growing at a "strong pace" while acknowledging that inflation remains elevated.

"The accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 2% goal," Fed officials said.

Wall Street had forecast that the Fed would stand pat on rates. Investor reaction was muted, with the three major indexes remaining essentially flat after the Fed's announcement.

The Fed's benchmark rate is its main weapon for stabilizing consumer prices, which have crept up in recent months. The latest rate decision comes less than a week before Americans head to the polls for the midterm elections, where affordability issues will be top of mind for voters. President Trump has long urged the Fed to ease borrowing costs.

During a press conference after the rate decision, Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh said economic indicators are moving in the right direction, citing robust consumer spending, strong capital expenditures and healthy overall economic growth.

But he said inflation remains "too high" and acknowledged that the Fed hasn't yet succeeded in bringing inflation under control.

"On the employment side of the Fed's dual mandate, conditions are quite positive, and yet Americans, of course, are still dealing with inflation that has been running above the committee's objective for close to six years," he told reporters. "It's our responsibility to get inflation down."

Ten members of the Federal Open Market Committee, which sets interest rates, voted to hold the benchmark rate steady. Two FOMC members — Fed Board Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Federal Reserve Bank President Beth Hammack — voted to raise it by 0.25 percentage points.

"We just came out of a heck of a family fight, a serious, sober discussion," Warsh said Wednesday. "I think we came to the right policy decision for this moment."

Investors and Wall Street analysts widely expected the Fed to remain on hold in October, but many economists think policymakers will raise rates at their last meeting of the year in December and again in 2027.

"The two hawkish dissents, the wording of the policy statement, and prior comments from officials all suggest the central bank will hike rates in December," Michael Pearce, chief U.S. economist for forecasting firm Oxford Economics, said in an email following the Fed's decision.

Samuel Tombs, chief U.S. economist at investment advisory firm Pantheon Macroeconomics, said financial markets are pricing in an additional 0.75 percentage points of tightening over the next year as the Fed tries to douse inflation.

Warsh demurred when asked if Fed officials would raise rates at their Dec. 8-9 meeting, citing his stated reluctance to telegraph his policy views.

"A lot of things can happen, so I'm not going to be able to share with you my musings about what happens when this group gets together six weeks from now," he told a reporter.

The Iran war has kept global energy costs elevated, making it harder to bring down prices in the U.S. Last month, the closely watched Consumer Price Index rose at a 3.7% annual rate; core inflation, which strips out volatile food and energy prices, rose 2.6% from a year ago. Both measures were above economists' expectations.

Solid economic growth and steady consumer spending are also exerting upward pressure on prices, complicating the task of taming inflation, according to economists.

The federal funds rate — what banks charge each other for overnight loans — influences the cost of borrowing across the U.S. economy. A rate hike can spur higher borrowing costs for bank loans, credit cards and auto loans, while boosting returns for savings accounts.

The Fed's pause in rate hikes could provide borrowers with some breathing room, Bankrate home lending expert Linda Bell said, citing potential relief for home equity rates. Still, consumers may be in for higher borrowing costs down the road. Matt Schulz, a chief consumer finance analyst at LendingTree, said it's possible some loan rates could continue to climb heading into the holiday shopping season.

"Even though the Fed is leaving rates alone this month, it might raise rates again at its last meeting of the year in December," Schulz said in an email ahead of the Fed's decision. "That is far from guaranteed, but it is something that Americans should be aware of. It means that the debt they run up during the holidays could get a little more expensive to pay off before the year ends, through no fault of their own."

While the Fed's decision to leave rates unchanged was widely expected, Warsh elicited surprise by announcing changes to the central bank's meeting schedule.

Policymakers will continue to meet eight times throughout the year. But starting in 2027, one of those meetings will be reserved for discussion and strategizing on monetary policy, Warsh said. At that meeting, the Fed will not vote on interest rates or issue a policy statement.

By 2028, Warsh said he expects two meetings to be dedicated to policy discussion and six to issuing rate decisions.

The change aligns with Warsh's broader vision for the Fed. He has been vocal in arguing for more limited communication from Fed officials, known as forward guidance, to preserve their flexibility in setting policy.

"Getting policy right involves assessing near-term developments at timely, regular and information-rich intervals," he said. "But it's equally important we reserve time and space as an intellectually diverse group for strategic deliberations, for thinking anew about big questions, looking out over longer time horizons, focusing, as I say, on the left side of the decimal point."
