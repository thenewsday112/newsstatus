#132. Chiefs coach defends signing kicker suspended after sexual misconduct allegations

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Kansas City Chiefs head coach Andy Reid offered only a short explanation Wednesday when he was asked why the team was comfortable signing kicker Justin Tucker, who has not played in the NFL since 2024 after a string of sexual misconduct allegations.

“He’s done his suspension. Took care of his business there. I’ll leave it at that,” Reid said when he was asked what message signing Tucker sent to female fans.

Asked what level of involvement team ownership had in the signing, Reid said, “We try to do our homework best way we possibly can and talk to a lot of people and make sure things are OK.”

On Wednesday, the Chiefs signed Tucker, 37, to their practice squad. Kansas City was looking for competition at the kicking position because of the poor performance of starting kicker Harrison Butker, who has missed five of his 16 field-goal attempts this season.

Tucker last played for the Baltimore Ravens during the 2024 season. In January 2025, The Baltimore Banner reported accounts from six women who accused Tucker of nonconsensual sexual behavior during massage therapy sessions from 2012 to 2016, including that he exposed his genitals and brushed women with his exposed penis. The following month, 10 more massage therapists came forward with more accusations against Tucker.

He denied all allegations at the time, initially describing them as “unequivocally false.”

“I maintain I did not act inappropriately at any point before, during, or after a professional bodywork treatment session, nor have I ever been told I am unwelcome at any massage therapy provider. These claims are simply not true,” Tucker said in a statement in February 2025. “It devastates me to know that anyone I have worked with would not have felt respected and valued as a professional, but more importantly as a person, and to anyone who has felt otherwise, I am sorry.”

The NFL suspended Tucker for the first 10 weeks of the 2025 season following an investigation, but he remained unsigned for the entire season. He worked out with the Miami Dolphins earlier this year, according to NFL reporter Aaron Wilson.

Asked about his decision-making process when it comes to signing a player such as Tucker, Reid said: “I try to do homework on it, see exactly what’s taken place since. He served a suspension; there’s been time involved with it. That’s how I evaluate it.”

Asked the kind of homework he did on Tucker, Reid said, “I’m not going to get into all of that.”

Reporters also asked Chiefs quarterback Patrick Mahomes about the signing Wednesday, and he expressed confidence in the team’s front office.

“We had a lot of people that we’ve added in and out,” Mahomes said, per ESPN. “I try not to get too high or too low on anything. I know [general manager Brett] Veach [and the front office] do a great job of bringing great players in. But for me, it was about the Denver Broncos and moving on and continuing to get better.”

Tucker is second all time in the NFL in field-goal kicking percentage. He struggled during his final season with the Ravens, however, converting a career-worst 73.3% of his field-goal attempts.

#55. Trump administration will allow pharmacies to make popular peptides

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Secretary of Health and Human Services Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has supported broader access to peptides. A new policy will make it easier for small compounding pharmacies to make them. Heather Diehl/Getty Images hide caption

Unproven but popular peptides touted for wellness have been available on the gray market, despite an official ban. Now, the Department of Health and Human Services is looking to bring them out of the shadows.

HHS said in a new policy that it will exercise enforcement discretion for a half dozen peptides, essentially looking the other way when they're made by small compounding pharmacies.

Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has been a vocal supporter of peptides, saying he has used some of them himself.

Peptides are strings of amino acids, shorter than proteins, that can exert powerful effects in the body. Some occur naturally and others are synthetic variations. Wellness influencers and some clinicians have promoted unproven peptides for a wide range of uses, though the evidence for their safety and effectiveness is sparse.

So even though these peptides have not been through the Food and Drug Administration's rigorous drug review and approval process, they could become easier to get. But the policy's focus on smaller producers may mean that pharmacists will have a tough time meeting demand.

In July, a panel of advisers to the FDA voted in favor of expanded access to six peptides through compounding pharmacies. However, at the same meeting the FDA's own scientists recommended against unwinding the existing restrictions on them.

The new policy says compounding is a safer alternative to illicit "research-use-only" peptides that are available online. They're often sourced from sketchy suppliers overseas.

The peptides, and some of their unproven uses, are:

Joshua Fritzler, the president of Olympia and Wesley Pharmaceuticals in central Florida, says people have been asking about these peptides for years. But because they were illegal to compound, his company didn't make them.

The new policy doesn't change their legal status, but it does create a path for small compounders, called 503A compounding pharmacies. It doesn't apply to the larger ones, called 503Bs, that are outsourcing facilities producing higher volumes of medicines.

So at Fritzler's company, only its smaller compounding facility will be able to make the peptides – and only in small quantities, he says.

"We won't be able to meet demand," he says, explaining that Olympia will likely only compound one of the six peptides and needs to get its facility ready first. "I don't think all the [503]As in the country could meet the demand."

The Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding's CEO Scott Brunner wrote in a statement that his group is pleased with the administration's decision to allow the peptides to be compounded because it offers a "more accountable alternative" for patients seeking gray market peptides.

"It also brings patients into a system where pharmacy standards, regulatory oversight, and serious adverse event reporting apply," he says.

Brunner warned, however, that it may take some time for compounding pharmacies to begin dispensing these products to patients. That's because ingredient suppliers need to make sure their active ingredients meet the policy's requirements — and pharmacies need to verify that, too.

Several consumer advocacy groups say the policy puts patients at risk, however. The nonprofit Public Citizen noted that Kennedy seems to have acted unilaterally rather than through the typical rulemaking process.

"By allowing unproven and potentially unsafe peptides to be manufactured, marketed and prescribed without concern about potential federal enforcement actions, Kennedy has undermined the FDA and threatened public health," Dr. Robert Steinbrook, Health Research Group Director at Public Citizen wrote in a statement. "All peptide substances should go through the standard FDA approval process for new drugs. The HHS Secretary should support, not sabotage, needed FDA enforcement actions to protect consumers."

Steinbrook advised clinicians to talk to their patients and tell them about the risk of contamination and other adverse effects.

The Partnership for Safe Medicines, which includes various pharmacy and pharmacist organizations around the country, filed a citizen petition last week, urging the FDA to ignore the recommendation from its advisory committee in July to allow compounding pharmacies to make the six peptides.

"Today's decision leaves Americans in a grey area as to whether peptides are safe or not, even though the FDA's own scientists recommended against voting in favor of these substances due to concerns with evidence," its executive director Shabbir Safda wrote in a statement.

Apple says that third-party developers like Microsoft and Adobe can and will update their apps to expand buttons and other touch targets to be larger. But even software that hasn't been explicitly updated for the new computer will support touch. Apple says that behind the scenes, its software converts every touch tap into a traditional click. So any button that can be clicked can also be touched.

Using the laptop with your finger feels good, but it won't change the way most people use their laptops. It could be useful for iPhone app developers, who can now test the way their apps work on their Mac. An accessibility feature allows users to pinch to zoom on any part of the user interface.

Instead of the notch at the top of previous MacBooks, where Apple hides its cameras, the new MacBooks have a hole-punch cutout in the display, like recent iPhones. Apple's Dynamic Island feature — new to the MacBook — expands to show live activities and Siri.

Apple made a big deal about the laptop's new hinge, which is meant to keep the screen from shaking when you touch it. For example, if you tap the screen on a video call, your camera won't shake as much. But the hinge feels largely the same as Apple's previous MacBook Pros.

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Passengers on a live-aboard round-the-world cruise say they were abruptly asked to leave the ship and given only hours to disembark.

Villa Vie Residences, which operates the residential cruise ship Villa Vie Odyssey, said on Tuesday it had halted operations due to surging fuel costs.

The ship was docked at the port of Sihanoukville in Cambodia when the announcement was made, with passengers saying they were given only eight hours to disembark.

"On October 27, 2026, we received notice from the CEO at around 6 a.m. that all residents had to disembark by 2 p.m. that day," passengers Lanette Canen and Johan Bodin told Business Insider by email.

If you are or have been a resident of the Villa Vie Odyssey and have a story to share, get in touch with this reporter: jzitser@businessinsider.com.

Canen and Bodin have been on the Odyssey since it first set sail in September 2024 and run a YouTube channel called Living Life on a Cruise about their experiences on board.

"We knew leaving was a possibility, but the eight-hour notice still surprised us," they said.

The couple shared a video on their YouTube channel that appears to show the moment a staff member announced that passengers must leave the ship.

"Unfortunately, they were unable to secure the fuel necessary to continue operations," a male voice says in the video.

A notice on the Villa Vie Residences website says the company is working with residents to arrange temporary living arrangements and that it is evaluating restructuring options over the next 60 days.

"I get it. I get it. I feel awful," he said in a phone interview. "They're counting on me to make the decisions here and there's nothing else I'm focused on. I'm the villain in the story, but I'm doing everything I can to support the residents on a solution forward."

Petterson said that until last week, he was in talks with a potential investor and was expecting a new infusion of cash. When the deal fell apart, he said, the company was out of options.

Villa Vie Residences Chief Executive Kathy Villalba didn't immediately respond to requests for comment.

Villa Vie Residences said in a statement Tuesday that the surging cost of fuel since the beginning of the Iran war had made operating the cruise liner increasingly difficult.

The company said its monthly fuel expenses per cabin, which had previously averaged about $1,800, had risen as much as 344% in parts of Asia, to an estimated $6,000 to $8,000.

"Since the conflict began in late February, we have taken significant steps to reduce fuel consumption, including slowing the itinerary and spending more time in port," Villa Vie Residences said.

"Those measures have not been enough to offset the extraordinary increase in fuel costs, and we have been unable to continue carrying the resulting operating losses."

On August 1, Villalba, the CEO, sent residents a message titled "Urgent operational update" on an internal messaging platform. In the message, she referenced a "fuel contribution" that had been previously requested of residents. Business Insider has viewed a screenshot of the message.

"When the fuel contribution was requested several months ago, participation was not universal," she wrote in the message. "The contributions received helped some, but they covered only part of the extraordinary increase in fuel costs."

Villalba wrote that the "cash-flow" challenge had continued, and the Odyssey needed 650 metric tons of fuel, adding that fuel prices had almost doubled.

"We have now reached a critical point. The lack of full participation, combined with the unprecedented increase in fuel costs, means we must make immediate and creative operational decisions to preserve the voyage," her message said.

It continued, "We are asking every Resident who did not previously contribute to please do so now. We are also asking those who are able to provide additional support to consider helping with this immediate fuel requirement."

Villalba described the financial need as "real, immediate, and necessary to keep Odyssey moving." It went on, "This is a moment when preserving our home and continuing the voyage requires support beyond contractual minimums from those who are able to provide it."

Among the passengers asked to disembark on Tuesday with only a few hours' notice was Barbara Fiegel, 62, who has lived with her partner aboard the ship since December 2024.

"Everybody's just shocked, everybody's just saddened by this whole issue," she said. "We're still processing, trying to debate what we're going to do."

Fiegel said she and her partner booked a hotel room in Cambodia, then returned to the ship Tuesday evening and Wednesday afternoon to empty their safe and pack their belongings. "Today, as we approached, as we went back on the shuttle back to the ship, I couldn't stop crying," she said.

They plan to return on Thursday to collect their remaining items. Fiegel described the atmosphere on the Odyssey as a “ghost town.”

Joel Greiff, 57, moved aboard the ship at the start of the month and planned to live on the ship for 10 or 20 years or more. “I don’t think that will be happening,” he said.

When residents were told to disembark, Greiff emptied his cabin. “I personally grabbed everything,” he said.

He said he would like to return if Villa Vie Residences resumes operations. If not, he is planning to move to Thailand. In the meantime, he has rented an apartment in Cambodia for a month.

Greiff said he plans to return to the Odyssey on Thursday to assist other residents and “help with their luggage, help them find a place, and just start getting them and all their stuff off the ship.”
