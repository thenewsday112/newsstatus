Google is making it easier for businesses to connect their apps to its AI experiences across Search and Gemini with the debut of its Connected Apps Platform.

Introduced in Gemini and Google Search earlier this year, connected apps let users securely link and interact with popular third-party services, then use them to take specific actions. For instance, you could connect to Canva to design a flyer, or to YouTube Music to build a playlist.

We’ve seen varying takes on the idea by other consumer-facing AI services like ChatGPT and Meta’s Muse. The premise is that such integrations could ultimately shift consumer behavior away from launching and using individual apps to instead using their favorite services through their preferred AI provider.

However, to successfully capture and retain consumers’ attention, there have to actually be a wide range of connected apps to explore.

Google’s idea is to open its doors to any business or developer that wants to reach users through Search or Gemini by allowing them to submit their own connected apps and monitor their performance.

The platform preview is currently available in beta to developers in the U.S., and will allow developers to build and submit apps that can be used across Google’s products.

To get started, developers would integrate with a cloud-hosted MCP (Model Context Protocol) server or Android MCP using the AppFunctions APIs, then use the Google Connected Console to register, test, and submit their app, Google explains.

Essentially, this process lets devs connect their app to Google’s services so it can be used with the search giant’s AI features. Developers can also choose whether they want their app to be available via Search, the Gemini app, or both.

After the app is live, developers can track traffic, latency, and error rates, and maintain control over their brand identity, such as their verified logos and authenticated accounts.

Similar to how SEO allowed companies and websites to show search engine rankings, this could well be one of the initial steps for businesses that want their services to be more visible to users on the agentic web.

Google already has over 50 Connected Apps available in Gemini, including from partners like Adobe, Experian, Localiza, Peloton, and others, in addition to its own expansive set of services, from Workplace and Photos to YouTube Music and Google Home.

These apps will now come to Google Search over the next few weeks. Search today has fewer integrations, featuring apps like Instacart, Canva, YouTube Music, Yahoo Fantasy Football, and others.

Google said meditation app Calm is another new partner on this effort, and that its app will soon launch on both Gemini and Search.

The new Connected Apps platform is in public preview for U.S. developers who sign up for the waitlist. Google did not share when it would become more broadly available.

At the Android Dev Summit on Wednesday, Google’s smart glasses partner Xreal launched its new Xreal Aura wired smart glasses, powered by the Android XR and Qualcomm’s Snapdragon Reality Elite platforms. The companies had teased the glasses earlier in the year at Google’s I/O developer conference.

As Meta expands its push into smart glasses, competitors like Apple and Google are keen to release their own devices. Much like it did with Android smartphones, Google is relying on an assortment of partners, including Samsung and Xreal, to bring devices powered by its Android XR platform to consumers.

The new Xreal Aura smart glasses are tethered to a smartphone-like device for processing power, rather than handling all the computing themselves. The glasses then display the content in the wearer’s field of view.

The device’s design, meanwhile, is closer to Meta’s smart glasses than Apple’s Vision Pro, which is a larger headset.

Xreal Aura uses Sony’s Micro-OLED display with 1200p resolution at 120 Hz and a 95-degree field of view — screen specs that put the glasses above the Ray-Ban Meta Display on paper. However, Xreal’s smart glasses are heavier at 95 grams compared to Meta’s 69 grams, and you have to carry the puck.

At launch, the Aura supports many popular Google Play apps and games along with specific XR experiences, including the NBA App for Android XR; Nevatars; Fallout: Factions; Project Hail Mary: Journey Among the Stars; the NASCAR XR App; and the San Francisco Ballet Company’s “The Nutcracker.”

Users can opt to plug a computer or handheld gaming console directly into the Xreal Aura via USB-C to make it an augmented display. The device can also allow for various entertainment and productivity use cases by spinning up a multimonitor setup or a big virtual screen.

For audio, the Xreal Aura uses Bose’s tech to power its sound while watching videos or listening to music.

Given that the new smart glasses are powered by Android XR, Gemini is available to users as a Q&A assistant and can help them understand on-screen context.

The Xreal Aura is available to order starting today in the United States, Canada, Japan, andSouth Korea. The 12GB + 256GB version costs $1,279 with a limited launch price of $1,099 through December, and the 16GB + 512GB version is priced at $1,499 with a launch price of $1,299 through December. The company said it will start shipping by mid-November.

In May, Xreal CEO Chi Xu told TechCrunch that building a smart glass business is hard and everyone is losing money. He also said that the Aura glasses will have a wide range of use cases, and the industry has reached an inflection point of having the right hardware, software, and design smart glasses to reach scale.

“You need all the key pieces ready — you need the hardware ready, the operating system needs to be ready, and then you need a great user interface,” Xu said at that time.

#381. New York governor signs AI chatbot child safety law

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A separate law aims to shore up security on connected baby monitors.

New York Governor Kathy Hochul has signed a law that aims to protect children from AI chatbot harms. The legislation requires AI companies to verify users' ages. They'll need to offer users at least one verification method that doesn't entirely rely on government ID. Companies will have to delete this "age information" after use.

If a user is under 18 or a company has yet to confirm the person is an adult, their chatbot will need to abide by certain restrictions. For instance, it cannot pretend "to be a real person, to be alive or to have human emotions." According to a press release, the legislation also bans chatbots from:

Claiming a personal, romantic, professional or authority-figure relationship with a child

Unprompted emotional questions, and using a child's private health or personal information from earlier conversations

Promoting or helping with suicide, self harm, eating disorders or drug and alcohol use

Encouraging kids to keep secrets, isolate themselves or avoid asking parents, trusted adults or professionals for help

Any sexually explicit content or child sexual abuse material

There will be a website through which the public can file complaints to New York's attorney general. Companies that break the law may be on the hook for civil penalties of up to $25,000 per violation, as well as damages, restitution and other forms of relief. They may also have to delete any data they collect unlawfully along with models trained on that information. The law will take effect in six months.

"Under this new law sponsored by Senator Gonzalez and Assemblymember Bores, no longer will minors have access to chatbots that pretend to be their friends and boyfriends and girlfriends," Hochul said at a press conference. "No longer will chatbots be allowed to keep sensitive information about a minor's emotional and physical health. No longer will chatbots discourage kids from seeking help from licensed professionals. And I'm not sure why we have to pass a law against something so obvious, but no longer will minors have access to chatbots that may in any way encourage thoughts of suicide. Now, this law builds on our groundbreaking Safe for Kids Act that sent a message loud and clear to technology companies: We will not allow you to monetize our children's mental health."

"New York just made history by signing into law the strongest kids' AI safety bill in the country," Jim Steyer, founder and CEO of Common Sense Media, said in a statement. "AI companies offer chatbots with features that are dangerous, and even deadly, for kids and teens. That is not innovation; it is reckless endangerment of our children. Now, those harmful features will be outlawed in New York."

Steyer added that the legislation is a template for the rest of the US. Earlier this month, eight states filed separate lawsuits against OpenAI, accusing the company of endangering children.

In September, New York City banned public schools from letting children from 2-K to eighth grade access generative AI tools in class. There are some exceptions, including for those who require assistive technology. During the one-year ban, the city will carry out studies into the impact of the tech on children.

Hochul also signed legislation that will require baby monitors with an internet connection that are sold in the state to prevent unauthorized users from seeing or hearing any activity that the devices capture. These baby monitors will also need to have warning labels that remind users why it's important to use security features and highlight the risks of unsecured connections. The law takes effect in 90 days.

#49. RFK Jr. takes shortcut to make some unapproved peptides available for use

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Department of Health and Human Services is giving compounding pharmacies a temporary green light to make six peptides while the agency continues to work on a formal rule change.

In a notice filed Wednesday, the agency said it would exercise “enforcement discretion,” meaning it will not take legal action against compounding pharmacies that make the six unapproved peptides. In practice, that means these pharmacies can start offering them now, even though the peptides aren’t yet on the Food and Drug Administration’s list of ingredients compounding pharmacies are allowed to make.

The six peptides are BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500.

They’ve been promoted online as treatments for everything from faster healing to younger-looking skin and a longer lifespan. None are approved by the FDA, and the agency’s own scientists concluded there is insufficient data to prove they are safe and effective.

The FDA said compounding pharmacies must certify the identity and purity of the compounds. They must also now report side effects, which was not previously mandated.

The move comes as a growing “gray market” has sprung up around peptides, where retailers sell them labeled “for research use only,” a legal workaround that lets them claim they aren’t meant for humans. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and others have argued that allowing peptides to be compounded would move people away from this gray market and toward safer products.

“Under President Trump’s leadership, HHS is working to put prevention and patient safety at the center of health care—and that includes confronting the risks of black-market peptides,” Emily Hilliard, an HHS spokesperson, said in a statement.

Dr. Peter Lurie, a former FDA official who now leads the Center for Science in the Public Interest watchdog group, said the FDA was essentially “turning a blind eye” to widen access to drugs that have not been proven to work.

Compounding pharmacies generally may use only ingredients the FDA has added to an official list known as the bulk drugs substances list. Adding an ingredient requires the FDA to propose a rule, take public comment and issue a final rule, a process that can take several months.

The agency took a step toward that process in July, when an FDA advisory panel narrowly voted in favor of adding six peptides to the bulk list. The panel included six new appointees with ties to the peptide industry and another who is the son of a U.S. representative who has pushed Kennedy to make peptides more available. (During that meeting, some committee members expressed concerns that the recommendation could make it harder for the FDA to say no to other unapproved drugs in the future.)

On Tuesday and Wednesday, the FDA was scheduled to meet outside groups, including Medivant Healthcare, a compounding pharmacy that offers peptides, according to Reginfo.gov, a federal website that tracks government regulations.

Shabbir Safdar, executive director of the Partnership for Safe Medicines, which researches the dangers of counterfeit medicines, said both the enforcement discretion and upcoming rule change would expose Americans to “untested, dangerous substances.”

“Imagine what it means for the nation’s top drug regulator to announce they won’t enforce the laws on people making completely untested compounds and distributing them to the public,” Safdar said.

When then-President Barack Obama presented the award in 2012, he praised Huerta as a tenacious leader and joked that he had stolen her slogan for his 2008 campaign.

This year, Huerta sent shockwaves through the Latino community when she revealed that she was sexually abused in the 1960s by the farmworker movement's co-founder, César Chavez, leading to the birth of two children, a secret she kept for 60 years and long after he died.

From the early 1970s on, Huerta's life partner was Richard Chavez, César's brother, and together they had four kids. She also had seven children from two earlier marriages.

Huerta said she had kept silent for so long because she feared that coming forward would compromise her life's work and unravel all the progress made for workers.

When asked during a 2026 interview with the news organization Latino USA about keeping the secret, Huerta said: "It was my personal pain, it was my personal problem and, you know, I think it was worth it because it was my cross to bear. I'll say it that way."

Huerta was born Dolores Clara Fernandez in 1930 in Dawson, New Mexico. Her father was a miner and union activist who went on to win a seat in the state Legislature. Her parents divorced when she was 3, and her mother took her and her brothers to California.

Immersed in a melting pot of Mexican, Filipino, African American, Japanese and Chinese working families, Huerta learned from her mother — who owned a restaurant and hotel — about independence, compassion and activism.

Huerta was a young, energetic elementary school teacher in the farm-rich San Joaquin Valley when she decided to answer another calling after growing frustrated by her students' poor living conditions.

"I couldn't stand seeing kids come to class hungry and needing shoes," she said in a 2017 PBS Independent Lens documentary on her life. "I thought I could do more by organizing farmworkers than by trying to teach their hungry children."

Huerta's early work with a civil rights group in Stockton resulted in her meeting Chavez. The two wanted to focus more on the plight of farmworkers, so in 1962 they started the National Farm Workers Association, which became United Farm Workers of America a few years later. She was a key leader and negotiator for the union for years.

She was among those who joined Filipino grape pickers on the picket line in what became the five-year "Delano Grape Strike." With Chavez, she negotiated collective bargaining agreements and contracts with big growers, winning the first health and benefit plans for farmworkers.

Chavez, who died in 1993, once described her as fearless, committed to the cause and tough when it came to negotiations.

She met U.S. Sen. Robert Kennedy of Massachusetts during his visit to Delano in 1968 and became a volunteer on his presidential campaign, registering Mexican Americans in California to vote. Huerta was on stage with Kennedy after he won the California primary and he publicly thanked her moments before he was shot.

The assassination devastated Huerta, but she kept organizing for farmworkers' rights.

Huerta was jailed 22 times for union demonstrating. In 1988, she was seriously injured while protesting Republican presidential candidate George H.W. Bush's opposition to the UFW grape boycott during his visit to San Francisco.

Caught in a crowd, she was struck by a police officer's baton, suffered three broken ribs and a pulverized spleen, and required more than a dozen blood transfusions. She reached a settlement with the city to resolve her claims.

After a long recovery, Huerta shifted focus and hit the road to campaign for women's rights and encourage Latinas to run for office.

She also campaigned for Democratic Presidents Bill Clinton, Obama and Joe Biden. California Gov. Gavin Newsom considered her a close friend.

Emilio Huerta, one of Dolores' 13 children, once pointed to his mom's courage during an interview, saying it was one of the most significant drivers of her life's work.

"To dedicate your life to building a dream of a union for farmworkers, to take on agriculture, which to this today is still a powerhouse. ... That took a lot of guts," he had said.

Huerta learned the art of lobbying politicians. She secured financial aid for the unemployed and disability insurance for California farmworkers. She spoke out against toxic pesticides, pushed for amnesty for immigrants in the country illegally and fought federal guest worker programs.

By directing nationwide boycotts of grapes and lettuce in the early 1970s, she helped to create the economic pressure and political climate necessary for California to pass what would be the first state law recognizing farmworkers' right to collective bargaining.

Her work earned her many accolades. She was the first Latina to be inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame and she's received nine honorary doctorates from U.S. universities.

Schools are named in her honor in California, Texas and Colorado. Her image graces many murals, and streets bear her name, but she didn't always win favor with more liberal activists.

In 2016 when she endorsed Hillary Clinton instead of Sen. Bernie Sanders of Vermont in the presidential Democratic primary, actor Rosario Dawson — a Sanders supporter who played Huerta in a 2014 movie about César Chavez — said Huerta had become "an instrument of the establishment."

Guitarist Carlos Santana later oversaw a documentary based on Huerta's life after the pair had a late-night conversation about music while in Hawaii. Santana, a Miles Davis fan, discovered Huerta was a huge fan of bebop legend Charlie Parker.

"I even met Charlie Parker. That's my claim to fame," Huerta joked during an interview with The Associated Press when she was 87.

It was her work as a leader within the labor movement that reverberated through the U.S. and beyond, making her name synonymous with civil rights. In the 2025 documentary "Seeds of Change: The Life and Legacy of Dolores Huerta," she recounted her decades-long journey in activism and talked about the inspiration she drew from the women's movement during the 1970s, saying it provided validation that she too could stand on the stage with other labor leaders.

"I hope that my legacy will be one of an organizer that organized to fight injustice," she said in the film. "We want to give our children an inheritance of justice, right, not jewels or property or money because they'll just fight over it. So the main thing to do is to give them this love for justice — and that is the greatest gift I think that any of us can have."

A spider, when knocked off a wall, doesn’t fall for long. Within milliseconds, it spins a soup of proteins into sturdy climbing gear. Using a thin, shimmering strand of silk, the spider hauls itself back up.

The strand is tougher than Kevlar, the synthetic fiber in bike tires and bulletproof vests, and stronger than steel. It’s flexible and biocompatible with human bodies. Scientists dream of making spider silk into high-performing materials for seat belts, helmets, naval materials, and even scaffolds to help repair brain cells. But attempts to make spider silk in the lab have fallen short because researchers don’t know enough about how a spider spins “silk dope,” a liquid blend of proteins, water, and salt, from its silk glands into a remarkable solid.

Mapping out the molecular mechanisms behind the process is “really the holy grail” for the field, said Gregory Holland, a chemist and spectroscopist at San Diego State University. Recently, he led research that described how the molecules in liquid spider dope reorganize into semiliquid droplets called condensates before turning into solid silk fiber. The result is an atomic-level look into the spider’s silk factory.

The findings are also a practical guide to the formation of condensates, which nature has used repeatedly to organize molecules for genetic processes and for the creation of impressively strong biological materials.

“It’s becoming clear that [condensates are] a common strategy that’s being used across a range of different organisms,” said Matthew Harrington, a biological materials scientist at McGill University who was not involved in the study. By unpacking how nature uses condensates, scientists hope to re-create some of its most rugged materials.

Spiders are incredible natural factories. They can spin up to seven types of silks, and the same spider can often spin several of them. Aciniform silk wraps up insects for later snacking. Rubbery flagelliform silk reinforces the capture spiral to absorb shock. Dragline silk anchors webs and spiders to walls. Each type of silk has different properties depending on the species and related to how its molecules are assembled. And silks of the same type can have slightly different properties depending on the species.

In 1907, scientists discovered that spider silk is made of long, modular proteins, later named spidroins, which are in the same group of fiber proteins as collagen and keratin. More than half a century later, X-rays started to reveal the fiber’s underlying structure. The silk is made of flat sheets of protein, known as beta sheets, that are embedded in a less ordered, non-crystalline matrix.

In the early 2000s, as they inspected the spidroins in solution, researchers didn’t see organized, fixed structures like those seen in most proteins. Instead, the spidroins featured intrinsically disordered regions that fluttered around, forming and re-forming various shapes. Researchers then started to untangle how this soup of unstructured proteins could organize into strong, solid fibers.

One early theory suggested that proteins in the dope start to align with one another, as in a crystal, while remaining dynamic, in a transitional state known as “liquid crystal.” Another considered spider silk to be like silkworm silk, which has proteins organized into micelles, or small spheres, which then compress into silk fiber.

Then, in 2007, researchers noted that spidroins engineered in the lab formed liquidlike globs. Soon these kinds of structures would become inescapable in modern biology. In 2009, scientists at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Massachusetts and the Max Planck Institute in Germany discovered droplets of aggregated RNA molecules in fertilized worm eggs. These were condensates — liquid- or gel-like bubbles of concentrated proteins suspended in solution. Though observed for more than a century, condensates started to be seen as a critical organizational tool for molecular processes across life.

The researchers realized that condensates concentrate molecules, pre-organizing them into a flexible microenvironment without locking them in place like a solid. “Because they’re fluid, they’re malleable,” Harrington said, “so you [i.e., biology] can kind of shape them into whatever you want.”

A time-lapse video shows an orb-weaving European garden spider (Araneus diadematus) spinning its web.

More research showed that living cells use condensates everywhere, to organize DNA, regulate gene expression, and package proteins. At a macro scale, they help species build interesting materials. They help mold squid beaks into a gradient of stiff parts. They form strong adhesives that allow mussels to attach themselves to rocks, and marine cement that sandcastle worms use to build protective tubes.

“It’s been a hot area in biology,” Holland said.

Though researchers had described condensate-like blobs forming in lab-made spidroins more than a decade earlier, they weren’t thought of as condensates until 2020, when Keiji Numata, a biomaterials chemist at Kyoto University, suggested that spider silk undergoes a condensate phase during its transition from liquid dope to solid thread.

Condensates form by “liquid-liquid phase separation,” a process through which molecules in a liquid can start to interact weakly with one another, easily forming bonds and breaking them, as a result of a stimulus such as a change in pH, chemicals, or temperature. Numata proposed that phosphate in the ampulla, the middle section of a spider’s silk glands, might initiate these interactions. But no one had described the atomic-scale mechanisms that drive their formation until Hannah Johnson, then a graduate student in Holland’s lab, decided to take a look.

Every few months throughout 2023 and 2024, a package of female black widow spiders arrived at the San Diego State University chemistry department. The arachnids, jet black with a dash of red, cultivate a powerful venom that can be painful to humans. Except for that minor inconvenience, they make good subjects for studying spider silk. Their dragline silk spidroin proteins are some of the best-characterized in all spiders. And they’re available year-round, unlike some spiders that spin their webs seasonally.

Six times over the course of two weeks, Johnson donned two pairs of protective gloves. Over the course of an hour, she and several students “silked” black widow spiders, which had been knocked unconscious by carbon dioxide. With tweezers, the scientists gently pulled the strand of silk from the spider’s spinnerets — the abdominal spigots that fibers emerge from — and reeled it in by winding it around a vial with a spinning motor.

“The spiders who give a ton of silk are our favorites,” Johnson said, “but I have a soft spot for the rebels who don’t want to cooperate.” Throughout the process, the researchers fed the black widows a cocktail of amino acids (like “protein shakes,” Johnson said) enriched with particular isotopes that they could detect in the lab.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy can map the structure of a molecule. It sends out radio frequencies to disturb the nuclei inside atoms, which then move in a way characteristic of the type of atom. Johnson used NMR on the liquid spider dope, as well as on condensates collected from the spiders’ ampullae. Meanwhile Kevin Chalek, then a postdoctoral researcher in Holland’s lab (now a researcher at the University of California, Los Angeles), mapped the atomic structure of the proteins in the solid fiber.

“We were trying to bridge the gap between what is happening when the protein is inside the spider and when it’s outside the spider,” Chalek said. “These are the same protein, [so] how can they be so different? And what exactly is happening between the two?”

Johnson observed that after she added phosphate to the liquid silk dope to start the condensation process, side groups on two amino acids (arginine and tyrosine) in the intrinsically disordered regions of the spidroin proteins started to interact weakly with one another. This interaction displaced water, organizing the disordered proteins into a denser, gel-like material — a condensate. The interaction persisted in the solid fiber, suggesting that the amino acids serve as “molecular stickers” that guide the disordered proteins into condensates and ultimately into fibers.

Others on the team used AlphaFold, an artificial intelligence tool that models protein structures, and molecular dynamics software to predict this process computationally. Researchers know that spider silk’s strength comes from the sturdy beta-sheet protein structures in its final fiber. The team’s simulations showed that these amino acids line up right up at the bends in the beta sheets, suggesting that they are helping to guide the spindroins into their final structure.

“Understanding how those side groups interact and how they can develop structure that’s greater than the sum of its individual parts … is fantastic,” said Chris Holland, a biologist at the University of Sheffield who was not involved in the study and is not related to Gregory Holland. And they used real spiders, instead of synthetic silks, “which is brilliant.”

Their findings are “direct evidence” that condensates can form from native silk, which is “quite significant,” said Daiwen Yang, a biologist at the National University of Singapore who was not part of the study.

Their description of condensates doesn’t rule out earlier theories of spider silk formation. Gregory Holland, who had previously shown the presence of micelles in native spider silk, suggested that all three — micelles, condensates, and liquid crystals — could be stages of the same phase transition. Micelles that form in the liquid dope prevent the messy, disordered proteins from clumping and getting tangled. The proteins can also pre-assemble into condensates, or align into structures like liquid crystals before they are released from spinnerets as highly ordered fibers. But the actual process is likely “more dynamic and heterogenous” than sequential, Gregory Holland said, with several of these structures forming in the mixture at the same time.

Harrington agreed. “I don’t think these are mutually exclusive,” he said. “You might have different sorts of phase transitions that are occurring at different stages of the processing.”

Still, there’s more to understand about how these interactions add up on this factory line. There are likely other significant amino acid interactions that the researchers haven’t yet been able to map out in detail. They also want to look for triggers that initiate the process beyond phosphate. “There could be a range of other things that are driving structural changes in these proteins,” Chris Holland said. “We don’t know all of the switches yet.”

Describing the entirety of the intricate silk-spinning process will take a lot of effort, but the payoff could be huge. Spiders produce small amounts of usable silk compared to silkworms, which have been reared for their cocoons for more than 4,000 years. It takes thousands of silkworms to make a silk shirt; it would take around 1 million spiders, in part because they don’t produce cocoons that can be easily processed. There is a more practical reason: Their cannibalistic nature makes them difficult to keep because each spider needs its own container.

Producing spider silk in the lab could result in a new range of strong yet flexible materials. Kevlar, a synthetic fiber, is currently our toughest material, but it requires processing at very high temperatures and acidic conditions. “Here, you have an organism like a spider or a mussel,” Harrington said, “which is basically making a superior material under ambient conditions in seawater or in just plain air.”

Humans haven’t figured out how to match those organisms yet. When scientists synthesize spider silk in the lab, using the exact sequence of amino acids to build a spidroin protein, the resulting silk is not as strong as natural silk. “Something is happening there that we’re also struggling to mimic,” Chalek said. He thinks additional strength must be provided by something beyond the protein’s beta sheets, arising from other aspects of silk’s structure, or perhaps from parts of the spidroin protein frequently left out of synthetic silk solutions.

“I think once we really understand those blueprints,” Chris Holland said, “we’ll be able to do more stuff with silk.”

Analysts polled by StreetAccount expected $32.32 billion. The growth rate in the June quarter was 42%.

As of Wednesday's close, Microsoft shares were up about 10% year to date, trailing the S&P 500 index, which has gained 13% over the same period.

During the fiscal first quarter, Microsoft said it would cut 4,800 jobs, and followed up by announcing on Monday that it's offering buyouts to up to 60,000 employees, as it strives to balance a costly AI build-out with capital efficiency. Microsoft also established, during the quarter, an AI implementation unit and revealed a Copilot AI "super app" with coding abilities.

#310. AI execs may be too out of touch to build for the general public

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Former Yahoo CEO and early Google engineer Marissa Mayer is catching a lot of flak for how she uses AI. In an interview about her new app Dazzle, an AI assistant that learns from your camera roll, Mayer poses what she believes is an impressive flex for the product: It helped her come up with three bullet points about her 10-year-old daughter for a birthday presentation at school, describing her as “empathetic, creative, and an animal lover.”

It’s a vague description, like a generic horoscope — it could probably describe a lot of 10-year-olds.

Some onlookers, like progressive activist Leah Greenberg, marveled that “the fact that Marissa Mayer would voluntarily disclose this to a reporter is a pretty good demonstration of how deeply the culture of Silicon Valley has diverged from the rest of humanity.”

"She thought it was perfect."www.nytimes.com/2026/10/25/s…

Perhaps Mayer drew so much backlash because we have repeatedly observed sheltered billionaires suggest that we outsource child-rearing — one of life’s greatest tests of patience, humility, and kindness — to a computer that is incapable of understanding human emotion.

We’ve been primed for this frustration. Last year, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman told Bored Ape owner and late night host Jimmy Fallon on national television, “I cannot imagine … figuring out how to raise a newborn without ChatGPT.” The public didn’t love that comment, either, but Altman wasn’t fazed by the negative feedback. A few months ago, he said on X that a “cool use case” of ChatGPT Work would be creating a podcast that talks about your kids’ upcoming birthdays or soccer games and “explain[s] your kids’ interests.” (He was brutally ratioed with comments like, “What if you just talked to your children?”)

After publication, Mayer defended herself in a long X post. She claims she was not relying on Dazzle to explain her child to her, but rather, she was testing what her prototype could do — it resurfaced photos of “forgotten kindergarten projects,” like a “snake devenomer” or “sandcat playground,” illustrating her daughter’s creativity and care for animals.

Fair enough. But Mayer remarks in the same interview that people could also use her AI to outsource the mental load of “figuring out the theme for your child’s birthday party.” She says she’s used her app to plan a playdate for her daughter, book family activities on vacation, and decide what to be for Halloween. She even proposes that her AI assistant could coordinate plans with her husband’s AI assistant. When the reporter asks her if it’s depressing to imagine this “frictionless future … planned by AI,” Mayer replies, “I haven’t really thought that through.”

These leaders seem to write off the naysayers as part of a small, angry fringe group. Yet a Pew Research Center study reports that 50% of U.S. adults think that AI will reduce people’s ability to form meaningful relationships. Only 5% of respondents said AI would make it easier to build these connections.

Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg, however, joins his Silicon Valley kin in envisioning a world where our social needs are met via AI.

“The average American I think has, it’s fewer than three friends, three people they’d consider friends, and the average person has demand for meaningfully more, I think it’s like 15 friends,” Zuckerberg said last year on a podcast. “The average person wants more connectivity, connection than they have.”

Zuckerberg predicts that it will become more socially acceptable to outsource our emotional needs to AI therapists or even AI romantic partners. “There are all these things that are better about physical connections when you can have them,” he added. “But the reality is that people … feel more alone a lot of the time than they would like.”

Zuckerberg goes on to explain how virtual-reality embodiments of AI avatars are only growing more realistic. Meta seems to be more keen on improving our relationships with these approximations of humans, rather than between the actual billions of humans who use its platforms each day.

Maybe part of this stems from the culture of the tech industry, which prizes efficiency. People try to biohack their own bodies by optimizing their sleep schedules with health trackers or injecting unregulated “peptide stacks” to maximize their productivity.

If you’re building electric vehicles or computer chips, then sure, efficiency is a clear north star. But our relationships with other people are not a tech product that we’ve been challenged to ship with as little capital as possible. Human relationships are inherently messy, but our connections with one another can be what gives our lives meaning. When we delegate those interactions to computers, we surrender the difficult, yet fulfilling experience of being people.
