#155. Federal Reserve leaves interest rates unchanged despite rising inflation

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Federal Reserve voted Wednesday to keep interest rates unchanged, its final monetary policy decision before the midterm elections.

The decision to hold rates steady came despite inflation that jumped from a 3.4% annual rate in August to 3.7% in September.

Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh also announced that the central bank will reduce the number of meetings at which it votes on interest rates from eight to seven next year and by two in 2028. Warsh said that the seismic change to the schedule would help improve the “quality” of its decisions.

A major driver of that inflation is the soaring cost of gas, diesel and crude oil. These higher prices have hit consumers hard, and many economists say the spillover effect of rising gas prices has started to show up across the broader economy.

In its post-decision statement, the Fed acknowledged the problem that inflation is causing but said it needs more data before moving forward with a rate hike.

“Inflation remains elevated. The accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee’s 2 percent goal,” it said.

The Fed’s preferred inflation gauge, the personal consumption expenditures price index, will not be released until Thursday; it is expected to touch 3.7%.

“This suggests that the Fed might not be done tightening and future hikes will depend on the incoming data,” economists at Morgan Stanley said. In central bank speak, “tightening” refers to raising rates.

“As we widen the frame to survey the broad economic and financial picture, real activity is clearly pointing in the right direction,” Warsh said in a post-meeting news conference. “That’s good news, but inflation is too high.”

“Energy and related prices have moved higher again in recent weeks, and our job is to ensure that relative price changes in some sectors do not broaden out across the economy,” he added.

Wednesday’s vote was not unanimous, unlike September’s 12-0 vote to hike rates, the first time since 2023 that the central bank had raised its benchmark rate.

Two officials, governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, dissented in favor of a rate hike. The dissent by Cook, whom President Donald Trump is attempting to fire, was her first ever.

An interest rate hike could have added slightly more pressure to already beleaguered consumers.

Voters have consistently told pollsters that rising prices, especially in the housing and energy sectors, are their primary concerns as they cast their ballots.

The most recent NBC News poll found Trump’s approval rating at 27% for his handling of the cost of living and 29% for his handling of the Iran war.

Two-thirds of Americans said the soaring gas prices triggered by Trump’s Iran war were either a “very serious” or “somewhat serious” problem. More than half of respondents said current economic conditions had caused them to change what kinds of groceries they buy.

Warsh said the midterms and politics “played no role in the decision” to keep rates where they are.

Asked about Trump’s recent claims that members of the Fed’s board and rate-setting committee were hostile to the president, Warsh defended his colleagues.

“We just came out of a heck of a family fight, serious, sober discussion,” he said. “I think we came to the right policy decision for this moment, and I’m honored to be here and honored to be the chairman of this group.”

The central bank also acknowledged the economic anxiety gripping much of the country.

“Economic activity is expanding at a strong pace,” the rate-setting Federal Open Market Committee said. “While uncertainty remains elevated owing, in part, to geopolitical developments, domestic spending has been resilient.”

Wednesday’s announcement and the Fed’s acknowledgment that inflation remains well above its goal set the stage for a rate increase during December’s monetary policy meeting.

Charcuterie is a spread of meats and cheeses typically displayed on a wooden board or a marble or acrylic platter. Traditional charcuterie boards include various types of meats and cheeses, but you may also see crudités boards consisting of vegetables and dips, or dessert boards commonly seen during the holiday season.

Though sometimes used interchangeably, there are a few distinct differences between charcuterie and cheese boards. A traditional charcuterie board must include meats (or charcuterie, the French term for a category of prepared meat products from pork, like ham, sausage, pâtés, salami and confit), according to Simms. A cheese board, on the other hand, includes just what its name suggests: a variety of cheeses and other accompaniments like dips and crackers.

“So, if there is no charcuterie involved, it is not considered a charcuterie board,” says Simms.

Charcuterie boards are typically made of wood or marble. Most experts prefer wooden boards for their durability and solid base. Some of them also have handles for practical transport and storage, and side edges to keep treats from falling off, says Rener. Remember that you need to clean and disinfect your board after every use to avoid unappealing smells — you can wash most with soap and water. You’ll also need to condition your wooden board monthly to prevent it from drying out and cracking, says Rener.

Marble boards are another great option because they’re typically scratch-resistant and have a sleek design, making your spread more appealing to guests. Marble is also cooling, so it can keep food fresher for longer compared to wooden boards, experts say.

Simms recommends playing with a variety of textures and flavors, including sweet, salty and crunchy. Typically, charcuterie boards include a mix of meats and cheeses that complement each other, plus a few extras to add texture or sweetness. If you’re a beginner, work with two to four simple varieties of meats and cheeses, like mild salami and Brie, says Cheyenne Nichols, chef and owner of Finest Cravings catering company.

Most experts agree that a charcuterie board will always need some sort of meat. Some of the best ones are prosciutto, coppa, salami and pate, says Simms, who works with cured meat producers like Olympia Provisions, La Quercia, Casella’s, Coro and Fra’mani.

You’ll want to include three to five different cheeses, ideally options that differ in texture (soft or spreadable, semi firm or hard), according to Simms. Your cheese should also vary in milk-type, including cow, goat, sheep and buffalo, she says.

“You’ll want really soft cheese, like a goat cheese or a Brie or Camembert, to serve as the anchor for the board, and then a crumbly cheese of some sort, whether that’s Parmesan or an aged cheddar,” says Rener. Some of my experts’ other favorites are Gouda (soft cheese) and Boursin garlic and herb (spreadable cheese). You may also want to include a “funky” or “stinky” cheese, like blue cheese, to provide a unique taste, says Rener.

Certain cheeses can also add an aesthetic value to your board. “A manchego is great because there are so many different fun ways to slice cheeses like that,” says Rener. She also loves including wine-soaked cheese because the rind adds beauty and texture.

“We love going to our neighborhood cheese shop and getting samples from our local cheesemonger — this is the best way to discover fun, new cheese,” says Simms.

If you want to incorporate fruits, highlight seasonal ones. For example, include peaches if it’s summer, cherries in the spring or pears during winter, according to Rener. There are also a few must-have fruits to include regardless of season: grapes, strawberries, blackberries and raspberries.

Rener also recommends adding two to three types of nuts on your board, including a neutral one like cashews or almonds, and bolder ones like caramelized pecans, spicy pistachios and honey roasted peanuts. “I go crazy for a good candied nut on my charcuterie board,” she says. “Skip the boring nuts and try something new for your board.”

This is where you can add anything your heart desires, primarily if you want additional textures or flavors, or to fill in any gaps. Local honey or honeycomb, seasonal jam and grainy mustard are great spreadables to feature, according to Simms, who also loves pickled cucumbers and olives.

Most people will include jams like a sweet chutney to complement fresher cheeses like manchego, or a savory pepper jam for more buttery cheeses like Brie, says Rener. You can also add crackers, a baguette, crostini and breadsticks for some crunch.

The beauty of a charcuterie board is that you can include whatever type of food you want, and however much you want. But if you’re not sure just how much to add, there are a few parameters to work with, experts say. If you’re going the traditional meat and cheese route, aim for a minimum of two to three ounces of meat and cheese per person for an appetizer portion, and double that amount if you’re not serving other food and it will be more of a meal, says Simms.

My experts suggest working in odd numbers: Choose three or five selections and pick foods diverse in texture (soft, semi firm, hard) and milk-type (cow, goat, sheep).

#416. I Put a Bunch of AI Agents in Charge of a Fantasy Soccer Team. They’re Not Better Than Humans—Yet

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I have consulted my AI-powered crystal ball, and it’s telling me that frontier models are going to get a lot better at making predictions in the near future. Just ask my Premier League fantasy team.

A few weeks ago, I deployed a small horde of AI agents to craft and manage an experimental roster. Given the proliferation of AI agents into pretty much every aspect of our lives, I wanted to know if a swarm of bots could put together a winning team.

I first looked at the potential for AI here a few years ago, playing around with a tool from the UK’s Turing Institute that selected teams after modeling previous fantasy league data. Its suggestions were remarkably bonkers—it didn’t incorporate real-world information, like a player’s injury or a team’s change in management.

This time, while on a flight to visit family, I asked Codex for help. First, I fed the model a couple of relevant research papers. Then, I asked it to build a harness—instructions and code that guides different agents through various steps—that would check recent news and run a model to make predictions.

It quickly came up with a setup that seemed to collate and analyze way more information than I ever would have. And yet I was surprised by how dumb the initial team suggestions were. Leaving Erling Haaland on the bench? Really, Codex?

I then asked Codex to create a team of agents that did different things: several that read news and blog posts; others that analyzed recent results; more that ran models; and a bunch that critiqued suggestions. This seemed to significantly improve the suggestions. I tried increasing the number of agents to more than 100, hoping this would make the system smarter still, but it didn’t change much besides running up my API bill.

Still, the improvement from a few years ago was striking. According to Haifeng Xu, a computer scientist at the University of Chicago who studies the uses of AI in market situations, newer models may be even better at making predictions because they’re able to consume and analyze huge amounts of real-world information, and they’re able to orchestrate more complex systems for their guesses.

“AI is just getting smarter in understanding the world—I feel like that's the fundamental reason they’re improving,” Xu tells me.

Late last year, Xu was marveling at the growing popularity of prediction markets like Kalshi and Polymarket. He wondered how the best commercial models might fare on those markets, so alongside several colleagues, he began testing their forecasting skills.

They had models guess, for example, who would win the NBA Championship and who President Trump would meet with.

“Very quickly we started to realize that models did really well,” Xu tells me. The models were sometimes able to do better than the average person without any special instructions or guidance, he says. In their testing, GPT-5 was the best at making predictions, followed by Grok 4, Claude Sonnet, Gemini, and Llama.

The team went on to develop Prophet Arena, a benchmark that measures a model’s ability to make predictions. It’s one of a number of benchmarks designed to test AI’s future-gazing capabilities; another, Prediction Arena, from researchers at Harvard University and a company called Arcada Labs, recently gave frontier models $10,000 to wager on prediction markets over 57 days. The models consistently lost money in their experiment, but the researchers saw evidence of them improving consistently over time.

Xu tells me that AI can most effectively make predictions using a custom harness, and notes that having agents with unique tasks—reading the news, collecting other data, and analyzing the whole system, for instance—seems to be the most effective approach.

Interestingly, AI still often lags behind very skilled experts known as superforecasters. Xu explains that, although humans cannot consume as much information, their understanding of the real world—that is, of the dynamics behind a news story—is deeper.

That said, there’s growing evidence that the human superforecaster’s edge is eroding. A contest called the Metaculus Cup recently showed that AI can sometimes beat even the best human predictors. And a benchmark called ForecastBench could provide further evidence that AI can match human skills when the results of an ongoing contest conclude soon. In fact, I think we can expect the big labs to turn their attention to future-gazing as a powerful new way to measure progress in machine intelligence.

All of these predictive experiments seem to suggest that, like math, coding, and law, industries predicated on trading may soon be upended by AI. Perhaps the best human traders will take on a new kind of role: supervising and orchestrating AI agents capable of divining the future far better than they can.

For the time being, my AI’s suggestions still don’t seem all that impressive. Its team hasn’t outscored the average over the past weeks. I certainly won’t be using any of its suggestions in my own selections.

But AI will no doubt get better, perhaps making it all too easy to dominate fantasy league pretty soon. I’m kind of hoping it won’t, though, as it would spoil the fun of trying to earn bragging rights by winning all by yourself.

Pines had photographed CEOs before, and he was racking his brain for ways to make the visuals for Holmes' Forbes cover story memorable.

He never could have predicted that his shot of Holmes would become the symbol of the now-disgraced CEO's meteoric rise and spectacular fall, not to mention imitated on TV series, put on billboards, and discussed in a popular documentary.

Twelve years later, Pines' photo of Holmes has re-entered the zeitgeist thanks to Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim's hit documentary, "You Can See Everything," which chronicles the life of Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, in the final weeks before Holmes reports to prison for defrauding investors.

Pines' famous shot is brought up several times in the doc. At one point, while having her body scanned to create an AI hologram, Holmes breaks down, recalling how her image has become a symbol of duplicity in the press.

This isn't the first time Pines' photo of Holmes has gone viral. The shot was used on the poster for Alex Gibney's 2019 documentary on Holmes, "The Inventor: Out for Blood in Silicon Valley." The shot is also re-created in the 2022 Hulu miniseries "The Dropout," which stars Amanda Seyfried as Holmes. But Pines said "You Can See Everything" has brought an even larger wave attention to his work.

"I posted on my Instagram the entire shoot in a flipbook motion piece, and it has a million views," Pines said. "My posts don't get anywhere near that."

When Pines arrived on set that day in 2014, he knew he wanted to take photographs that functioned as both a portrait of a young founder and a showcase of her invention.

#570. Scaling Hierarchical Networks

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since Richard Sutton’s influential essay in 2019, the concept of the bitter lesson has guided much of modern progress in AI. This concept espouses general methods that leverage computation and learn with scale, as free of human-engineered priors as possible. Our interpretation is that, fundamentally, intelligence is about learning from raw data: autonomously finding patterns, building abstractions, and developing meaning from messy low-level inputs, ideally end-to-end and as data-efficiently as possible. As AI advances into complex domains such as science, robotics, and biology – where data is scarce and often difficult for humans to understand, let alone engineer for – internalizing this philosophy to develop more general learning algorithms is more important than ever.

Language models are perhaps AI’s biggest success story today, and largely embrace the bitter lesson. Their abilities are largely attributable to scaling a few simple algorithms such as the Transformer architecture, next-token prediction, reinforcement learning, and test-time computation. However, they still rely on hand-crafted encoding steps such as tokenizers, which severely hinder their ability to learn end-to-end from the underlying data. Towards unlocking another step change of scaling, we believe that developing byte-level language models is foundational for AI progress – not just for improving standalone LLMs, but also as a litmus test for general learning methods. If we can’t even learn language well from characters and bytes, what right do we have to make progress on the next frontier?

In 2025, we developed the concept of hierarchical networks (H-Nets), which not only addressed tokenization in language models, but took a step toward even more general-purpose architectures that could learn from raw data across different modalities. We’ve since significantly improved the technology of H-Nets and are excited to share their capabilities! In this series of two posts, we’ll dig into new results and discuss the implications.

In this first post of a two-part series, we’ll explore scaling laws for H-Nets and their qualitative behaviors.

In the next post, we’ll do a deeper exploration of a larger range of models to understand better why H-Nets can be so much more efficient for language modeling.

Altogether, our results provide evidence that H-Nets are a foundational architecture that truly aligns with the bitter lesson, learning meaning directly from raw data and significantly improving efficiency and scalability. This research agenda represents a major opportunity to advance how AI can learn from all data, and we couldn’t be more excited to push the frontier!

We introduced hierarchical networks (paper, blog) last year as a new end-to-end architecture that models multiple levels of abstraction in data, showing promise in language modeling and beyond. In a nutshell:

Hierarchical networks (H-Nets) compress data through a recursive, data-dependent dynamic chunking process.

The terminology was inspired by the existing concept of chunking in cognitive psychology, which is an essential part of human intelligence. We believe it is also necessary for the frontier of artificial intelligence.

In the context of language modeling, the idea is to build new learning primitives that allow models to recursively chunk low-level data into higher-level meaning: characters into words, words into phrases, phrases into concepts.

In the context of this post, for the most part, one can think of hierarchical networks as replacing tokenization. Instead of pre-processing data from bytes into tokens (fixed chunks of bytes), H-Nets perform the chunking dynamically directly inside the model. Furthermore, the signature characterization of H-Nets is that they hierarchically perform this chunking end-to-end. In contrast, standard tokenized language models and even other alternatives (some of which we’ll see in Post II) employ multiple stages of training, and the process of chunking / tokenizing the data is performed entirely offline.

Language models don’t read text directly but instead take as input a sequence of tokens from a fixed vocabulary. A natural vocabulary is the 256 byte values, producing one token per UTF-8 byte. Byte-level sequences, however, are long and expensive for standard language models to process. Tokenization aims to alleviate this problem by compressing the byte sequence into frequently occurring chunks. The most common approach, Byte Pair Encoding (BPE), starts from the 256 bytes and repeatedly merges the most frequent pair of adjacent tokens on a fixed training corpus, until the vocabulary reaches a set size (e.g. 128k for Llama-3). On our data mix, each BPE token covers about 4.57 bytes, making sequences much shorter than at the byte level. However, this compression is fixed, and tokenization remains one of the last parts of the language-modeling pipeline that is designed by hand rather than learned.

We’ll see in this post series that even in the narrower context of replacing tokenization, the end-to-end property of H-Nets provides real performance gains. Beyond tokenization, H-Nets have broad utility as a foundational architecture:

Figure 1 illustrates a 2-stage H-Net and defines the main components of the model: the encoder, main network, and decoder. We sometimes call the encoder/decoder together the outer networks and the main model the inner network.

Throughout this post, we show results with a much improved version of H-Nets, which we plan to release. We’ll comment more on differences from the earlier architecture later in this post.

Carefully controlled H-Nets - varying only the tokenizer - show a serious efficiency gain over tokenized language models. Expanding the H-Net design space can only improve its relative advantage.

As with many architecture papers, the original paper on hierarchical networks focused on tuning language models of particular fixed sizes and data horizons (e.g. ~1.3B-parameter models on 100B tokens from FineWeb-Edu have been very common). Although they looked very promising, a missing piece was the lack of compute-optimal scaling laws, now a standard way to measure pretraining innovations. For example, Kimi’s popular innovations on Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) and attention residuals measured compute multipliers of 1.16x and 1.25x, respectively.

In order to validate the gains of our improved H-Net architecture, we first set out to build a modern LLM scaling ladder, and then replaced its tokenization components with a carefully controlled hierarchical network.

This recipe directly showed stronger results on validation loss (1.3x), with gains increasing on subdomains such as code (2x), as well as improving out of distribution (2x on evaluation, and up to 5x on out-of-domain code).

Since byte-level and BPE models process the same data at different granularities, extra care is needed to keep the methodology controlled.

Data mixture: Our training dataset consisted of 70% English, 20% code, 5% math, and 5% multilingual, measured by the number of bytes.

Data processing: Every sample is processed as both bytes and BPE tokens, splitting at the same boundaries; both byte- and BPE- processed datasets therefore contain exactly the same text with aligned document boundaries.

Metric: We use the standard bits-per-byte (bpb) metric for measuring next-token prediction loss in a way normalized between different tokenizers.

In-corpus BPE tokenizer: LLMs generally use off-the-shelf BPE tokenizers; an advantage of H-Nets is that they learn their chunking directly on the training distribution. To be extra careful, we also trained a BPE tokenizer on the corpus. We used this for the BPE scaling laws below, which ended up resulting in about a 1.1x compute gain on both validation loss and downstream evals (so the H-Net would have an even greater compute multiplier against a standard off-the-shelf tokenizer!).

The BPE tokenizer’s vocab size is 128k, matching the Llama-3 tokenizer that we were working off of. The in-corpus tokenizer compressed the dataset slightly more, increasing the average BPE token length from 4.45 (Llama-3 tokenizer) to 4.57. Measured in tokens of the in-corpus BPE tokenizer, the training dataset comprised 64.7% English, 23.7% code, 6.1% math, and 5.5% multilingual.

For our baseline, we fit a well-tuned scaling law for a standard BPE Transformer. To do this, we constructed a scaling ladder, following modern scaling recipes such as Kimi and MAI, fully defining a family of models by the number of layers L. We set the hidden dimension D to a multiple of 128 such that the aspect ratio D/L is approximately 75. For all models, we use multi-head attention with the number of heads equal to the number of layers and a fixed head dimension of 128. For the MLPs, we use SwiGLU layers with the intermediate dimension set to 3.6D, again rounding to the nearest multiple of 128.1

We then fit compute-optimal scaling laws with the standard iso-FLOP sweep methodology.

There are so many moving parts: how many stages of chunking, the respective compression rates, the architecture configurations of each stage (outer vs. inner networks), and the hyperparameters for each component.

We decided to investigate a narrower setting: how do H-Nets compare against BPE models, if restricted to being interpreted as purely replacing the tokenization portion of the language model?

To do this, we converted each rung of the BPE ladder to a byte-level 1-stage H-Net. A 1-stage H-Net can be viewed as a standard language model with learned tokenization: the inner network operates over chunks (which play the role of tokens) and the outer networks perform the role of dynamic tokenization, encoding bytes into chunks and decoding them back. Training a larger hierarchical network following an open-source recipe, varying only the architecture with two stages of dynamic chunking, led to >10x compute and data efficiency improvements

We aimed to scale H-Nets up larger than the scaling ladders and the earlier versions (the first paper trained up to the equivalent of a 1.3B parameter model for 100B tokens). As a baseline, we chose SmolLM3, a 3B Transformer LLM released by Hugging Face with a fully documented training recipe. Because the SmolLM3 team released the model architecture, the training datamix, and several checkpoints throughout the training run, we were able to reproduce the recipe and compare the models throughout the training process - including past the horizon we trained to!

Data: SmolLM3’s pretraining consisted of three phases with 8T/2T/1T tokens, using the Llama-3 tokenizer, for a total of 11.1T tokens. As our target horizon was much shorter, we used only the stage-1 data mixture. We made a best-effort reproduction built from the same public sources. Controlling for data is subtle; most training recipes report their data mixture as a fraction (e.g. SmolLM3’s is 73.2 / 12.1 / 12.0 / 2.7 for English web / multilingual / code / math), but this is usually in tokens. To try to control for data properly, we decided to match the underlying byte-level mixture (just as we did for the scaling ladders), which ended up being 76.5 / 10.9 / 10.3 / 2.2.

Optimization: We match the baseline identically without additional tuning for the H-Net: AdamW (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1, clipping 1.0, peak LR 2e-4, same WSD schedule with 5B token-equivalents of warmup. We cooled down for the last 15% of training; one difference from the baseline is that SmolLM3’s cooldown phase appeared during its 3rd pretraining stage (after 10T tokens), with a different data mixture.

Architecture: We built a two-stage hierarchical network over bytes.

Unlike the scaling ladders, we focused more on aligning with the philosophy of hierarchical networks: allowing the model to perform dynamic chunking in multiple stages, ideally following the semantics of the data. This made matching all axes to the baseline impossible, as parameters and compute get spent in very different ways. We aimed to control for FLOPs/byte, so that every minibatch of training sees the same underlying data and spends the same amount of compute as the baseline.

The H-Net’s total parameter count ended up at 3.67B, against 3.08B for the baseline. The difference is mostly the encoders and decoders plus the four extra main-stage layers; the byte embedding is tiny.

We evaluated using benchmarks and the same evaluation suite as the official SmolLM3 results, among their set of “Reasoning & Commonsense” and “Knowledge & Understanding” evaluations.

Figure 7 shows both models’ benchmark scores over training. The H-Net reaches SmolLM3’s final average score after ~1T tokens compared to ~11T tokens. Since the two models are compute-matched per byte, the saving in training FLOPs is proportional.

Despite our effort to control for data and compute fairly, in hindsight we realized it’s still not obvious what a proper methodology should be. For example, let’s look at English vs. code: the reason SmolLM3’s token mix of 73.2% / 12.0% respectively becomes 76.5% / 10.3% at the byte level is because tokenizers compress English more (see Figure 8b), which itself is an artifact of BPE tokenization (it spends more effort compressing more frequently appearing data, so it’s tied to the arbitrary data distribution).

On the other hand, the philosophy of H-Nets is to compress data according to an “intrinsic” notion of compressibility and semantics. For example, we find that H-Nets compress code a lot more because code is naturally compressible; and we attribute much of H-Net’s improvements on code to this ability to understand the data better. But at the same time, this means that H-Nets spend less compute on code (because the same piece of code data gets turned into far fewer chunks sent into the main network) relative to the BPE baseline! So would a better way to control it be to let the models spend similar amounts of compute per source instead?

In short, BPE models and H-Nets can compress subdomains in opposite ways - for example, BPE compresses code less than English while H-Nets compress it more - so it’s impossible to both data- and compute- match, and the right way to compare models is subtle. These are all open research problems!

Ongoing research applies hierarchical networks to modalities beyond language, showing promise as a general learning algorithm that learns from raw data in any domain

This post has so far focused on the capabilities of hierarchical networks as applied to language modeling on bytes, which captures the ideal of learning from raw data in the context of language.

We believe that the utility of H-Nets goes far beyond language: the core concept of chunking low-level data into higher-level patterns is fundamental to intelligence, and can be applied to any domain.

One such area is audio, a core focus of Cartesia, where the inability of models to autonomously abstract raw audio into phonemes and words represents a major bottleneck toward multimodal speech-to-speech models (and in fact was a motivation for us to start investigating H-Nets years ago!).

More broadly, bytes are not necessarily a universal encoding for all domains. Most domains have a concept of “primitive building blocks”, such as characters or bytes for language, pixels for images and video, waveforms for audio, nucleotides for biology, and so on. We’ve started thinking of different modalities as being built from various base atoms, and of so-called atomic models as the truly bitter-lesson-pilled way to build powerful foundation models for multimodality. We highlight some examples of additional domains.

DNA is the lowest-level programming language of biology. Depending on the context, its natural level of abstraction, and therefore its natural primitive units, changes drastically. Because of this, we hypothesized that H-Net’s dynamic tokenization would be significantly more effective than a static tokenization scheme on the DNA domain.

We compare an H-Net and a Transformer on autoregressive modeling of DNA sequences. Both models operate at the nucleotide resolution as input (so the vocabulary size is 4, plus some special token IDs). The H-Net has a target compression ratio of 6×: its main network sees approximately one chunk for each six nucleotides.

H-Net was trained only on the next-letter prediction task for prokaryote (bacterial and archaeal) genomes, so the adherence of H-Net’s token segmentation to biology’s natural structure in the human genome is purely emergent behavior. Here we display some of these examples:

DNA and genes. DNA is a string of four letters: A, C, G, and T. A gene is the stretch of DNA responsible for producing an RNA sequence, which is usually translated into a protein. Only its coding region actually spells out the protein, while the DNA around it is far less constrained and more information-sparse: the promoter region before it, the signal that ends the RNA after it. They control when and how the protein is made, not what it is. Additionally, in human genes, introns, non-coding regions that sit in the middle of the coding region, are spliced out and also do not contribute to the protein sequence.

Codons and the reading frame. The coding region is read three letters at a time, from a start codon (usually ATG) to a stop codon (TAA, TAG, or TGA). Each three-letter codon names one amino acid in a protein sequence. Where reading starts sets the reading frame: shift it by one letter and every codon downstream changes.

The late onset of these behaviors highlights the nontriviality of the “tokenization task” in the DNA domain, and supports the thesis that hand-crafted tokenization schemes would be even more difficult and less effective in multimodal domains than in language.

Hierarchical networks are naturally defined for one-dimensional sequential data, where chunking and segmentation are intuitive. Extending them to higher-dimensional modalities, however, presents a significant technical challenge – and simultaneously a great opportunity, as vision is a domain naturally suited to hierarchies. In ongoing research, we’ve developed new techniques to apply H-Nets to N-dimensional data such as images and videos. When integrated into a pixel-space diffusion model, H-Nets not only improve generation quality, but also uncover unsupervised semantic concepts directly from raw data – mirroring the emergent behaviors we saw with language.

In more detail, we take a standard pixel-space flow-matching baseline JiT, and replace its backbone with a multi-dimensional H-Net. Just as with 1D H-Nets, the model learns both where to chunk and how much to compress. This allows it to dynamically reallocate compute across three axes: spatially (focusing on detailed regions over simple ones), integration time (varying effort by denoising step), and samples (allocating more compute on complex images). Consequently, the model consolidates homogeneous or low-information areas and focuses on salient details, and discovers semantic segmentations of the image completely without supervision. A final interesting detail is that unlike existing token-merging methods that rely on the similarity of contiguous patches, our 2D H-Net doesn’t enforce local grouping. Because of this, the model often appears to infer underlying 3D structure from flat 2D projections. For instance, it routinely groups disjointed background regions into a single coherent chunk, even when they are separated in the image by occluding foreground objects.

#99. Fed holds interest rates steady even as inflation remains stubborn

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh and his colleagues voted to hold their benchmark interest rate steady Wednesday. The central bank had raised rates in September, in an effort to curb persistent inflation. Andrew Harnik/Getty Images North America hide caption

The Federal Reserve opted not to raise interest rates Wednesday, but signaled that it's not retreating from the fight against stubborn inflation.

The central bank renewed its pledge to deliver price stability, a goal that's been made more difficult by a spike in energy prices tied to the U.S. war with Iran.

"Inflation remains elevated," the Fed's rate-setting committee acknowledged in a statement.

At its last policy meeting in September, the Fed raised rates for the first time in more than three years. Since then, data has shown inflation has continued to accelerate, with consumer prices in September up 3.7% from a year ago — a bigger annual increase than the previous month.

"It's 'wrong way," says Dan North, a senior economist at the insurance company Allianz Trade. "That inflation line is going up, not down."

The decision not to adjust interest rates comes less than a week before votes are tallied in the midterm election, which may have contributed to the Fed's caution.

"It would be suspicious if there were a hike before the midterm," North said.

After a pause at this week's meeting, Fed policymakers are widely expected to raise their benchmark interest rate at their next meeting in December, in an effort to tamp down demand and bring prices under control.

Two members of the rate-setting committee wanted to move more quickly. Fed governor Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack of the Cleveland Federal Reserve Bank voted to raise rates by a quarter percentage point on Wednesday, while a majority of Fed policymakers opted to stand pat.

The Federal Reserve's benchmark rate — currently 3.75 to 4% — helps to establish the cost of other short-term lending for things like credit cards.

Longer-term interest rates are set by the bond market but can also be influenced by Fed decisions. Mortgage rates climbed to a three-year high of 7.4% earlier this month, before easing slightly. The high cost of home loans has been a drag on the housing market, which has been in a slump for the last four years.

"The A.I. companies don't care what interest rate they have to pay," North says.

The frenzied push to build A.I. data centers is pushing up prices for construction materials and electronic equipment.

Consumer spending has also remained resilient, fueled in part by upper-income shoppers who benefit from the rising stock market.

That's not the case for less wealthy families, whose wages are not keeping pace with the rising cost of living. Average wages rose just 3% for the 12 months ending in September.

"Lower and middle-income Americans are having a real struggle," says Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics. " Their purchasing power is actually declining. And that's a pretty uncomfortable place to be. And that likely goes to explain to a large degree why people don't feel very good about the economy."

#333. Fed leaves rates unchanged: What it means for credit cards, mortgages, auto loans and savings accounts

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Federal Reserve left interest rates unchanged at its October meeting, even as rising energy costs threaten to keep inflation elevated and further strain already-tight household budgets.

Higher energy prices stemming from the Iran war may prove stickier than expected, but the central bank's decision to hold rates steady gives policymakers more time to assess the economic fallout. The dynamic could also lead Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh and the Federal Open Market Committee to consider raising rates at their December meeting in order to bring inflation down to the Fed's 2% target.

For now, however, persistent inflation and higher borrowing costs are compounding affordability challenges for many households, economists say.

Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics, said in a post on Oct. 12 that the war has cost the average American household about $2,000 so far, mostly due to higher energy costs, in addition to heightened interest rate costs.

"High-net-worth consumers are enjoying the runup in AI stocks and the value of their equity holdings, and are spending with some gusto," Zandi told CNBC. "Lower- and middle-income consumers are increasingly struggling with falling real wages and high rates, and are much more cautious and careful spenders."

The Federal Reserve's decision to keep rates at the current target range of 3.75% to 4%, and the expectation that more rate increases are in the pipeline, means consumers are unlikely to see much relief from high borrowing costs anytime soon.

The Fed influences the federal funds rate, an interest rate benchmark that sets what banks charge each other for overnight lending. That rate ripples throughout the economy, influencing both consumer borrowing costs and returns on savings.

Short-term consumer lending rates, such as credit cards, closely track the prime rate, which typically moves in tandem with the Fed's benchmark and is generally about 3 percentage points higher. Longer-term rates, like those on 30-year mortgages, meanwhile, are driven more by inflation expectations and the economic outlook.

Credit cards provide one of the clearest examples of the Fed's influence. Because most cards have variable rates, borrowers typically see changes in the federal funds rate passed on within one to two billing cycles.

But even as the Fed's benchmark rate has largely held steady — except for September's 25-basis-point increase — the average credit card interest rate has been trending higher amid concerns of rising delinquencies in the face of stubborn inflation, according to LendingTree.

"Credit card rates have risen for three straight months and are now at their highest levels since December 2025," said Matt Schulz, LendingTree's chief consumer finance analyst. "Rates are highly likely to rise in the next few months, too, as card issuers implement the Fed's September hike," he added.

Unlike credit cards and other variable-rate loans, 15- and 30-year fixed mortgage rates don't move in lockstep with the Fed's benchmark rate. Instead, they generally track long-term Treasury yields, which have recently climbed amid concerns that ongoing U.S.-Iran tensions and higher energy costs could keep inflation elevated.

As a result, 30-year fixed mortgage rates have remained above 7% for more than a month, according to Mortgage News Daily.

"There is no single switch that will bring mortgage rates meaningfully lower," said Jeff DerGurahian, LoanDepot's chief investment officer and head economist. "We likely need several factors to fall into place, including cooler oil prices, softer consumer spending and a steady run of inflation reports showing progress toward the Fed's 2% target."

Meanwhile, President Donald Trump signed an executive order that aims to reduce some mortgage costs as rising interest rates and inflation make homeownership less affordable.

Auto loan rates are fixed for the life of the loan, and market rates are tied to several factors, including the Fed's benchmark. "The headline new-car APR has essentially flatlined at the 7% threshold all year," according to Joseph Yoon, senior analyst at Edmunds.

Without a rate hike, car buyers may be offered lower rates on auto loans in the weeks ahead, but only temporarily as dealers roll out special year-end financing offers, he said.

"If that number ticks down over the next two months, it won't be because the broader lending environment suddenly turned loose," he said. "Historically, the fourth quarter creates an artificial sense of rate relief because automakers and their captive finance branches start lowering interest rates to move outgoing cars before December 31."

And yet, the "big catch," Yoon added, is that subsidized holiday rates are largely reserved for new car shoppers with top-tier credit. "For everyone else — particularly shoppers browsing the used-car lots where interest rates continue to hold above 10% — the high cost of borrowing isn't budging anytime soon," he said.

Federal student loan rates are also fixed for the life of the loan, so most borrowers are somewhat shielded from Fed moves. However, rates for new borrowers are already higher based on the results of the 10-year Treasury note auction held in May. Currently, interest rates for new federal student loans range from 6.52% to 9.07%, depending on the loan type.

On Saturday, Trump said he "might" support legislation capping federal student loan interest rates in the future. The bill, introduced by Reps. Jared Moskowitz, D-Fla., Mike Lawler, R-N.Y., and Anna Paulina Luna, R-Fla., is still in committee.

Savings rates tend to be correlated with changes in the target federal funds rate. Although holding the Fed's rate unchanged has kept savings yields largely steady, some top-yielding online savings accounts can offer above-average returns and currently pay more than 4%, according to Bankrate.

"Now is a great time to shop around for a high-yield savings account to take advantage of today's high rates," Schulz said. "The returns are below the record highs of a few years ago, but they're still far greater than what you can get from a traditional savings account with a giant megabank."

For many households, the Fed's most significant impact comes through its efforts to keep inflation in check, economists say.

When higher interest rates help slow price growth, consumers may find it easier to afford everyday necessities such as groceries and gas.

"The Fed cannot drill for oil, but it can make borrowing more expensive. Higher rates can restrain demand and limit businesses' ability to pass along costs," said Sung Won Sohn, professor of finance and economics at Loyola Marymount University and chief economist at SS Economics.

However, elevated borrowing costs can intensify affordability challenges for consumers. Some experts suggest consumers can help offset some of that strain by paying down high-interest debt, negotiating better loan terms and taking advantage of higher yields on savings.

"These steps can also improve access to more favorable financing options should interest rates moderate in the future," said Charlie Wise, head of global research and consulting at TransUnion.

#339. Apple's first touchscreen MacBook Pro feels both familiar and radically improved

It's thinner, lighter and has a better screen — but it's still a MacBook Pro.

I've gotten a chance to quickly check it out the "MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display" and — it feels like a MacBook Pro. Apple didn't reinvent the wheel with this redesign, even though there are a ton of significant changes. Despite the reduction in weight (the 14-inch is three pounds, while the 16-inch is four), Apple kept the same set of ports on these new models, and the keyboard and trackpad appear to be the same reliable and comfortable options that have been on the MacBook Pro since its big 2021 redesign. It's funny how shaving half a pound makes a huge difference, but it absolutely does in this case.

This isn't the first time that Apple has dramatically reduced the weight of the MacBook Pro — the infamous 2016 Touch Bar models were also about the same weight. But they also ran Intel chips and had notorious difficulty staying cool and performant under heavy work loads. I have to wager that won't be the case here, but it's definitely something we'll want to look out for when we do more in-depth testing.

In 2024, Apple released the iPad Pro with its "tandem OLED" screen, one that I called the nicest display I had ever seen on an Apple device. Well, that title now goes to the MacBook Pro. The OLED technology makes for the blackest blacks, and the set of demo photographs and videos loaded on the laptop really show it off.

Using touch on the MacBook Pro works just as you'd expect. You can swipe through images, use two fingers for multi-touch gestures like panning and zooming, draw in apps like Pixelmator or adjust editing sliders in Photos. The UI has not significantly changed — you'd never know at first glance that this MacBook Pro was designed for touch. UI elements like the "stop light" buttons in the top left for window management expand slightly when you touch them, but there's otherwise no major concessions to this being an OS that supports touch.

The touch performance is pretty good, though I did feel like there were occasional stutters while switching between Spaces or full-screen apps. But I'm also chalking that up to the fact that I'm not at all familiar just yet with swiping and tapping in macOS just yet. The good news is that if you're familiar with gestures on your trackpad, like using a three-finger swipe to move between full-screen apps, those gestures work here as well. You can also "flick" apps to different sides of the screen to set up how your windows are laid out, something that you can already do on the iPad.

Ultimately, this is one of those expected product glow-ups where almost everything significant has changed, but it still feels perfectly familiar. That's a good thing: the 2016 major MacBook Pro revamp was one of the darkest moments for the Mac, as the company threw out too much of what worked while adding things of questionable value.

That's not the case here. The new MacBook Pro still has a great keyboard and trackpad, it still has powerful chips and plenty of ports. But the size and weight reduction is significant and the new display is extremely impressive (even if you don't use the touchscreen). Of course, this will cost you; Apple's highest-tech has never been cheap, and it's even worse than usual thanks to the ongoing storage and RAM crisis. But the $400 premium for these new laptops over the non-OLED verions at least feels justified.

Oh — and the glowing logo on the lid is just as cool now as it was 30-something years ago. I'm glad it's back.
