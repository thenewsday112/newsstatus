#358. GitLab Security Incident: Attempted Exploitation of CVE-2026-97229

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On October 20 and 21, an attacker tried to exploit a GitLab vulnerability (CVE-2026-97229) on our instance, a week after the fix was released. The attack came close to working. We have found no evidence that it succeeded, but we cannot rule it out, so we treated the instance as compromised and rebuilt it from scratch. Our checks show that the aports repository, packages, and the October 21 releases were not tampered with.

GitLab is being tested before we open it to the public again. All passwords have been reset, so when it is back you will need to set a new one with “Forgot your password?” on the sign-in page. We will post exactly what you need to do when it reopens. In the meantime: if you used the same password for your gitlab.alpinelinux.org account anywhere else, change it there. We have seen automated login attempts against our GitLab with passwords leaked from other sites.

If you have questions or concerns please reach out to us at infra@alpinelinux.org.

The vulnerability: CVE-2026-97229 is a critical (CVSS 10.0) vulnerability in how GitLab evaluates regular expressions in CI configuration. The underlying bug is in Onigmo, the regular expression engine built into Ruby. GitLab published fixed releases on October 12, but the fix itself is a set of patches to the Ruby that GitLab bundles in its own packages and container images. Upstream Ruby shipped the fix on October 23, in Ruby 3.3.13, 3.4.12 and 4.0.9.

The attack: On October 20 and 21, a newly registered account sent crafted CI configurations to our GitLab instance, which was still running 19.3.4 and did not have the October 12 fix. This crashed GitLab worker processes 8 times. In 4 of those crashes the attacker got partway through the exploit and failed at the next step. The attacker also downloaded our GitLab container image, which suggests they were tuning the exploit to our exact build. We have found no evidence that the attacker ran code on the server, but we cannot rule it out.

Our upgrade didn’t fix the vulnerability: Upgrading to GitLab 19.3.6 on October 22 didn’t fix the vulnerability. The fix isn’t in GitLab’s own code but in a patch to Ruby, which GitLab applies only in its official packages. Our image used standard Ruby, so it didn’t get the patch.

If you run GitLab with your own Ruby (from source or in your own container image) rather than GitLab’s packages, upgrading GitLab is not enough: you also need Ruby 3.3.13, 3.4.12, 4.0.9 or later.

If you use our alpinelinux/gitlab container image, pull alpinelinux/gitlab:19.3.6 again. The image published on October 27 contains Ruby 3.3.13. Any 19.3.6 image you pulled before October 27, and all older tags, are vulnerable.

What could have been exposed if the attack succeeded

Because we cannot rule out code execution, and the evidence we would need to confirm it no longer exists, we took the harder route and rebuilt the GitLab instance from scratch. This removes any foothold a possibly successful exploit could have left behind.

While GitLab is closed, our package mirrors, cgit, and mailing lists/IRC are available as normal.

We have found no sign of tampering in aports or the releases.

Every aports commit added October 20 to 22 arrived through a recorded push by a known developer. We checked this against records the attacker could not reach: a developer’s clone and all 45 builders. Every package built from October 20 to 24, on every architecture, came from commits as published on git.alpinelinux.org. The October 21 releases (3.21.9, 3.22.7, 3.23.7, 3.24.3) were built from those same verified commits.

We ran these checks on October 24, covering commits from October 20 to 22 and packages built up to October 24. Our GitLab stayed vulnerable until we closed it on October 27, so we are extending the same checks to that date.

The bug is in Ruby’s regular expression engine, so any program that compiles regular expressions from untrusted input is exposed, not only GitLab. Alpine 3.22, 3.23, 3.24 and edge ship Ruby 3.4.12, and Alpine 3.21 ships Ruby 3.3.13; both include the fix. Run apk upgrade to get it.

#247. One passport isn’t enough for some wealthy Chinese. Here’s where they’re looking next

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For some wealthy Chinese, securing a foreign passport is becoming the ultimate trade-off: giving up their Chinese citizenship.

Wealth advisors say a growing number of affluent Chinese are considering renouncing their nationality as Beijing intensifies scrutiny of offshore wealth, with concerns over taxes, geopolitical uncertainty and greater international mobility driving their interest in alternative passports.

China does not recognize dual nationality, meaning Chinese nationals seeking to acquire foreign citizenship must renounce their passports.

"We have had some clients who are willing to give up their Chinese citizenship to hold multiple passports," said Armand Arton, CEO of immigration migration consultancy Arton Capital, who added that the choice of passports depends on their lifestyle and business needs.

Meanwhile, Ryan Lin, director at Singapore-based legal firm Bayfront Law, said he has seen an increase in wealthy Chinese considering renouncing their nationality since July.

That month, Beijing introduced new measures targeting offshore trusts, with certain incomes and transactions potentially subject to a 20% tax. The changes have prompted some families to reassess how they hold and transfer assets overseas.

Previously, clients would discuss the possibility of giving up their citizenship. Now, more are expressing concrete intentions to do so, Lin told CNBC.

Manish Tibrewal, co-founder of multi-family office Farro Capital, also reported growing interest in foreign passports.

The wealth advisory firm is helping some Chinese families pursue Caribbean citizenship alongside Singapore permanent residency, with the longer-term possibility of obtaining Singapore citizenship, subject to government approval.

Tibrewal said the number of Chinese families actively exploring alternative citizenship options since July far exceeds those who have completed the process last year.

Inquiries from Chinese clients have risen 33% since July, investment migration firm Savory & Partners said, while Global Citizen Solutions reported an increase of nearly 40% compared with the same period last year.

Both firms specialize in investment migration, which helps wealthy individuals obtain foreign residency or citizenship, often through qualifying investments in their destination countries.

Renouncing Chinese citizenship does not automatically erase existing tax liabilities though.

Individuals could remain liable for taxes on historical gains, depending on how their wealth was structured, even after giving up their nationality, but a genuine change in citizenship and residency could affect future obligations, experts said.

For many wealthy Chinese, relinquishing their nationality remains a significant sacrifice, particularly for those whose businesses and fortunes are tied to the mainland.

Lin said those most willing to take that step generally have few remaining business links to China, with much of their wealth and operations already based offshore.

For wealthy Chinese seeking an alternative passport rather than merely overseas residency, the Caribbean has emerged as a popular choice.

Countries such as Antigua and Barbuda offer citizenship-by-investment programs that allow eligible applicants to obtain nationality through qualifying investments or contributions.

Tibrewal said Caribbean citizenship can potentially be secured within six months, making it considerably faster than pathways in Europe or the United States, where residency requirements often precede naturalization.

Arton said the appeal is less about permanently relocating and more about greater flexibility to travel and establish an international base.

Global Citizen Solutions said the Caribbean recorded the strongest growth in Chinese inquiries among the destinations it tracked this year.

European countries are also attracting interest, although many offer residency pathways rather than immediate citizenship.

"For Chinese HNWIs who are not willing to give up their citizenship, a portfolio of residencies or long-term visas is a more appealing option," Arton said.

Portugal remains popular through its golden visa program, which offers residency through qualifying investments and a potential longer-term route to citizenship.

Arton also added that Italy is attracting wealthy families through its favorable tax arrangements for qualifying new residents, international schools and financial services infrastructure.

Hungary is another emerging destination, offering long-term residency through qualifying investments starting at 250,000 euros ($290,000).

“Hungary is the one that surprises people,” according to Global Citizen Solutions. “As the larger European golden visa routes have closed or become more expensive, Hungary offers residency in the Schengen area through a regulated fund investment.”

Singapore remains attractive to wealthy Chinese because of its political stability, established wealth management industry, international schools and proximity to mainland China.

Some families are pursuing Singapore permanent residency as part of longer-term citizenship plans, although naturalization is neither automatic nor guaranteed.

Tibrewal said his firm was assisting some clients with plans involving Caribbean citizenship and Singapore residency, with Singapore citizenship a possible longer-term objective.

Australia is another popular option, particularly for families prioritizing education and quality of life.

Malaysia is also gaining attention, especially Kuala Lumpur and Penang, where lower living costs, Chinese-speaking communities and international schools appeal to families, said the migration consultancies.

"We also see interest beyond the obvious hubs in Asia, including Penang and Thailand's long-term resident visa, from families who value lifestyle and lower costs," said Global Citizen Solutions.

Meanwhile, Argentina and parts of Eastern Europe are emerging as alternative destinations for those seeking to diversify their international residency options.

Arton Capital identified Argentina as a market worth watching, while Savory & Partners highlighted growing interest in Serbia, North Macedonia and Bulgaria.

Advisors caution that interest in overseas citizenship does not necessarily translate into completed renunciations or physical relocations.

"It's important to recognize that exploring overseas residency, building a passport portfolio, and relocating an entire family are very different decisions," Arton said.

Upwind, a San Francisco startup that uses AI to secure cloud infrastructure, has raised a $400 million Series C round at a $3.8 billion valuation led by Bessemer Venture Partners, with participation from existing backers including Greylock Partners and Craft Ventures.

The round comes nine months after Upwind closed its previous $250 million financing at a $1.5 billion valuation.

The fresh funding, which has been in the works for months, nearly fell apart after Datadog attempted to buy the company, according to a person familiar with Upwind. Datadog is a publicly traded company that sells tools for monitoring how software runs, known as observability. It didn’t offer a premium above the $3.8 billion valuation set by venture capital investors, the person said, so Upwind decided to stay independent.

Four-year-old Upwind has grown rapidly amid rising AI-driven cybersecurity threats. The startup has been adding $20 million to $30 million in annualized recurring revenue (ARR) each quarter and is projected to end the year at roughly $90 million, according to a person familiar with Upwind’s financials. That means that VCs and Datadog valued the startup at more than 40 times its forecast ARR, a common yardstick for pricing software companies. The high multiple is also well below the 80x ARR fetched by Cyera, another fast-growing cybersecurity startup, according to TechCrunch reporting from earlier this year.

Cybersecurity is poised to be the third big wave of AI software that companies will pay for after AI coding tools and customer support, Theory Ventures general partner Tomasz Tunguz recently wrote in his widely read blog.

By walking away from an acquisition offer, Upwind clearly believes that the company will ultimately be worth more than the $3.8 billion that Datadog was ready to pay for it. However, given how few cybersecurity companies have gone public in recent years, an eventual sale remains the most likely outcome for the startup.

Upwind continues to draw acquisition interest, according to a person familiar with the company.

The startup was founded by Amiram Shachar, who sold his previous company, the cloud spend management startup Spot.io, to NetApp for $450 million. Upwind calls its approach to thwarting threats “runtime” security, which means it alerts and remedies attacks and vulnerabilities in active services in real time.

#277. FCC chair wants to let prisons jam illegal cellphones

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The agency will vote next month on relaxing its long-standing opposition to jammers.

The US Federal Communications Commission announced today that it will vote on a new rule that would allow state prisons and jails to use cellphone jammers. The goal of the change would be to block use of illegal cellphones by inmates, since having outside contact could allow them to continue engaging in criminal activity.

The planned vote during the agency's open meeting on November 19 would consider allowing state and local incarceration facilities to opt in to using jammers; use of the tech would not be required. Any tech that interferes with communications signals is illegal for non-federal parties to use. In 2018, the Federal Bureau of Prisons began testing "micro-jamming" in federal prisons as a way to block unauthorized cellphone use by inmates. This move would potentially open up similar tools at the state and local levels, and provide a formal pathway for federal facilities to jam signals as well.

It's a reversal from previous policies where the FCC has not allowed implementing jammers as a method of reducing illegal cellphone use in prisons. Previous concerns about jammers suggested the tech could interfere with emergency and public safety communications, as well as creating new security risks by putting more jamming tools into the wild where they could be exploited by bad actors. The bulletin today notes that if the new rules are adopted, correctional facilities would need to meet some standards, including proving "that the jamming solution does not cause harmful interference outside of the designated geographic areas."

Today's move isn't the first time the FCC has changed its approach toward prisons under the current administration. In July 2025, the department said it would delay implementing a cap on the rates charged for prison phone calls.
