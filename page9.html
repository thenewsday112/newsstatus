#282. Inside India newsletter: New Delhi approves $22 billion in military spending amid China border tensions

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Hello, this is Priyanka Salve, writing to you from Singapore.

Welcome to the latest edition of "Inside India" — your one-stop destination for stories and developments from the world's fastest-growing large economy.

India, the world's fifth-largest military spender, shares a contentious relationship with two of its nuclear-armed neighbors — China and Pakistan. Last year, New Delhi carried out military strikes against alleged terrorist infrastructure in Pakistan, and last week an Indian official confirmed some border clashes with China.

As tensions at its borders simmer, India this week announced plans to spend billions of dollars on procuring modern warfare equipment. From whom will it get the goods?

Any thoughts on today's newsletter? Share them with the team.

The world's second-largest arms importer, India, is pouring billions of dollars into modernizing its military as it seeks to counter the technological advancements of neighboring China, with whom it shares an uneasy peace.

Last week, an Indian military official said that confrontations with Chinese soldiers were ongoing across several locations along the country's north-eastern border.

Shatrujeet Singh Kapoor, the director general of a division of India's central armed forces, the Indo-Tibetan Border Police, also called China the "biggest adversary" and urged for modernization of defense equipment. "It will be difficult to compete with China using traditional methods," Kapoor said.

Following Kapoor's comments, India approved spending 2.12 trillion rupees ($22 billion) to modernize its defense equipment on Monday. The country plans to spend on long-range kamikaze drones, surveillance and strike systems, counter-drone technologies, short-range air defense, artillery, helicopters and naval platforms.

Experts told CNBC that New Delhi increasingly views Beijing as a threat, especially given the Sino-Pakistan military ties, and the latest purchases were aimed at bridging the technological gap with China.

A spokesperson for India's foreign ministry said on Friday that "the situation in the India-China border areas gets disturbed from time to time when Chinese forces diverge from established patrolling patterns." Beijing responded, saying it "strictly abides by the relevant agreements and common understandings reached between the two countries."

India's military policies are largely driven by its long-standing conflict with Pakistan, and an "increasing perception of China as a threat," Pieter Wezeman, senior researcher at the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, told CNBC.

According to a SIPRI report earlier this year, 61% of Chinese arms exports were supplied to Pakistan between 2021 and 2025.

India's latest weapons purchases will involve importing components and technology from South Korea for the artillery, engines and avionics from France for the helicopters, and drones from the U.S. and Israel, Wezeman said.

Collaboration with Russia and Japan could also be on the cards, given India's need for a technological upgrade.

Since April, India has greenlighted military spending totaling 3.7 trillion rupees, which is more than 50% of the 6.73 trillion rupee worth of approvals — a 10-year high — in the financial year ended March 2026, Citi said in a report on Tuesday.

While New Delhi has said it plans to procure 82% of the defense equipment from local industries, foreign companies are likely to play a key role in providing technology crucial for military modernization.

Foreign partnerships remain important for technologies that India does not yet produce independently, Arpit Chaturvedi, South Asia advisor at strategic advisory firm Teneo, told CNBC. India relies on imported technologies for specialized components such as aircraft engines, propulsion systems, sensors and electronic subsystems, he explained.

"American, Israeli, and Russian defense manufacturers are likely to find substantial opportunities in India's expanding defense market," through joint ventures, co-production and technology partnerships with Indian companies, Chaturvedi said.

India was the world's second-largest importer of major arms in 2021–25 with an 8.2% share of total global imports, even as its reliance on overseas suppliers dropped by 4% during the same time due to growing local design and production abilities, according to SIPRI.

New Delhi is also the world's fifth-largest military spender, with $92 billion, but this is less than 30% of Chinese military expenditure and around a 10th of America's, Wezeman of SIPRI pointed out.

India faces some practical challenges in matching China's scale of military modernization, but the conflicts in the Middle East and Ukraine have shown that mass-produced systems like drones can address asymmetry, Collin Koh Swee Lean, a senior fellow at Singapore's Nanyang Technological University, said.

#174. Early vote one week out shows Democratic enthusiasm in Texas, Georgia: Analysis

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Democrats are getting a higher share of that group, an ABC News analysis shows.

With less than a week to the midterms elections, an ABC News analysis of the early vote in the key states of Texas and Georgia shows signs of more enthusiasm among Democrats, who are getting slightly higher turnout among those who don't vote regularly -- particularly in Texas -- compared to what they got in 2022.

More than 39 million Americans have voted early as of Wednesday afternoon, according to the Election Lab at the University of Florida.

Much of the apparent surge in turnout is among voters who likely would have voted later in the pre-election period or on Election Day, the analysis shows. Less-habitual voters who are voting early, while a much smaller number compared to regular voters, appear to be more likely to vote for the Democrat.

The data from voter records firm L2 that ABC News' analysis is based on does not indicate who someone might have voted for and does not say anything about who might yet turn out on Election Day.

The analysis also uses L2's models for whether a voter is likely to vote Democratic or Republican, as neither Texas nor Georgia register voters by party. The modeling uses available information like turnout in primaries and other factors to predict which party people are likely to support.

More than 5 million voters had cast early ballots in Texas as of Wednesday, according to the Election Lab at the University of Florida, more than 20% of the state’s registered voters.

There are closely watched races for Senate and governor in the Lone Star state; Democrats hope to flip both despite not having won a statewide race since 1994. President Donald Trump rallied there twice on Tuesday and Republicans say the president’s swings through red-leaning states are meant to turn out his base and motivate Republicans who otherwise might have sat out this midterm.

As of Monday, 51% of the early vote in Texas is from voters modeled as likely to vote for Democrats while 45% is from voters modeled as likely to vote for Republicans, the analysis shows -- a 14 percentage point swing for Democrats from 2022 when they were behind Republicans by around 8 percentage points when early voting was finished. Donald Trump won Texas by 14 points in 2024.

In Texas, regular voters who voted in four out of the last four federal elections made up a greater share of the turnout as of Monday than they did in the complete early vote in 2024 and 2022, according to the analysis. This doesn’t necessarily mean that more habitual voters have shown up, just that fewer less-regular voters have. The habitual voters make up 60% of the early vote this year, as of Monday; this group made up 47% of the total early vote in 2024 and 44% of the total early vote in 2022

The early vote data shows that Democrats are getting far higher turnout among the less frequent voters in Texas who likely prefer Democrats, in comparison to the less frequent voters who likely prefer Republicans. Whether it be during the remaining days of early voting or on Election Day, Republicans will need to mobilize their less enthusiastic supporters.

Among voters who did not vote in any of the last four federal elections but who have voted early in Texas as of Monday, 64% of them are modeled as likely to vote for a Democrat and 21% as likely to vote for a Republican. In the total early vote in 2022, 50% of these voters were modeled as likely Democratic voters and 39% were estimated to be likely Republican voters.

In the early vote to date, there are far fewer less-habitual voters compared to high-turnout ones and they take up a smaller share of the early vote overall. Still, close elections are decided at the margins and polling in Texas has shown the Senate and governor’s races being close.

More than 2.1 million voters had cast early ballots in Georgia as of Wednesday morning, according to the Election Lab at the University of Florida, where the Senate and governor's races are also being closely watched.

In the Senate race, GOP Rep. Mike Collins is trying to unseat incumbent Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff. Democrats are trying to flip the governorship as Democratic former Atlanta Mayor Keisha Lance Bottoms faces Republican businessman Rick Jackson in a race both parties see as competitive.

According to ABC News' analysis, 54% of the early vote in Georgia is from voters modeled as likely to vote for Democrats while 38% is from voters modeled as likely to vote for Republicans.

In Georgia, habitual voters who voted in four out of the last four federal elections comprised a greater share of the turnout as of Monday than they did in the early vote one week out from the 2022 midterms.

Habitual voters made up 67% of the early vote in 2026 as of Monday while they comprised 55% of the total early vote one week from Election Day 2022.

Among voters who did not vote in any of the last four federal elections but who have voted early in Georgia as of Monday, 52% of them are modeled as likely to vote for Democrats and 12% are modeled as likely to vote for Republicans. In the early vote at this time in 2022, 51% of those voters were modeled Democratic and 27% were modeled Republican

Meanwhile, among voters who voted in two out of the last four federal elections who had voted early in Georgia as of Monday, 56% are modeled Democratic and 24% Republican. In the early vote at this point in 2022, 54% of those voters were modeled Democratic and 35% Republican.

Early voting ends in both states on Friday.

#172. As Waymo expands overseas, it remains a niche cash-burning business

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Waymo is dominant in the U.S. and expanding overseas, but it remains immaterial in terms of revenue to parent Alphabet, which is pouring cash into its growth.

In Alphabet's third-quarter earnings report on Wednesday, the company said its Other Bets segment, which includes Waymo, generated $372 million in revenue, up 8% from a year earlier, and accounting for just 0.3% of total sales.

That revenue comes at a steep price. Operating loss in the Other Bets segment widened to $2.05 billion during the quarter from $1.43 billion a year ago. The unit also houses drug design research business Isomorphic, which is using Google's Gemini 4 Argon AI models.

CEO Sundar Pichai touted Waymo's expansion toward the end of his introductory remarks on the earnings call. The company has a commanding lead in the U.S. robotaxi market, where it faces emerging competition from Amazon's Zoox and Tesla. Waymo said in August that it plans to roll out robotaxi rides in Germany in 2027, marking its third international market and first in the EU.

"Waymo now operates in 15 major U.S. cities and just recently opened up service to the public in San Antonio, San Diego and Tampa with planned expansions into Canada, Germany, Singapore and Spain," Pichai said on the call.

Waymo's steepest competition on the global stage comes from Baidu's Apollo Go, which is based in China.

For years, Alphabet was able to operate Waymo as a side project while its core online ads business racked up tens of billions of dollars in annual profit and threw off mounds of cash. But with the company now investing heavily in artificial intelligence, free cash flow has turned negative and Wall Street has shown signs of concern.

Alphabet reported an earnings and revenue miss on Wednesday, sending the stock down almost 10% in extended trading. The company maintained its full-year capital expenditures forecast at between $195 billion and $205 billion, largely for AI infrastructure, and reported negative free cash flow of over $9 billion for the quarter.

Earlier this month, Waymo announced that it had closed a $5 ‌billion term loan, its first debt financing. In February, the robotaxi business announced that it had raised $16 billion at a $126 billion valuation in a funding round led by Alphabet that included support from Andreessen Horowitz, Fidelity, T. Rowe Price, Sequoia Capital, Kleiner Perkins and others.

Last week Waymo started offering driverless rides to and from the Las Vegas airport, after previously announcing airport service in five other cities, including San Francisco and Phoenix.

"Waymo is on a great trajectory," Pichai said on Wednesday. "We have now driven more than 300 million fully autonomous miles."

Tesla says on its website that driverless vehicles in its Robotaxi network have completed about 3.8 million miles "unsupervised," or without a human driver at the wheel. CEO Elon Musk said on the company's earnings call last week that the Robotaxi business should become a "meaningful" revenue contributor by the second half of 2027.

Voters in Alaska's closely watched Senate contest are choosing between four candidates who survived a nonpartisan primary earlier this year. Those include Peltola and Sullivan, along with a retired teacher named Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. The ballot — which allows voters to rank the candidates rather than just selecting one — identifies the sitting senator as a Republican and an incumbent, while Danial J. Sullivan Jr. has no party affiliation listed.

On Wednesday, the Times reported that a Democratic operative who worked with Peltola's campaign and national Democrats helped encourage Daniel J. Sullivan to run, citing text messages and interviews. The newspaper reported that it appeared to be part of a wider effort to draw more candidates into the race.

The second Sullivan has vexed Republicans for months, with the incumbent senator calling his same-name challenger a "sham candidate" who was trying to trick voters. Daniel J. Sullivan has denied those allegations and insisted he is genuinely interested in running for Senate.

State officials initially sought to keep him off the ballot, finding that he was trying to confuse voters and arguing his was not a "good-faith candidacy." The Alaska Supreme Court rejected that decision and ruled that the other Sullivan can appear on the ballot.

Last week, the Justice Department filed a lawsuit seeking to prevent the election from being certified if Sen. Dan Sullivan loses due to "voter confusion" about the dueling Sullivans, in what Attorney General Todd Blanche described as an effort to "put guardrails in place." Asked what he thought about the suit, Mr. Trump told reporters: "Well, I'm the one that said, 'Do it.'"

Nate Adams, a spokesperson for Sen. Dan Sullivan's campaign, said in a statement in reaction to the Times' reporting that "Alaskans deserve the truth."

"Mary Peltola and her campaign have spent months gaslighting Alaskans, denying a scheme they were running all along," Adams said.

Mr. Trump called the situation "voter fraud at the highest level" in comments to reporters.

"It's terrible what's happened in Alaska," he said Wednesday. "They created a fake candidate."

Democrats have denied any role in Daniel J. Sullivan's candidacy. A Peltola campaign spokesperson said it "has no involvement with either Sullivan campaign."

"Mary got in this race because Alaskans deserve a champion who is fighting to lower their costs and put Alaska first, and no tweet or threat from any DC politician will keep her away from that fight," the campaign spokesperson said.
