#518. World Series 2026: 10 wild stats from the Brewers' first title

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Milwaukee Brewers are your 2026 World Series champions, having capped off a dominant postseason with a 4-1 win over the Tampa Bay Rays on Wednesday.

It's a cathartic night for the Brewers, who were previously tied with the San Diego Padres as the oldest MLB team to have never won a World Series title. They are now permanently off that list, thanks to a team that leveraged its strengths all years.

How did the Brewers do it? Let's dive into that via numbers.

We're going to go ahead and list off every starting pitcher the Brewers faced in their 12 postseason games: Robbie Ray, Michael King, Nick Pivetta, Walker Buehler, Tarik Skubal, Yoshinobu Yamamoto, Blake Snell, Tyler Glasnow, Nick Martinez, Drew Rasmussen and Freddy Peralta.

That is a formidable group, even by playoff standards. Combined, they have 15 total All-Star appearances (and five Cy Young awards) compared to the Brewers rotation's two, both by Jacob Misiorowski. And even Misiorowski's first All-Star appearance last year was a matter of some controversy.

Would you like to know how long that group lasted against the Brewers lineup on average?

It's 4.2 innings. Only one pitcher lasted longer than five innings at all and it was surprisingly Snell, despite the Los Angeles Dodgers left-hander being infamous for short starts even while posting zeroes.

The Brewers chased nearly every pitcher they faced this postseason by doing what they did all regular season. No team swung less at pitches. No team drew more walks. Only two teams in MLB missed less often when they did swing. They hit the most ground balls and the most balls to the opposite field.

They didn't hit home runs much, ranking only 29th in MLB, but that entire previous paragraph turned their offense into a torture device for even some of MLB's best pitchers.

Funnily enough, the Brewers starters lasted just as long as those opponents this postseason at 4.2 innings per start, but for very different reasons (and that number goes up to 4.5 when you take out Shane Drohan's one-inning appearance as an opener).

The Brewers starters were definitely solid this postseason, but Milwaukee removed them as early as possible in basically every game and rolled out its comically dominant bullpen.

The Brewers relievers pitched 65 innings this postseason and posted a 1.38 ERA and a .173 batting average against, per MLB.com's Sarah Langs.

That was the Brewers' formula when you zoom out as much as possible: force the other team's starter out early and turn the game into a battle of the bullpens. It worked to perfection.

Jacob Misiorowski was still the Brewers ace in the postseason.

There's something to be said, though, about what that means for baseball. The starting pitcher who regularly goes seven innings or more in a start is bordering on extinction. Sandy Alcantara led all pitchers with 214 innings during the regular season, which would have ranked for ninth in MLB 10 years ago and 22nd in MLB 20 years ago.

Teams are now perfectly willing to accept a five-inning start, if it means those are five good innings. This has been a slow shift in MLB for years, but the Brewers just brought it into sharp focus.

That's the proportion of innings the Brewers bullpen covered during the postseason.

That makes Milwaukee the fourth champion in MLB history to get a majority of its innings from relievers, joining the 2020 Los Angeles Dodgers, 2021 Atlanta Braves and 2024 Dodgers.

It had never happened before 2020, another sign of the aggressive-bullpen era. The closest any team ever got before that was the 1960 Pittsburgh Pirates, though that was hardly a conscious decision. That team took three double-digit losses to the New York Yankees before winning in seven games, with relievers forced to cover for short starts in each defeat.

That is the number of home runs hit in the 2026 World Series, per ESPN, the fewest since the 1985 Fall Classic between the Kansas City Royals and St. Louis Cardinals.

The Brewers had three of those homers, from perhaps the three players you would least expect: Sal Frelick, Cooper Pratt and David Hamilton, the No. 6, 7 and 9 hitters in the Game 5 lineup.

The Brewers built their offense around not needing homers to punish pitchers, but they still out-homered the competition in every round, with a total margin of 13 to 4.

In another world, the 2026 World Series ends in Game 4 on Tuesday as a Brewers sweep and rookie shortstop Cooper Pratt gets remembered as the guy who homered his club to its first title.

A 12th-inning collapsed prevented that from coming to pass, but it did give the 22-year-old a chance to make some history. With two more hits in Game 5, Pratt became the first rookie to post a base hit in six straight at-bats in the World Series.

Pratt made his MLB debut in June and was playing high school baseball only three years ago, with the Brewers taking him in the sixth round of the 2023 MLB Draft. He rocketed to the majors thanks to his well-regarded glove, then made his mark with his bat.

With a single in the seventh inning, Brewers star Jackson Chourio became MLB's all-time leader in postseason hits before turning 23, breaking a tie with Andruw Jones with his 31st hit.

Jones posted his 30 hits in 45 games for the Atlanta Braves. Chourio posted his 31 in 25 games. He also tied some guy named Babe Ruth for the most hits in the first four games of a World Series, and now has the second-most in five games behind only Pepper Martin in 1931.

You would think with either of the above stats, Pratt or Chourio would have been named World Series MVP. It's a sign of how thoroughly great William Contreras was that neither were.

Contreras earned MVP after a postseason in which he was basically the best player in baseball. The three-time All-Star slashed .373/.448/.588 at the plate during the postseason. He threw out 6-of-13 baserunners. And he won 21 out of 31 ABS challenges (67.8%) in the first postseason with the system, with accuracy and decisiveness becoming a running joke in the Fox Sports booth.

In the World Series, Contreras hit 8-for-21 (.381) with four RBI, including one on a double in Game 5.

What else could you possibly want from a catcher?

Misiorowski began the World Series by throwing the fastest pitch in its tracked history at 103.5 mph. He would go on to throw 89 pitches of at least 100 mph between his starts in Games 1 and 5.

Misiorowski didn't reach the end of the sixth inning in either start — exiting Game 5 due to calf cramps — but he allowed only one run in 10 2/3 total innings. His five strikeouts were lower than his regular season norm, but he was still an ace by Brewers standards.

Aaron Ashby went from a relatively anonymous set-up man to the most feared reliever in baseball over the course of the postseason, making nine appearances in 12 games and pitching multiple innings in all but one.

The left-hander didn't allow a single run in his 18 innings pitched, the most innings a reliever has ever thrown in a postseason without giving up a run. He didn't factor into Game 5 after experiencing diminished velocity in a long Game 4 appearance, but he had already contributed more than enough.

There will be plenty of analysis about what it means the Brewers — a classic small-market, big-brain team — winning the World Series means for baseball.

Their $142 million payroll, as estimated by Roster Resource, ranked 19th in MLB this season, right between the Kansas City Royals and Cincinnati Reds. They were actually the financial heavyweight this series, as the Rays ranked 28th at $90 million, followed by their ALCS opponent Cleveland Guardians at $87 million.

You build a champion on that budget with smart decisions and long-term planning. The highest-paid player on the 2026 Brewers was Christian Yelich ($26 million), still playing on the mega-deal he got from his MVP days, followed by the mostly injured Brandon Woodruff ($22 million) and two trade deadline acquisitions in Dustin May and Antonio Senzatela ($12 million each).

No other player on the Brewers made more than $10 million this year. Chourio and Pratt, both 22-year-olds who just starred on a World Series team, are both under team control through at least 2031, at the cost of around $132 million.

Contreras will be back next year on a $14.5 million club option. Nearly every other big contributor is under contract beyond 2027, save for NLCS MVP Jake Bauers, a pending free agent. And let's not forget the Brewers still ranked third in MLB Pipeline's farm system rankings after the trade deadline, with a group headlined by No. 1 overall prospect Jesús Made.

The Brewers were incredible this year. There is every reason to believe they will stay incredible for years to come.

#9. How LADWP's rates compare to other utilities in Southern California

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Updated on: October 28, 2026 / 10:12 PM PDT / CBS LA

Amid controversy over steep utility rate hikes for Los Angeles Department of Water and Power customers — some in the thousands of dollars—CBS California Investigates took a closer look at how rates compare to other utilities in the region, and at the high salaries of dozens of executives.

Tuesday night, the LADWP Board of Commissioners passed a resolution requesting a review of the agency's rates, structures and how they're applied to individual customers. LADWP provided CBS California Investigates with a news release about the board's resolution; click here to read it.

Tim O'Connor, executive director of LA's Office of Public Accountability, which is the independent ratepayer advocate for the City of Los Angeles, says, "it's a great step in the right direction."

He added, "however, of course, the devil's going to be in the details for how you do that review."

O'Connor said the review should also improve transparency to customers and should aim to modernize rate structures.

"Our rates were designed over 10 years ago, and a lot has changed since then," O'Connor said. "So, being able to reward customers based on when they use it, and to encourage new technology, and to give people the data to be able to control their use, these all have to be on the table, and are all things that we think are going to be part of the review."

Something else advocates believe should be analyzed in the review is the high salaries of LADWP supervisors and executives.

CBS California Investigates found that nine executives at LADWP make more than $700,000 a year, and more than two dozen supervisors make more than $600,000 a year.

Customer rates are paying those salaries, but O'Connor says the rates are primarily funding major capital projects, operations expenses, and maintenance expenses.

"When we look at the individual salaries, we're not seeing that as the driver of what people are feeling in their pocketbook. We're seeing that as a challenge of the utility needing to attract and retain staff that can keep the lights on and to keep the water flowing," O'Connor said. "It doesn't mean that we shouldn't be reviewing that and making sure it's reasonable, that is absolutely something we should be doing, but when we look at the cost drivers, we're seeing other things that are driving those costs as opposed to salaries."

In their statement to CBS California Investigates, LADWP provided a breakdown of overtime paid to its more than 13,000 employees. The utilities company said overtime accounts for 5% of its $10.8 billion budget. Total labor costs account for 18% of LADWP's budget.

"The employees you inquired about hold highly skilled positions that are essential to operating, maintaining, and restoring the City's electric infrastructure," LADWP said in its statement. "These classifications are in high demand throughout the utility industry, making recruitment and retention increasingly competitive. LADWP's compensation structure is designed to help attract and retain the specialized workforce required to safely and reliably operate one of the nation's largest municipal utility systems."

LADWP added that its overtime compensation increased 17% from roughly $426 million in 2024 to more than $499 million in 2025.

An analysis from UCLA School of Law Emmett Institute on Climate Change and the Environment found that LADWP's rates have surged by 45% in the last year, compared to Southern California Edison, which has only increased 4%.

Denise Grab, Emmett Clean Energy Law and Leadership Project Director, explained the increase is "significantly in excess of inflation."

O'Connor says his office has also found the increases have outpaced inflation.

"We know that the department, for many, many years, was keeping rates pretty much indexed to inflation," O'Connor said. "Since the mid-2023-2024 timeline, the investments the Department of Water and Power has been making have really increased, which has caused those rates to increase as well, and exceed inflation. We did our own study that showed that in the last 18 months, the price for water and power has indeed surpassed inflation. Now, of course, our rates are still lower than utilities like Southern California Edison, which mean our bills are lower, but that doesn't mean that we shouldn't be rethinking and reforming the rates we have to make sure that the people in Los Angeles can afford the utility bills that they get in the mail."

Grab says one reason for the steep increase is to fund more capital projects.

"This is kind of in the context of many years of rates that were very cheap compared to the state average, and, without huge hikes year over year. So LADWP is kind of playing catch-up for having deferred some maintenance for some period of time, but they have had a significant increase, which has, I think, caused a bit of sticker shock for a lot of Angelenos across the city," Grab said.

In its statement, LADWP said its rates are also lower than nearby utility companies, "demonstrating the value of public power and a City-owned utility."

Still, Grab says SCE rates are generally higher than LADWP by 20%.

A study by Grab's organization in 2025 found LADWP rates were not as high as some other utilities in Southern California, including Glendale Water and Power and Southern California Edison.

A 2026 study commissioned by the city of Pasadena and conducted by a company called New Gen Strategies also found Glendale and SCE have higher rates in October 2026 than LADWP, Burbank Water and Power, and Pasadena Water and Power.

O'Connor and Grab both warn rates will continue to increase in the coming years. Grab says aging infrastructure, wildfire liability and prevention, and rising air conditioning usage due to heatwaves will drive those future rate spikes.

In fact, a recent presentation from LADWP shows rates are expected to more than double what they are now.

Grab says as rates continue to go up, LADWP needs to improve its communication with customers.

"They've had such low rates for so long that I think many people were not paying that much attention to, kind of the particulars of their, of their bill and how it was calculated, and I think it's shocking folks now with, with these increases," she said. "So, I think really communicating where the bill is coming from, how it's calculated, how folks can save money, and when folks need to save money, because the rates vary over the course of the year, power is more expensive in the summer. I think really understanding when and how folks need to take measures, if they can, to help reduce power at key times."

LADWP says it is rolling out new smart-meter installations to help customers better understand their bills over the next year. Grab says these meters will give customers real-time feedback so they can course-correct and keep their bills lower.

O'Connor agreed that more information to customers will be key moving forward.

"We can't just tell people to turn down their thermostats during heat waves. We need to be giving people the ability so that they don't have to turn their thermostats down, and so that their houses retain cold air, so that their houses are able to withstand those heat waves, and you don't have to run your air conditioning 24 hours a day," O'Connor said.

O'Connor added that LADWP also should evaluate its solar energy pricing and consider making more people eligible for payment assistance programs.

"We know that energy has different value at different times of the day," he said. "In California, we have been so successful at getting more solar energy, whether it's from rooftops or from big solar plants out in the desert. We have an oversupply of energy at certain times. We should be pricing it so people use energy at that time, so that we can soak up that extra, really low-cost energy. Right now, the times that we have to reward people for that are mismatched from when this solar is available, so we need to be reforming and reframing those time-of-use rates, like the utilities that are governed by the California Public Utilities have done.

"The second very clear example is our low-income supportive rates. We need to be able to increase the amount that's available to people, and increase the floor where people can qualify. When we look at the way that people qualify and you index it to the federal poverty level, it's inappropriate to apply an index that looks at national poverty statistics and applies it to Los Angeles, because the cost of living here is so much higher. So, we need to be rethinking not just the structure of the times, but also the supportive rates that can help people afford the energy bills and the energy use that they rely on to keep their kids cool, to keep the elderly cool, and to provide basic services for the people of Los Angeles."

#10. Brewers beat the Rays to win the first World Series title in franchise history

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Milwaukee Brewers catcher William Contreras holds the MVP trophy after winning baseball's World Series against the Tampa Bay Rays, Wednesday, Oct. 28, 2026, in St. Petersburg, Fla. Morry Gash/AP hide caption

ST. PETERSBURG, Fla. — The small-ball Milwaukee Brewers playing in the smallest market in the majors are World Series champions for the first time.

Hey, Milwaukee: grab a brat, raise a beer and prepare for that parade.

Young ace Jacob Misiorowski threw 5 2/3 scoreless innings, rookie Cooper Pratt had two more hits and two RBIs, and Milwaukee beat the Tampa Bay Rays 4-1 in Game 5 on Wednesday night to set off a long-awaited celebration among thousands of Brewers fans in attendance at Tropicana Field.

"It's the best feeling ever. All of our fans are here. I know the city's waited a long, long time for this," captain Christian Yelich said. "We're so happy to bring it home to them. I can't wait to get back there and celebrate."

World Series MVP William Contreras hit an RBI double and Misiorowski allowed just two hits before leaving the game because of a right calf cramp. He got a standing ovation from the Brew-Crew fans, who took over the section behind Milwaukee's dugout for the last three games.

The 22-year-old Pratt set a Series rookie record with six straight hits before his sacrifice fly in the sixth. Jackson Chourio set a five-game Series record with 11 hits.

"This is one of the greatest moments of my life. Oh my gosh. It doesn't even feel real," said Pratt, who made his major league debut in June. "This is surreal. It only took four months!"

The championship comes in the 58th season for a franchise that began as the Seattle Pilots in 1969 and moved to Milwaukee the following year. #380. MLB Lockout 101: Everything to know about baseball's likely upcoming work stoppage

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The 2026 Major League Baseball season is over, and the Milwaukee Brewers are World Series champions for the first time. The Brewers finished off the Tampa Bay Rays on Wednesday night in Game 5 of the 2026 World Series. MLB Opening Day for 2027 is scheduled for March 24. But whether fans are watching baseball again or not in less than five months is very much up in the air.

Major League Baseball's players and owners are in the process of negotiating a new collective bargaining agreement, or CBA. The CBA is the accord that governs the working relationship between the players, as represented by their union, the MLBPA, and the clubs. It covers pretty much every aspect of that relationship that you can imagine, all the way from the overarching salary and payroll structure to daily meal money and days off during the season.

Not surprisingly, it's the economic platform of the game that's the toughest to negotiate because that is always, in essence, a fight over money, or how the league's substantial revenues will be divided up between teams and players. That's keenly relevant right now because players and owners are sparring over whether or not MLB will have a cap on salaries moving forward. The owners have renewed their push for a salary cap after not aggressively (or at least not this aggressively) pursuing one since the 1994-95 players' strike.

Matters are such that the owners are expected to implement a lockout of the players after the current CBA expires at 11:59 p.m. ET on Dec. 1 of this year.

There are two kinds of labor stoppages in MLB: the strike and the lockout. A strike is when the labor side – players as represented by their union, in this instance – puts a halt to operations. A lockout is when the management side – team owners and the commissioner's office doing their bidding, in this instance – initiates the stoppage. In plainspoken terms, a strike is a refusal to work, and a lockout is a refusal to permit work to be done. For those on the outside, the result is the same, in that there's no Major League Baseball during a lockout or strike.

When a lockout is in force in MLB, as is expected to be the case after this CBA expires, nothing happens on the major-league front. And we do mean nothing.

There's an almost literal element to a lockout in that anyone on the 40-man roster of any MLB club will be locked out of any team facility for the duration of the stoppage. That also means no access to team trainers or team physicians or members of the coaching staff. Beyond that, there's a transaction freeze in place starting the moment the lockout begins (more on that in a moment).

Additionally, MLB is expected to remove player images and videos from the league's official website, MLB.com, and player images and even names would be removed from articles on MLB.com. A league memo acquired by The Athletic also forbids contact between players and anyone on the club side, at least to the greatest extent possible. The latter extends all the way down to a recommendation against sending holiday cards to players who have been locked out.

Bottom line: A lockout means the sport at the highest level is on hold across all fronts until further notice. "Further notice" almost certainly means when a new CBA is agreed to in principle and then ratified by both sides.

As just noted, a lockout means a transaction freeze, which means no players on any 40-man rosters can be traded and no major-league free agents can be signed for as long as the lockout lasts. Free agency, however, begins on Monday (Nov. 2), so there is roughly one month available for those looking to make trades or signings (once the five-day exclusive negotiating window closes and qualifying offers are extended) before the freeze sets in.

It's worth noting that in the run-up to the last lockout of 2021-22, we saw a swarm of free agent signings just before the lockout began. We could see that flurry of moves again, particularly in light of proposed changes to contract structures that would limit lengths and dollar amounts. Would players like Tarik Skubal, who are eyeing mid-nine-figure contracts, want to get their deals done quickly and hope they're grandfathered in?

That said, there's a lot more uncertainty about what the game's financial structure will look like after this CBA negotiation concludes, at least relative to the last time we did this. That may have a chilling effect on the trade and free agent markets leading up to the lockout date. Clubs don't like mass uncertainty when it comes to making major roster decisions, and they may prefer to see what the league's salary and revenue sharing systems will look like for the next half-decade (the term of each CBA these days) before committing to signings and trades.

If the lockout spans a meaningful amount of time, then that means a great deal of usual winter business will be put on hold. The MLB Winter Meetings and the major-league portion of Rule 5 Draft are typically held in the first half of December, so those could be imperiled. Last time out, the major-league portion of the Rule 5 Draft was indeed canceled, although the November Rule 5 protection deadline had already passed. If the lockout stretches into January, then the exchange of arbitration figures between eligible players and their teams will be delayed. Get well into late January and early February without an agreement, and arbitration hearings and the spring training schedule could be shortened or imperiled altogether. Needless to say, local businesses and elected officials in Florida and Arizona would very much not like that. That, though, could be the least of our worries.

It very well might. It's too soon to say, but there's the real potential for disruption and, perish the thought, maybe even cancellation of the 2027 season. The latter is of course the worst-case scenario, and there's no reason to think there's a strong threat of that possibility, at least this far out. During the last lockout, though, the two sides weren't fighting over quite as much territory, and we very nearly lost games from the regular-season schedule. Lest anyone forget, the last time the owners vigorously pushed for a salary cap, we wound up losing the World Series.

Secretary of Health and Human Services Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has supported broader access to peptides. A new policy will make it easier for small compounding pharmacies to make them. Heather Diehl/Getty Images hide caption

Unproven but popular peptides touted for wellness have been available on the gray market, despite an official ban. Now, the Department of Health and Human Services is looking to bring them out of the shadows.

HHS said in a new policy that it will exercise enforcement discretion for a half dozen peptides, essentially looking the other way when they're made by small compounding pharmacies.

Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has been a vocal supporter of peptides, saying he has used some of them himself.

Peptides are strings of amino acids, shorter than proteins, that can exert powerful effects in the body. Some occur naturally and others are synthetic variations. Wellness influencers and some clinicians have promoted unproven peptides for a wide range of uses, though the evidence for their safety and effectiveness is sparse.

So even though these peptides have not been through the Food and Drug Administration's rigorous drug review and approval process, they could become easier to get. But the policy's focus on smaller producers may mean that pharmacists will have a tough time meeting demand.

In July, a panel of advisers to the FDA voted in favor of expanded access to six peptides through compounding pharmacies. However, at the same meeting the FDA's own scientists recommended against unwinding the existing restrictions on them.

The new policy says compounding is a safer alternative to illicit "research-use-only" peptides that are available online. They're often sourced from sketchy suppliers overseas.

Joshua Fritzler, the president of Olympia and Wesley Pharmaceuticals in central Florida, says people have been asking about these peptides for years. But because they were illegal to compound, his company didn't make them.

The new policy doesn't change their legal status, but it does create a path for small compounders, called 503A compounding pharmacies. It doesn't apply to the larger ones, called 503Bs, that are outsourcing facilities producing higher volumes of medicines.

So at Fritzler's company, only its smaller compounding facility will be able to make the peptides – and only in small quantities, he says.

"We won't be able to meet demand," he says, explaining that Olympia will likely only compound one of the six peptides and needs to get its facility ready first. "I don't think all the [503]As in the country could meet the demand."

"It also brings patients into a system where pharmacy standards, regulatory oversight, and serious adverse event reporting apply," he says.

Brunner warned, however, that it may take some time for compounding pharmacies to begin dispensing these products to patients. That's because ingredient suppliers need to make sure their active ingredients meet the policy's requirements — and pharmacies need to verify that, too.

Several consumer advocacy groups say the policy puts patients at risk, however. The nonprofit Public Citizen noted that Kennedy seems to have acted unilaterally rather than through the typical rulemaking process.

"By allowing unproven and potentially unsafe peptides to be manufactured, marketed and prescribed without concern about potential federal enforcement actions, Kennedy has undermined the FDA and threatened public health," Dr. Robert Steinbrook, Health Research Group Director at Public Citizen wrote in a statement. "All peptide substances should go through the standard FDA approval process for new drugs. The HHS Secretary should support, not sabotage, needed FDA enforcement actions to protect consumers."

Steinbrook advised clinicians to talk to their patients and tell them about the risk of contamination and other adverse effects.

The Partnership for Safe Medicines, which includes various pharmacy and pharmacist organizations around the country, filed a citizen petition last week, urging the FDA to ignore the recommendation from its advisory committee in July to allow compounding pharmacies to make the six peptides.

"Today's decision leaves Americans in a grey area as to whether peptides are safe or not, even though the FDA's own scientists recommended against voting in favor of these substances due to concerns with evidence," its executive director Shabbir Safda wrote in a statement.

The six peptides are BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500.

They’ve been promoted online as treatments for everything from faster healing to younger-looking skin and a longer lifespan. None are approved by the FDA, and the agency’s own scientists concluded there is insufficient data to prove they are safe and effective.

The FDA said compounding pharmacies must certify the identity and purity of the compounds. They must also now report side effects, which was not previously mandated.

The move comes as a growing “gray market” has sprung up around peptides, where retailers sell them labeled “for research use only,” a legal workaround that lets them claim they aren’t meant for humans. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and others have argued that allowing peptides to be compounded would move people away from this gray market and toward safer products.

“Under President Trump’s leadership, HHS is working to put prevention and patient safety at the center of health care—and that includes confronting the risks of black-market peptides,” Emily Hilliard, an HHS spokesperson, said in a statement.

Dr. Peter Lurie, a former FDA official who now leads the Center for Science in the Public Interest watchdog group, said the FDA was essentially “turning a blind eye” to widen access to drugs that have not been proven to work.

Compounding pharmacies generally may use only ingredients the FDA has added to an official list known as the bulk drugs substances list. Adding an ingredient requires the FDA to propose a rule, take public comment and issue a final rule, a process that can take several months.

The agency took a step toward that process in July, when an FDA advisory panel narrowly voted in favor of adding six peptides to the bulk list. The panel included six new appointees with ties to the peptide industry and another who is the son of a U.S. representative who has pushed Kennedy to make peptides more available. (During that meeting, some committee members expressed concerns that the recommendation could make it harder for the FDA to say no to other unapproved drugs in the future.)

On Tuesday and Wednesday, the FDA was scheduled to meet outside groups, including Medivant Healthcare, a compounding pharmacy that offers peptides, according to Reginfo.gov, a federal website that tracks government regulations.

Shabbir Safdar, executive director of the Partnership for Safe Medicines, which researches the dangers of counterfeit medicines, said both the enforcement discretion and upcoming rule change would expose Americans to “untested, dangerous substances.”

“Imagine what it means for the nation’s top drug regulator to announce they won’t enforce the laws on people making completely untested compounds and distributing them to the public,” Safdar said.
