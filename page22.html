DAYJOB: Tax A benchmark for AI tax agents

Real-world tax work is messy. Instructions are incomplete, records conflict, and the most important problems aren't always the ones explicitly assigned. DAYJOB: Tax evaluates whether AI agents can handle that reality, taking ownership of a tax professional's work from an initial client request to a finished deliverable.

Across 50 environments and 1,789 files, agents must navigate complex tax records, uncover issues clients may not know to raise, and make consequential judgments with incomplete information. Success requires more than tax knowledge and accurate calculations. It demands the initiative and professional judgment to figure out what needs to be done, investigate what matters, and deliver work that meets professional standards.

Anna was planning to sell her condo. She had moved out in August 2024 and wanted to know whether closing in October 2026 or January 2027 would be better for taxes.

An AI agent reviewed both options and correctly concluded that either date would satisfy the residence requirement for a tax break worth up to $250,000 in excluded gains.

Under the usual rules, homeowners must have lived in a property for at least two of the five years before selling it to qualify for the exclusion. Because Anna moved out in August 2024, she would stop satisfying that requirement around the end of August 2027. If her sale was delayed beyond August 2027, she could lose eligibility.

Across five attempts, Astra never identified the deadline. Opus identified it in all five attempts.

The agent evaluated the proposed dates correctly, but its advice left Anna unaware of a deadline that could affect a major financial decision. A tax adviser should recognize consequential issues even when the client doesn't know to ask about them.

This is the kind of professional judgment we're testing with DAYJOB: Tax, the latest addition to Surge AI's DAYJOB benchmark family.

DAYJOB evaluates AI agents on professional tax assignments requiring specialized expertise, complex materials, and decisions that aren't fully specified in the initial instructions. The benchmark contains 50 environments across eight areas of tax practice. Agents receive client requests and supporting records, then produce tax returns, workpapers, technical reviews, or client advice.

These assignments test professional judgment alongside tax knowledge, calculations, retrieval, and instruction following. Agents must decide what deserves investigation, resolve conflicting evidence, recognize when information is insufficient, and carry those decisions into their final work.

South Korea’s financial institutions have been hit by another wave of cyberattacks, highlighting the growing risk of AI-driven hacking in Asia. The latest incidents affect brokerages Daishin Securities and Mirae Asset Global Investments, as well as the crypto exchange Bithumb.

The incident comes roughly a month after a series of cyberattacks targeting South Korean financial institutions, raising concerns about vulnerabilities beyond just the country’s banking systems. Security researchers linked the September wave of attacks on South Korea to a suspected China-based hacker who relied on an AI penetration testing tool called Artex, though officials are still investigating the root cause of the latest breaches.

According to local news, Daishin reported a cyberattack that exposed the personal information of 38,490 customers, including names, email addresses, and mobile phone numbers. The company has notified affected customers, is cooperating with authorities in the investigation, and will compensate for any losses as required by law.

Meanwhile, Mirae and Bithumb reported separate cybersecurity incidents on Wednesday, per local media, though neither has confirmed that customer data was taken.

South Korea’s financial regulator has called for stronger cybersecurity measures following a string of attacks on financial firms and government agencies, per officials in Seoul on Wednesday. The country also plans to conduct security checks on dozens of IT systems in the private sector.

Yesterday, Japan reported a cybersecurity incident involving GMO Internet, following a series of attacks on its financial institutions. The wave of cyberattacks across Asia underscore growing cybersecurity challenges in the region, often as a result of years of weak cybersecurity; though, investigators have not established a connection between the incidents.

South Korea’s Financial Services Commission (FSC), Daishin Securities, Bithumb, and Mirae Asset Global Investments did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

#401. Audio Technica just released its first set of wireless StreamSet headphones

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

This expands the lineup beyond XLR and USB-connected models.

Audio Technica just released a wireless model in its popular StreamSet category of headphones. The new ATH-M50xSTS-WL is the very first wireless version in the lineup.

These cans don't connect via Bluetooth, as StreamSet headphones are primarily intended for content creators, streamers and gamers, all of which require ultra-low latency. They connect via 2.4GHz, so there's a USB transceiver that hooks up to PCs and gaming consoles like the PS5 and Switch 2. The battery lasts for around 24 hours and is charged via USB. The headphones can still work while being charged, via a wired connection.

Otherwise, these are extremely similar to the original wired ATH-M50xSTS headphones. They feature microphones based on the company's 20 Series and audio drivers pulled from the ATH-M50x professional monitor headphones.

The headset includes adaptive noise cancellation with two selectable levels. The company says an algorithm "continuously monitors audio input to prioritize the user's voice over ambient sounds," which includes stuff like PC fans and air conditioners.

There's a cardioid condenser microphone on a flexible boom arm that instantly mutes audio when flipped up. It uses the same 45mm audio drivers found in the ATH-M50x studio monitor headphones. I've used that particular and iconic model and can attest they do indeed sound great.

They ship with two types of interchangeable earpads. There's a pair for better sound quality, which is great for listening to music, and another designed with breathable mesh for long streaming and gaming sessions. The ATH-M50xSTS-WL headphones are available right now and cost $299.

#435. Sony Wants You to Forget About Discs With Its Gorgeous Transparent Controllers

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sony has a collection of colorful, translucent cosmetics for its PlayStation consoles and controllers. Announced Wednesday, the Prism collection brings three new, gorgeous color options that conjure a nostalgic, transparent tech feel.

They come in Flare Orange, Emerald Green, and Graphite Gray. (Based on the pictures, the gray looks more like a blue to me.) You can get Sony’s DualSense PlayStation 5 controllers in the Prism design or opt for PS5 console covers. On the controllers, translucent plastic wraps around almost the entire device’s frame, letting you view the components inside. On the console cover version, the transparency is more limited—you can see only the fan and a few other components.

The Prism Collection DualSense wireless controllers cost $85, while the console covers cost $75. Preorders start on November 10, but the collection won’t ship until February 4. Those are roughly the same prices as existing controllers and covers, though on the higher end. Basic color controllers start at $74 on the PlayStation website. Covers start around $59. The more colorful options on both products go up from there.

Apple’s original iMac largely popularized translucent, colorful tech that lets you see inside the machine. The look was en vogue for a while in seminal devices like the PlayStation 2 and Nintendo’s Game Boy Color.

That see-through aesthetic was a signature in the early 2000s. (My first CD player had a translucent top, and you could see the disc spinning around the whole time.) Twenty-some years later, it’s being revived as nostalgia bait in a transparent tech renaissance. You can find the transparent look in everything from gaming handhelds to a Theragun. Sony also has a transparent pair of earbuds.

Reminiscing about the good ol’ days might also remind you that Sony’s old consoles used to have disc drives. In July, Sony made a controversial decision to end production of physical game discs starting in 2028. Ownership advocates have decried the choice, noting that Sony’s own terms of service state that buying a digital copy of a game does not mean the customer owns it. It’s yet another move toward the end of physical media.

Still, the Prism collection sure looks pretty.

Nvidia on Wednesday released Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe, a free, open-weight model that transcribes audio in up to 40 languages. It also released version 2 of Granary, its open source speech dataset.

Nvidia boasts that Nemotron 3.5 can translate 31 languages to and from English, handle recordings up to an hour long, and tell up to eight speakers apart. It claims to beat rivals on benchmarks, including Hugging Face’s Open ASR Leaderboard.

These tools target developers building apps for business meetings and medical transcription, two of AI’s most established footholds in the workplace. Nvidia says developers can use the model, the updated Granary dataset, and tools like its NeMo Speech model to customize apps to recognize an industry’s jargon in each language. It joins Nvidia’s open-weight voice models, including the super small yet powerful Parakeet (1.1 billion parameters).

Nvidia’s latest voice entry comes during a busy period of AI voice releases: Mistral shipped Voxtral 3 earlier this month; ElevenLabs launched Realtime for instant speech in voice agents; and Salesforce released Launa-Omni, its voice model.

#444. Senators Demand to Know if Dr. Oz Collaborated With Election Denial Groups

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

With a week to go before the midterms, a group of Democratic senators are demanding that Mehmet Oz, the administrator for the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS), share communications his department may have had with far-right groups promoting a baseless conspiracy theory about left-wing groups stealing votes from residents of nursing homes.

In July, Oz’s agency issued a memo rescinding previous guidance on how staff at nursing homes could help residents to vote by assisting with mail-in voting or transporting residents to polling locations. The memo replaced that guidance with threats of prosecution if staff “engage in inappropriate coercion or other violations of residents’ rights.” Referring to this memo, election denial groups last month began to boost claims that voter fraud was happening inside care facilities.

That campaign was spearheaded by the Election Integrity Network, a group led by Cleta Mitchell, a former adviser to president Donald Trump. “These left-wing groups go into the nursing homes, and they go into the group homes, and they basically steal the votes of these most vulnerable,” Mitchell said in a recent appearance on Steve Bannon’s War Room show.

Now, citing WIRED’s reporting on the nursing home conspiracy theories, a group of senators that includes Elizabeth Warren, Richard Blumenthal, Alex Padilla, Mazie Hirono, Ron Wyden, Kirsten Gillibrand, Jeff Merkley, and Cory Booker wrote in a letter to CMS to ”urgently request that CMS rescind its July 2026 memorandum and reinstate its 2020 and 2024 nursing home voting guidance.”

“This is as low as it gets,” Warren tells WIRED. “The Trump administration’s unfounded allegations of election fraud are inspiring election denial groups to harass nursing home residents and staff. With less than a week until Election Day, every lawmaker in America should be standing up for fair elections and pushing back on this attack on seniors’ voting rights.”

A CMS spokesperson did not respond to questions about the agency’s communications with election denial groups but instead referred WIRED to the July 2026 memo.

In the letter, Warren and her colleagues specifically ask Oz about communications between CMS and Mitchell, the Election Integrity Network, and Project Civica, a New York–based election-denial group. The senators demand Oz “identify each organization and individual involved, the dates and nature of those communications, and any recommendations provided to CMS.”

Mitchell and EIN did not respond to a request for comment. "[We] had no communications or interactions with CMS regarding its memorandum or our Adopt a Care Facility initiative," Kim Hermance, president of Project Civica, tells WIRED. "Our only engagement with the agency's guidance has been reviewing the publicly available information on its website."

In August, Warren sent a letter to Oz questioning the July memo and asking him to reverse the changes. In September, Oz responded in a letter to Warren directly addressing the guidance that was removed from the CMS website in relation to helping nursing home residents with voting.

“Providing neutral logistical assistance, such as helping a resident request a mail-in ballot, completing a ballot per the resident's own expressed wishes, or arranging transportation to a polling location, is not the type of conduct that would give rise to a voter fraud investigation,” Oz writes.

Addressing the apparent contradiction in this response, the senators in their letter to Oz asked: “Why has CMS not reinstated its previous guidance clarifying nursing homes’ responsibilities to facilitate residents’ voting rights?”

With just days to go to the midterms, efforts to boost baseless conspiracy theories about voter fraud continue to gain traction. On Tuesday, Trump said he is willing to accept the outcome of the midterms if they are “honest elections.” But his administration has spent months pushing conspiracy theories about mail-in voting and noncitizen voting, while actively trying to disenfranchise voters.

Amazon unveiled five new Alexa devices today, including two new kinds of hardware: the Alexa Photo Frame, a smart picture frame, and the Echo Time, a smart alarm clock.

The Alexa Photo Frame is Amazon’s foray into the world of digital photo displays, a product category that has transcended from a clunky, last-minute holiday gift to a sleek, attractive way to look through your camera roll.

Aura is responsible for much of the growth in this category, so it’s no coincidence that the Alexa Photo Frame has a similar aesthetic feel: The screen is much easier on the eyes than the old, bright photo frames from years past. The device is said to be specifically calibrated for sharing photos, featuring a semi-matte, anti-glare display that makes it look more like a Samsung Frame TV than a laptop screen.

Also like Aura, the Photo Frame’s 10-inch screen looks like a matte photograph, helping it blend in with other artwork on your wall.

Amazon’s new device, though, adds the value of integration with users’ existing Amazon accounts. Prime members get unlimited storage on Amazon Photos, and Alexa+ is supposed to intelligently choose how and when to display photos.

“It knows to choose photos where you’re smiling and the lighting is right, and it matches the season you’re in, so you see last year’s family Halloween costumes in October instead of the middle of spring. It also knows what to skip, like screenshots and receipts,” Amazon said in a press release. You can also add voice notes to photos, and images sync automatically as you take them.

The Alexa Photo Frame is on sale now, and starts at $199.99 in Black, and $229 in Birch or Walnut wood.

Meanwhile, the Echo Time clock seems to be a response to the growing sentiment that people don’t want to rely on their phones for alarms, setting morning reminders, or checking the weather.

“Millions of customers start and end their days with Alexa — checking the weather and turning on the lights from bed in the morning or winding down with white noise at night,” Amazon wrote. “But the bedroom asks for something different from a device than the kitchen or living room does — so we designed an Echo specifically for it.”

Amazon says that the Echo Time, which costs $199.99, or $209.99 for a Kids version, has opt-in sleep sensing tools. You can choose to set a smart alarm feature that wakes you up within a 10 to 60 minute window of your alarm time, and can wake you when it detects you’re in a lighter phase of sleep, so that it’s less jarring to begin your day.

Like the popular Hatch alarm clocks, the clock can also mimic sunrise features to ease you awake.

Amazon also showed off updates to its existing lines of devices. We now get the Echo Show 16, a new Echo Dot, and Echo Studio with Home Camera.

The biggest upgrade to the Echo Dot is that it now includes a built-in smart home hub, which also includes an eero Wi-Fi extender that adds up to 1,000 square feet of coverage, helping it connect to lights, locks, and thermostats around your home. The new Echo Dot is also powered by Amazon’s custom AZ3 silicon chip, which is said to make it better at on-device processing to speed up conversations with its AI, Alexa+.

Amazon says its most advanced speaker, however, is the Echo Studio with Home Camera. This speaker doubles as a camera so you can check on your home while you’re out, and also has visual intelligence features.

“Alexa can also see and understand what’s in front of the camera and help you in real time,” Amazon explained. “Pull out a board game box and ask Alexa+ to explain the rules or hold up a cereal box and Alexa can recognize it and help you reorder more flavors.”

The Echo Dot is available at $99.99, and the Echo Studio with Home Camera costs $249.99.

Finally, Amazon unveiled the new Echo Show 16, which includes a 16-inch display. Amazon says the device has a 10% larger viewing area than its predecessor, but with a 40% smaller frame. It has Fire TV built in, plus Smart Home tools like Family Organizer. It can also function like a digital photo frame.

The Echo Show 16 is available for preorder for $499.99.

Could Netanyahu persuade Eisenkot to join him in a national unity government?

Eisenkot would have to abandon his key platform to do so. He's framed himself as the only alternative to Netanyahu, and built his campaign on ousting him from power.

Just last night, he took to the stage at his campaign HQ and called on Netanyahu to "announce his retirement from political life today," telling the crowd that Israelis are fed up with Netanyahu's "culture of lies", "division and incitement".

But that was before one of the main exit polls - which had initially projected a lead for Eisenkot's bloc - was revised, and he was overtaken by Netanyahu.

Netanyahu could try to persuade Eisenkot's Yashar Party to join his Likud Party in a unity government by ditching the far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties he is currently in coalition with. But last night at Likud HQ he thanked his "loyal partners" and called for a "strong right-wing government" - remarks some have interpreted as meaning he intends to preserve his alliance with them.

One thing that could make a unity government more likely is a renewed conflict with Iran, which could place serious pressure on party leaders to put aside their political differences and work together. In the days prior to the election, Israeli and US media reported that Netanyahu was pushing for fresh strikes on the country, and had pressed Donald Trump to approve US involvement.

This would no-doubt be framed by Netanyahu's critics as an attempt to save his own political skin by entering another regional conflict.

This would be a seismic shift for Israeli politics. Only one independent Arab party has ever formally been part of a coalition, in 2021, and it didn't last long.

Eisenkot and every other Jewish party in his bloc bar one have already ruled out entering government with them. But right now, it's the opposition's only path to a majority.

The Arab parties were the story of election night. They look to have defied expectations and could gain a record 19 seats - helped by an unprecedented turnout from Israel's Arab population, who account for about one-fifth of the country and often describe themselves as Palestinian citizens of Israel.

For years, many boycotted Israeli elections, saying voting would legitimise a system that they say treats them as second-class citizens. But their turnout last night was inspired by a desire to oust the most right-wing governing coalition in Israel's history.

The United Arab list, who are currently projected to win around 7 seats, have said they'd join an opposition government, while The Joint List, who are projected to win 12 seats, have said they'd support one from outside.

However, the only Jewish leader advocating this scenario right now is The Democrats' Yair Golan. And a coalition like this would be very fragile, especially on issues like Gaza and the occupied West Bank.

The fact there is such a unified front from the otherwise fragmented Jewish parties against Arab participation points to Israel's deep divisions.

Could Netanyahu persuade the right-wing parties in Eisenkot's bloc to break away and join his coalition?

The two most likely candidates for this are Naftali Bennett - joint-leader of the Beyachad political alliance - and Avigdor Lieberman, leader of the Yisrael Beiteinu party. They are both right-wingers who are hardline on security, and share some political ground with Netanyahu.

But both campaigned explicitly to oust him, and to extend military conscription to the ultra-Orthodox - bringing them into direct conflict with the two ultra-Orthodox parties already part of Netanyahu's government. So it's difficult to see them joining forces.

Some have suggested Bennett and Lieberman's parties could replace the ultra-Orthodox parties, but current electoral math suggests that grouping may not have the seats needed for a majority.

If none of the above scenarios can be negotiated, the default outcome would be new elections.

Many would see this as a tacit win for Netanyahu, who would remain as caretaker PM until the new elections take place - and he may believe he has a better chance of winning them.

Some analysts think the high Arab turnout would not be repeated - especially if their record representation in parliament led to no change. And it could also mobilise more right-wing voters in a second poll.

Netanyahu may also bet that the estimated 70,000 Israelis who flew home from abroad to vote, and are thought to skew against him, wouldn't do so again.

Additionally, several right-wing parties who failed to pass the electoral threshold of 3.25% may attract fewer votes or may not even run, thereby bringing more votes to Likud and its allies.

Between 2019 and 2022, Israel held five separate elections as Netanyahu and his rivals struggled time and again to co-operate.

#525. AlpineBSD

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AlpineBSD is an operating system based on the NetBSD kernel with a userspace inspired by Alpine Linux and the objective of making it possible to use as little AI-generated software as possible. There is no LLM output included in the base system or main package repository (AlpineBSD AI policy).

AlpineBSD is a work in progress. There is currently no installer available. For the adventurous, a very high-level installation guide is available here.

AlpineBSD ships the NetBSD kernel, libc, and essential platform-specific tools like mount.

NOTICE: The NetBSD kernel has spotty hardware support, much less so than Linux. You cannot necessarily expect AlpineBSD to work on any random computer you have lying around. Consult the NetBSD supported hardware list for more information, particularly for laptops.

AlpineBSD ships with the apk-tools package manager and busybox, and a slightly more GNU-oriented userspace, preferring e.g. GNU make over BSD make. AlpineBSD is not a fork of Alpine Linux, its package collection is original (though many packages are ultimately based on aports).

Alpine Linux uses the OpenRC init system. Currently, AlpineBSD uses s6 and we intend to add OpenRC as an alternative.

#576. The Agent SDK

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Software teams use agents defined as code to handle recurring work across the development lifecycle. These agents connect to the places where a team works and act when relevant events occur, whether that means reviewing a pull request, executing a compliance workflow, or investigating an incident.

The most useful agents are tailored to how a particular team operates. Until now, building one has generally meant adopting a product designed around someone else's workflow or starting from scratch.

Today, we're releasing the Agent SDK, the framework we use to build our own managed agents like Bugbot, Security Reviewer, and Rollouts. Agent SDK allows you to define agents in code and manage them like any other software project. Start from a template or point your coding agent at our docs. When the agent is ready, agent-sdk deploy it to Cursor infrastructure with one command.

The Agent SDK lets you quickly build and deploy agents that work the way your team does. They respond to the events your team already handles and follow policies your team can inspect and change.

Code review is one example. Each team has its own standards for what requires attention and who should review it. With the Agent SDK, you can build an agent that responds to pull request events from GitHub, GitLab, or Bitbucket. The agent can assess the risk of a change, route it to the right team, and approve changes that meet your policy.

As an example, a team within Cursor had built a large, fast-growing knowledge base of lessons learned and wanted every pull request checked against it. This way, the team's past decisions would hold even as the codebase changed. With the Agent SDK, they built kb-reviewer in about a day. It now runs on every PR in their main codebase, flags changes that contradict a recorded lesson, cites the note behind each finding, and encourages engineers to reconcile the discrepancies it reports.

For more complicated work, agents can hand a task to another specialized agent with its own tools and sessions. They can also divide a job among subagents working in parallel.

The Agent SDK also supports evals and event replay out of the box, allowing for rapid development with assurances that critical workflows execute the way you expect. The PGA Tour uses the Agent SDK to power their software development factory. Evals keep them moving quickly.

The Agent SDK has enabled PGA to build every component of our software factory on Cursor's infrastructure. It provides the flexibility we need to build agents that drive our development process. And built-in support for evals allows us to quickly iterate on new ideas and optimize existing capabilities reliably.

An Agent SDK agent is organized like a small software project. The instructions that guide it sit alongside code for its tools, connections to outside services, and the evals used to test it.

Each file represents something the agent knows or can do. Prompts are written in Markdown, while tools are defined as TypeScript functions. Because everything is code, changes can go through the same version control and review process as the rest of your software.

When you're ready to put an agent into use, you can deploy it to Cursor by running agent-sdk deploy from the project directory. The command builds the agent, gives it a URL, and starts it on our infrastructure. You can manage secrets and integrations from the dashboard, while continuing to use the same logs, evals, and playground available locally.

You can also run the agent on your own infrastructure. Each agent runs as a single Node process that can be packaged in a container.

From there, you can iterate locally and run evals before deploying with agent-sdk deploy.

The Agent SDK also ships with skills that guide coding agents through the development process. You can point an agent at the docs and have it help build the bot with you.
