Two trains have crashed in Spain just south of the capital Madrid on Wednesday morning.

Rail operator Adif says the incident occurred between a Renfe train and another of the Iryo company, which covers the Madrid to Seville route.

Here is what we know about the crash so far.

The two trains crashed just after 09:00 (08:00 GMT) between municipalities Mora and Orgaz, and about 140km (100 miles) south of Madrid.

#126. Trump methane policy rollback may have minimal impact on crude prices, EPA says

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

U.S. President Donald Trump's proposed rollback of methane emissions rules would do little to lower oil and natural gas prices, according to the Environmental Protection Agency’s own economic modeling.

Trump and EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin announced the proposal Tuesday in Lubbock, Texas, in a bid to lower regulatory costs and support domestic energy production.

“When owners and operators can produce, America benefits with lower prices,” Zeldin said in EPA’s announcement.

The EPA projects that the changes would lower U.S. crude prices by 11 cents a barrel in 2027, or 0.14%, while increasing domestic oil production by 0.09%. That amounts to roughly one extra barrel for every 1,000 the U.S. would otherwise produce, according to CNBC's calculations.

CLSA energy research analyst Baden Moore said uncertainty over implementation and the policy’s durability across administrations could constrain producers’ investment decisions.

That “limits the upside to current production rates in the near term,” including in 2027, he told CNBC.

He added that the changes could instead slow declines in existing production by allowing some wells to remain operational when environmental compliance costs would otherwise make them uneconomic.

Moore anticipates that the policy would affect only a small proportion of the U.S. shale market.

With CLSA expecting oil prices to remain above $80 a barrel, many wells would already be commercially viable, limiting the number whose continued operation would depend on regulatory relief.

The proposal could help many well operators while having a limited effect on overall supply.

Marginal wells — wells that produce small amounts of oil and gas — account for around 80% of active U.S. wells, but less than 10% of production, according to the International Energy Agency.

The EPA is considering lighter methane requirements based either on a well site’s production levels or whether it uses conventional or unconventional extraction methods.

The 2026 Major League Baseball season is over, and the Milwaukee Brewers are World Series champions for the first time. The Brewers finished off the Tampa Bay Rays on Wednesday night in Game 5 of the 2026 World Series. MLB Opening Day for 2027 is scheduled for March 24. But whether fans are watching baseball again or not in less than five months is very much up in the air.

Major League Baseball's players and owners are in the process of negotiating a new collective bargaining agreement, or CBA. The CBA is the accord that governs the working relationship between the players, as represented by their union, the MLBPA, and the clubs. It covers pretty much every aspect of that relationship that you can imagine, all the way from the overarching salary and payroll structure to daily meal money and days off during the season.

Not surprisingly, it's the economic platform of the game that's the toughest to negotiate because that is always, in essence, a fight over money, or how the league's substantial revenues will be divided up between teams and players. That's keenly relevant right now because players and owners are sparring over whether or not MLB will have a cap on salaries moving forward. The owners have renewed their push for a salary cap after not aggressively (or at least not this aggressively) pursuing one since the 1994-95 players' strike.

Matters are such that the owners are expected to implement a lockout of the players after the current CBA expires at 11:59 p.m. ET on Dec. 1 of this year.

There are two kinds of labor stoppages in MLB: the strike and the lockout. A strike is when the labor side – players as represented by their union, in this instance – puts a halt to operations. A lockout is when the management side – team owners and the commissioner's office doing their bidding, in this instance – initiates the stoppage. In plainspoken terms, a strike is a refusal to work, and a lockout is a refusal to permit work to be done. For those on the outside, the result is the same, in that there's no Major League Baseball during a lockout or strike.

When a lockout is in force in MLB, as is expected to be the case after this CBA expires, nothing happens on the major-league front. And we do mean nothing.

There's an almost literal element to a lockout in that anyone on the 40-man roster of any MLB club will be locked out of any team facility for the duration of the stoppage. That also means no access to team trainers or team physicians or members of the coaching staff. Beyond that, there's a transaction freeze in place starting the moment the lockout begins (more on that in a moment).

Additionally, MLB is expected to remove player images and videos from the league's official website, MLB.com, and player images and even names would be removed from articles on MLB.com. A league memo acquired by The Athletic also forbids contact between players and anyone on the club side, at least to the greatest extent possible. The latter extends all the way down to a recommendation against sending holiday cards to players who have been locked out.

Bottom line: A lockout means the sport at the highest level is on hold across all fronts until further notice. "Further notice" almost certainly means when a new CBA is agreed to in principle and then ratified by both sides.

As just noted, a lockout means a transaction freeze, which means no players on any 40-man rosters can be traded and no major-league free agents can be signed for as long as the lockout lasts. Free agency, however, begins on Monday (Nov. 2), so there is roughly one month available for those looking to make trades or signings (once the five-day exclusive negotiating window closes and qualifying offers are extended) before the freeze sets in.

It's worth noting that in the run-up to the last lockout of 2021-22, we saw a swarm of free agent signings just before the lockout began. We could see that flurry of moves again, particularly in light of proposed changes to contract structures that would limit lengths and dollar amounts. Would players like Tarik Skubal, who are eyeing mid-nine-figure contracts, want to get their deals done quickly and hope they're grandfathered in?

That said, there's a lot more uncertainty about what the game's financial structure will look like after this CBA negotiation concludes, at least relative to the last time we did this. That may have a chilling effect on the trade and free agent markets leading up to the lockout date. Clubs don't like mass uncertainty when it comes to making major roster decisions, and they may prefer to see what the league's salary and revenue sharing systems will look like for the next half-decade (the term of each CBA these days) before committing to signings and trades.

If the lockout spans a meaningful amount of time, then that means a great deal of usual winter business will be put on hold. The MLB Winter Meetings and the major-league portion of Rule 5 Draft are typically held in the first half of December, so those could be imperiled. Last time out, the major-league portion of the Rule 5 Draft was indeed canceled, although the November Rule 5 protection deadline had already passed. If the lockout stretches into January, then the exchange of arbitration figures between eligible players and their teams will be delayed. Get well into late January and early February without an agreement, and arbitration hearings and the spring training schedule could be shortened or imperiled altogether. Needless to say, local businesses and elected officials in Florida and Arizona would very much not like that. That, though, could be the least of our worries.

It very well might. It's too soon to say, but there's the real potential for disruption and, perish the thought, maybe even cancellation of the 2027 season. The latter is of course the worst-case scenario, and there's no reason to think there's a strong threat of that possibility, at least this far out. During the last lockout, though, the two sides weren't fighting over quite as much territory, and we very nearly lost games from the regular-season schedule. Lest anyone forget, the last time the owners vigorously pushed for a salary cap, we wound up losing the World Series.

There is a wrinkle of note that may make a worst-case scenario less likely. If the lockout drags on long enough to cancel 60 or more games from the 2027 MLB season, then the fallout could be significant. The Athletic recently reported that if the lockout extends into late May or so and costs at least 60 games, then most of the league's national broadcast and streaming contracts would be extended for another year, through 2029. That matters because Manfred and the league hope to bundle and centralize team streaming rights and share those revenues equally, and the current timeline would theoretically allow that to happen at the same time the national contracts are up for renegotiation in 2028. Tacking on another year to those national contracts would push back the significant windfall that owners and the league are expecting from that new media rights structure. This amounts to a major incentive, at least on the league side, to agree to a new CBA before June arrives.

Just players on the 40-player roster will be affected by the lockout, as mentioned. While that includes some minor leaguers, the vast majority of minor-league players are not on the 40 and thus will not be impacted. Minor leaguers now have their own CBA independent of MLB players, and that CBA is still in force. Thus, they're not being locked out. The minor-league season will proceed as normal, albeit minus those prospects on their respective 40s, just as it did during the 2021-22 lockout. So, yes, there will be affiliated professional baseball going on even if the lockout stretches into the 2027 regular season.

To hear commissioner Rob Manfred tell it, the lockout is designed to hasten negotiations between the two sides and speed up the process of forging a new CBA that, assuming it arrives in time, will cover the 2027-31 MLB seasons. Manfred cited this very reason leading up to the lockout of 2021-22, and during the drumbeat phase of this approaching lockout, he said this:

"In a bizarre way, it's actually a positive. Actions, though, hold sway over words, and after locking out the players, MLB owners didn't make their first CBA proposal to the players for another six weeks or so. In reality, the purpose of a lockout is to preempt a strike. The union can time a strike so as to exert maximum leverage and apply maximum pressure on ownership, say, during the stretch drive or soon after Opening Day or just in advance of the playoffs. From a fan and observer standpoint, though, at least this gets the clock started before games hang directly in the balance.
